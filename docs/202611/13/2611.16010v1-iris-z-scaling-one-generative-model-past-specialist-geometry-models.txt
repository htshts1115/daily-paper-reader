Iris-Z: Scaling One Generative Model Past
Specialist Geometry Models
Alejandro Cañada†, Chema Garabito
SperidLabs · †Project lead · Code and models: github.com/speridlabs/iris-z-sdk
ABSTRACT
Language moved from many task-specific models to one unified model: a single generative model with a sin-
gle generative training task that handles all of them within the same backbone, and keeps improving with data,
parameters and compute. We believe the bitter lesson applies just as much to computer vision, 3D and spatial
understanding, in the end to visual intelligence: the effort should go into general architectures that solve all these
tasks in a unified way, and into scaling them, rather than into task-specific designs. We test this on monocular
geometry, a task that has belonged to specialised architectures for years. Iris-Z is a general image generator that
produces appearance and geometry together. A single backbone denoises, reasons about and understands the
image and its depth at the same time, and one set of weights generates images with their depth, estimates depth
from photographs and paints images onto a given depth. At enough scale it surpasses state-of-the-art specialists
on established depth benchmarks while keeping the generative abilities of the backbone it started from without
specialized layers. We release two models for the community.

01 · Introduction
Language went from many task-specific models to
one unified model that handles all of them within the
same backbone: a single generative model with a sin-
gle generative training task, simple and foundational
enough to cover all the others.
Trained to predict
the next token on enough data, it learned to trans-
late, summarise and answer questions at a level com-
petitive with models built for each of those tasks [1].
And the same single model kept getting better at every
one of those tasks as data, parameters and compute
grew [2, 3], giving one way to scale every text task for-
ward at once. We believe this is the bitter lesson in its
plainest form: general methods that scale with com-
putation end up ahead of task-specific methods [4].
We believe the bitter lesson applies to computer vi-
sion, 3D and spatial understanding, and in the end
to visual intelligence, as much as it applied to lan-
guage. The effort should go into finding general ar-
chitectures that solve all of these fields and tasks in
a unified way, and then into scaling that one model
as far as possible, rather than into task-specific de-
signs. Visual intelligence is now following this path
through visual generative models. For example, Vi-
sion Banana instruction-tunes the Nano Banana Pro
image generator on a small amount of task data and
matches or beats specialists such as Segment Any-
thing 3 and Depth Anything without losing its ability
to generate [6]. Veo 3 segments objects, finds edges
and solves simple visual tasks without being trained
for any of them [5]. Models trained to both understand
and generate images with the same weights such as
BAGEL [7] and PixelUMM [8] also point in a similar di-
rection.
Data is the other half of this argument. General gen-
erative models can be trained on massive amounts of
visual data, web-scale images and videos that need
no labels designed for one task. A unified architecture
such as Iris-Z, where each modality is one more group
of tokens in the same sequence, lets more modalities
join that training. In our experiments, the larger the
pre-trained generator and the more data it is trained
on, the better it does on task-specific benchmarks
(Fig. 2b): parameter count, compute and above all
scalable pre-training data are what move the result.
The more compute and data go into a single architec-
ture, the better it becomes at each specific task, which
gives one way to scale every visual task at once.
Monocular geometry is a hard place to test this. The
reference model, MoGe-3 [11], is a task-specific ViT
that as of today leads the benchmarks. In MoGe-3’s
own comparison, models like Apple’s Depth Pro [12],
ByteDance’s Depth Anything 3 [13], UniDepth V2 or
UniK3D all stay behind it. Image generators that are
repurposed as depth estimators like the Marigold fam-
ily [14, 16], although strong, still perform worse than
MoGe-3 and also than MoGe-2 when evaluated on
MoGe-3’s held-out test sets. Vision Banana reports
a lower metric-depth error than MoGe-2 [10], but it
predates MoGe-3 and is not available to test.
We built Iris-Z to test this idea and to show that a gen-
eral model can beat models designed for one task.
We took a general image generator and taught it not
only to generate appearance (RGB) but to generate
and reason about geometry (depth) jointly with it, de-
noising both together as one RGBD output. In Iris-Z
appearance and geometry are produced together, and
a single backbone denoises, reasons about and un-
derstands both at once. Our view is that a generative
model should generate and understand the world in
all its modalities jointly, and that the geometry it pro-
duces along with an image is part of what it knows
about the scene and the world.
We see this as a path forward to unifying visual under-
standing. The same recipe extends to further modal-
ities, such as surface normals, segmentation, motion
or the camera, each added as one more group of to-
kens in the same sequence, so that a single model
learns all of them together and improves on all of them
as it scales.
In our experiments generalist image models have
proven better than specialists with enough scale. Iris-
Z-big is, to our knowledge, the first open general-
purpose generator to beat MoGe-3 on Marigold’s
benchmarks, and it is also ahead of MoGe-3 on aver-
age on MoGe-3’s own benchmarks at the resolutions
it was trained for, while still being a general genera-
tive model that does the same tasks it did before we
re-trained it. Each step of scale we added, more data,
a full fine-tune and a larger backbone, improved the
result (Fig. 2b) without the need for a depth-specific
layer. We take this as a sign that generative AI, com-
puter vision and spatial intelligence are converging on
one model that has all of these capabilities and scales
better than today’s specialised systems.

02 · Related Work
Monocular depth specialists. Learning depth from
a single image began with networks regressed on
one dataset at a time, with a scale-invariant loss to
deal with the ambiguity of a single view [23].
Mi-
DaS showed that mixing many datasets with differ-
ent scales trained with a loss invariant to scale and
shift transferred to unseen domains far better than
any single source [24]. DPT moved the same recipe
to a vision transformer [25].
Metric depth needed
camera-aware approaches like ZoeDepth which adds
metric heads to a relative model [26], Metric3D that
maps every image to a canonical camera [27] or
UniDepth that predicts the camera together with the
depth [28]. Depth Anything scaled the training data
instead, pseudo-labelling about 62M unlabelled im-
ages with a teacher [29]; its second version trains
that teacher on synthetic data only [30]. Depth Pro
then focused on sharp boundaries and focal length
estimation [12], and Depth Anything 3 extended the
approach to multiple views [13]. MoGe predicts an
affine-invariant point map with a robust alignment
in training [9], MoGe-2 adds metric scale [10], and
MoGe-3 refines the point map in 3D with sparse con-
volutions [11]. This line of work improved by finding
better depth-specific strategies and more varied and
better labelled data, while each model stayed task-
specific.
Generative priors for dense prediction. Marigold
showed that a text-to-image latent diffusion model
fine-tuned on a small amount of synthetic data be-
comes a strong zero-shot depth estimator [14]. Much
of the follow-up work made this faster: DepthFM re-
places diffusion with flow matching from image to
depth [32], Lotus predicts the clean target in a single
step [33], and Martin Garcia et al. show that a single-
step model matches the multi-step one [15]. Other
work extended it to new outputs and backbones. Ge-
oWizard predicts depth and normals together [31],
DepthCrafter applies the approach to a video gener-
ator [34], Marigold V2 moves to diffusion transform-
ers [16], and Iris-3B compares pixel and latent-space
generators [17]. In all of these the generator becomes
an image-to-depth model. The image is always the in-
put, and the ability to generate images is lost along the
way. Iris-Z keeps the generative objective and text-
to-image generation, and depth estimation is one of
several tasks the same joint model performs.
Joint
RGB-D
and
multi-modal
generation. A
smaller line of work produces geometry together with
appearance. LDM3D trains one latent diffusion model
to output an image and its depth from a prompt [35],
while JointNet keeps the text-to-image model frozen
and adds a parallel branch for a dense modality such
as depth [36]. Wonder3D extends this across views,
producing colours and normals with cross-domain
attention [37], and Aether trains a video model to pre-
dict geometry alongside the frames [38].
None of
these were shown to reach specialist accuracy on es-
tablished depth benchmarks, which is the gap Iris-Z
addresses.
Generalist vision through generation. Visual in-
context learning shows a model an example input and
its output side by side together with a new input, and
asks it to fill in the missing output, so the task is de-
fined by the example rather than by the architecture.

Bar et al. do this with an inpainting model on a grid
of images [39], and Painter trains a masked image

instruction-tunes a text-to-image model to perform
vision tasks named in text [41], and OmniGen trains
one generator for editing, conditional generation and
several classic vision tasks [42]. More recently, Vision
Banana showed that a frontier image generator with
instruction-tuning matches specialists on segmenta-
tion and depth [6], and Veo 3 solves visual tasks it
was never trained for [5]. Unified multimodal mod-

and diffusion in one transformer [43], Janus uses sep-

Scaling visual generators. Diffusion transformers
improve predictably with compute [45], and rectified-
flow transformers show smooth scaling of validation
loss that tracks human preference up to billions of
parameters [46].
Whether the geometry a genera-
tor learns scales the same way is less studied. Our
scaling path (Fig. 2b) is a first data point for the depth
modality.
03 · Method
Iris-Z comes from a pretrained text-to-image diffusion
transformer and the only new parameters we add be-
long to a learned modality embedding. There is no
depth head and no second network. Depth is encoded
as an image by the same autoencoder that encodes
the image and placed in the same token sequence,
and training uses the base model’s flow-matching loss
together with a loss on the decoded depth. Below we
describe how image and depth share one sequence
(Fig. 3), how depth is represented, which tasks are
trained, and how we sample. The backbones, train-
ing settings and data are in Sec. 4.
Image and depth in one sequence. The image and
its depth are both encoded as images by the back-
bone’s autoencoder, and their latents form one token
sequence together with the prompt. Each token car-
ries three embeddings that identify it. The spatial po-
sition embedding (the backbone’s rotary row and col-
umn) is the same for the image token and the depth
token of a patch, so the model knows they describe
the same point of the scene. The sequence position
embedding, an extra rotary coordinate the backbone
already uses to tell several input images apart, is 0
for image tokens and 1 for depth tokens. A learned
modality embedding labels each token as generated
image, generated depth, given image or given depth.
Image and depth each carry their own flow-matching
time t: whichever is generated follows the rectified-
flow objective of the base model [20, 46] at its own t,
and whichever is given is fed clean and modulated
with t = 0. Because attention runs over the whole
sequence, image and depth condition each other at
every step of denoising.
Depth as an image. Depth is stored as per-image
normalised log ray distance.
The 2nd to 98th per-
centile of an image’s log distance is mapped to
[−1, 0.95], so every image uses the same range and
the prediction is defined up to a scale and shift in log
space. Sky is set to +1, which leaves a margin be-
tween the farthest real depth and the sky. The value is
copied into three channels and encoded by the same
autoencoder as the image, without changes; decod-
ing averages the three channels. At inference, pixels
above 0.975 are marked as sky.
Do multiple tasks with the same weights. Each
training sample draws one task.
Joint: image and
depth are noised to the same time and both are scored
(prompt →RGBD). Image given: only depth is de-
noised (image →depth). Depth given: the reverse
(depth →image). Independent: image and depth are
noised to independent times. Image only: the depth
stays pure noise and only the image is scored (prompt
→image); this keeps training plain text-to-image gen-
eration and prevents the model from drifting away
from the base model’s images. With this mix of tasks,
one set of weights serves four tasks at inference. A
second loss decodes the depth the model currently
predicts and compares it with the target in pixel space.
Captions are dropped for a share of samples, so im-
age →depth works without a prompt.

04 · Implementation Details
4.1 Backbones
We use two open text-to-image transformers from
the FLUX.2 family as the backbone [18, 19]: Iris-Z-
big starts from FLUX.2 [dev] (32B parameters, Mistral
Small 24B text encoder) and Iris-Z-small from FLUX.2
[klein] Base 9B (Qwen3-8B text encoder). Both are
full fine-tunes of the transformer; the autoencoder
and the text encoder stay frozen.
FLUX.2 [dev] is
guidance-distilled: we remove its guidance embed-
ding and train classifier-free guidance back through
caption dropout. The only new parameters are the
modality embedding, initialised at zero, so training
starts from the unchanged base model.
4.2 Training
Both models are trained with AdamW in bf16 mixed
precision and with sharded data parallelism.
The
decoded-depth loss takes the clean depth latent im-
plied by the prediction, ˆx0 = xt −t v, decodes it with
the frozen autoencoder and computes an L1 to the
target over valid pixels, weighted by (1 −t) so that
near-clean steps count most; it is added with weight
1 to the flow-matching loss of every sample whose
depth is scored.
On sources with sparse or holed
depth (LiDAR, stereo, relative reconstructions) only
this decoded loss supervises depth, on measured pix-
els. Iris-Z-big keeps headroom for sky: real depth is
squeezed into [−1, 0.95] and sky alone is +1; Iris-Z-
small uses the full range. We keep two exponential
moving averages of the weights; the slower one is bet-
ter on every benchmark for both models and is the one
we release.
Iris-Z-big trains at a constant learning rate and is then
annealed linearly to zero over its last 750 steps; Iris-
Z-small keeps a constant learning rate. Both start at
about 5122 pixels and move a growing share of sam-
ples to about 10242, ending at 42% (Iris-Z-big) and
56% (Iris-Z-small).
4.3 Data
Each model was trained on about 3 to 4 million unique
RGB-D frames, drawn from a pool of real indoor cap-
tures, driving scenes with LiDAR and stereo, multi-
view reconstructions, rendered scenes and generated
images.
Every source was converted to metric ray
distance.
Sensor holes and sparse LiDAR stay as
holes: on those frames depth is supervised only on
measured pixels. Sky follows one rule per source and
is stored as a fixed far value. Every frame has a dense
caption, used as the prompt. Iris-Z-small saw about
3.8M unique frames, Iris-Z-big about 3.6M. Images
are stored at about 5122 and 10242 pixels. Validation
frames are held out per source, split by scene. We do
not train on the benchmark datasets.
4.4 Sampling and generation
Image →depth runs 4 steps from each of 10 seeds.
Each seed’s prediction is the average of the clean-
depth estimates ˆx0 = xt −t v from its last two steps.
The seeds are aligned to a reference by least-squares
scale and shift in log space and reduced by the per-
pixel median: 40 transformer passes per image. Im-
ages under 0.5 MP run at about 5122 pixels, larger

4.5 Generation is preserved
A depth estimator that forgets how to draw would be
just another specialist. We generate with fixed held-
out prompts and seeds and compare with the un-
touched base model on the same prompts and seeds
(Tab. 1, Fig. 4) to ensure that Iris-Z is in fact a general
model.
Prompt adherence holds as CLIP stays within 0.4 of
the base model, slightly above it for Iris-Z-small. Im-
ages keep the base model’s subject and composi-
tion (similarity 0.88–0.89).
Preference scores drop
(HPSv2.1 24.3 against 26.7 for Iris-Z-big) as the mod-

That same drift makes Iris-Z-big’s images more pho-

Benchmarks. Two tables, each run exactly as its pa-
per defines it.

NYUv2, KITTI, ETH3D, iBims-1, Sintel, DIODE and

Sets and resolution. We leave out two of MoGe-

Baselines. Marigold V2 (a single-step model by de-

3 refinement steps) are run by our code on the same

06 · Results
MoGe-3’s protocol. On MoGe-3’s own benchmarks
and alignment (Tab. 3, Fig. 5) Iris-Z-big is the most ac-
curate model on average (3.56 against 3.70 for MoGe-
3) and on six of the seven sets; MoGe-3 keeps DIODE.
Iris-Z-small is ahead of MoGe-3 on NYUv2 and HAM-
MER and behind on average (3.84).
Marigold’s protocol. Iris-Z-big is the most accu-
rate model on Marigold’s benchmarks (Tab. 4): 2.92,
against 3.12 for MoGe-3 and 4.10 for Marigold V2.
It is best on NYUv2, ETH3D and ScanNet; MoGe-3
keeps KITTI and DIODE. Iris-Z-small, at 9B, is level
with MoGe-3 (3.11).
Note that the estimates on the datasets that appear
in both protocols (NYUv2, KITTI, ETH3D, DIODE) dif-
fer because each protocol prepares the data differ-
ently: Marigold’s uses the native images and cameras
with fixed depth ranges and crops, while MoGe’s re-
renders every sample into a centred, undistorted pin-
hole view (cropping or zooming some sets).

07 · Discussion
Towards one model. We did not set out to build a
better depth estimator. We wanted to know whether a
model that only knew RGB could learn the geometry
of what it paints, without giving anything up. It could.
With no depth head and no new architecture, only
one extra embedding, Iris-Z-big now beats MoGe-3,
a model built for this one job, on the resolutions we
trained on, and it still preserves its generation capa-
bilities. Video and image generators are showing the
same thing on other tasks [5, 6], and we think this
points to one model that learns every modality of a
scene together, and keeps improving with data and
compute after task-specific designs have stopped.
Depth is the first modality we added; normals, seg-
mentation and multi-view geometry will come next.
Limitations. There are several limitations to this ap-
proach. Depth is still relative. The prediction is de-
fined up to a scale and shift in log space, so Iris-Z
gives neither metric distances nor the camera; MoGe-
3 predicts both. This is the gap we want to close next:
we will add one more group of tokens to the same
sequence that predicts the camera intrinsics.
With
the camera known, the depth map becomes a point
cloud from which the shift can be obtained, so what
remains should be a single global scale. Resolution
and depth range. We train at up to about 10242 pix-
els and do not evaluate on 2 to 8 MP images (Spring,
Synth4K). Cost. Each prediction takes 40 passes of a
9B or 32B transformer: about 15 s per 5122 image for
Iris-Z-big against MoGe-3’s 0.41 s on the same GPU.
We made that trade on purpose. The open question
was whether one generative model could match the
specialists at all; speed is a problem we already know
how to solve the same way image generation did. We
believe this is the right path, and that the optimisa-
tions will follow. Generation drift. Generated images
move towards the look of the training data: the hu-
man preference score of Iris-Z-big falls from 26.71 to
24.30, prompt adherence drops slightly (CLIP 27.42
against 27.85) and photorealism rises. Single images.
We evaluate one image at a time; consistency across
video frames or views is not measured.
