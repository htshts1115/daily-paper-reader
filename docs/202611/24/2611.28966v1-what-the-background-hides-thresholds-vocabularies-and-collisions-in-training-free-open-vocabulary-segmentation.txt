A B S T R A C T 

Training-free open-vocabulary semantic segmentation (OVSS) built on CLIP has advanced rapidly, with methods typically ranked by mean intersection-over-union (mIoU) across standard benchmarks. On datasets with a background class, however, implementations rely on per-dataset thresholds and background word lists that are rarely documented or unified (11 of 13 surveyed methods configure per-dataset background decisions). We audit configurations and papers of 13 training-free OVSS methods, re-run seven from official codebases, and compare official settings against a strict unified protocol under pre-registered contracts. The protocol gap depends strongly on the benchmark—ranging from 22.8 mIoU points on PASCAL VOC (VOC21) to 0.1 points (indistinguishable from zero) on ADE20K—and alters pairwise method rankings. Despite large shifts in absolute mIoU, most of the reported improvement of later CLIP-only methods over SCLIP remains under the unified protocol (roughly 79% of the published gain). Decomposing official background handling shows that it combines numeric settings and class-name files whose dominant component reverses across benchmarks, while no tested tuning-free rule transfers reliably across all datasets. Moreover, exact collisions between background words and target names—present in six of seven official COCO-Object configurations through “bed”—make affected classes unpredictable under official post-processing, while the main per-class drops under language-model-generated vocabularies on VOC21 are largely attributable to a few generated terms. We offer reporting recommendations and release our evaluation code, frozen configurations, contracts, and a collision-check tool. 

## 1. Introduction 

Semantic segmentation assigns a semantic label to every pixel of an image and is a basic step towards dense scene understanding Long, Shelhamer and Darrell (2015). Conventional models are trained under a closed-set assumption: they can only predict the categories in their training label set, and recognizing new ones requires new annotations and retraining. Open-vocabulary semantic segmentation (OVSS) removes this restriction by letting users specify the target categories with free-form text at inference time Li, Weinberger, Belongie, Koltun and Ranftl (2022); Ghiasi, Gu, Cui and Lin (2022). Most OVSS methods build on CLIP Radford, Kim, Hallacy, Ramesh, Goh, Agarwal, Sastry, Askell, Mishkin, Clark, Krueger and Sutskever (2021), which is trained contrastively on hundreds of millions of image–text pairs so that images and texts share one embedding space; a new category can be recognized simply by encoding its name. However, CLIP is trained to match whole images with their captions, so its patch-level features carry little spatial information. Used directly for pixel-level prediction, CLIP therefore produces poorly localized masks Wang, Mei and Yuille (2024). Current OVSS models follow two main technical routes to address this limitation: training-based and training-free methods. Training-based methods learn dense visual-language alignment through pixel- or region-level supervision Li et al. (2022); Ghiasi et al. (2022); Cho, Shin, Hong, Arnab, Seo and Kim (2024), or train using only image-text pairs Xu, De Mello, Liu, Byeon, Breuel, Kautz and Wang (2022); Cha, Mun and Roh (2023). However, these methods require additional data and training costs, and may be biased towards training categories. In contrast, training-free methods perform segmentation by adjusting the inference process of frozen pretrained models, without additional

This makes them suitable for annotation-scarce domains, such as remote sensing Li, Liu, Cao, Bai, Zhou, Meng and Wang (2025a). MaskCLIP pioneered the extraction of dense visual features for OVSS Zhou, Loy and Dai (2022). Subsequent methods focus on improving CLIP’s final-layer feature aggregation: SCLIP introduces correlative self-attention, NACLIP adds a spatial neighborhood prior, and ClearCLIP removes residual connections and the feed-forward network (FFN) to reduce noise Wang et al. Beyond the final layer, ResCLIP uses intermediate-layer attention weights and scores to improve spatial correspondence, while SC-CLIP repairs anomaly tokens and fuses multi-layer features Yang, Deng, Li and Duan (2025); Bai, Liu, Han, Zhang, Tang, Zhou and Lu (2025). Other methods leverage external visual foundation models, such as DINO and SAM Caron, Touvron, Misra, Jégou, Mairal, Bojanowski and Joulin (2021); Kirillov, Mintun, Ravi, Mao, Rolland, Gustafson, Xiao, Whitehead, Berg, Lo et al. (2023): ProxyCLIP computes proxy attention from DINO or SAM features, while Trident combines local feature concatenation, SAM-guided global aggregation, and mask refinement Lan, Chen, Ke, Wang, Feng and Zhang (2024b); Shi, Dong and Xu (2025). These methods are commonly evaluated using mean intersection-over-union (mIoU) on PASCAL VOC, PASCAL Context, COCO-Object, COCO-Stuff, Cityscapes, and ADE20K Everingham, Van Gool, Williams, Winn and Zisserman (2010); Mottaghi, Chen, Liu, Cho, Lee, Fidler, Urtasun and Yuille (2014); Lin, Maire, Belongie, Hays, Perona, Ramanan, Dollár and Zitnick (2014); Caesar, Uijlings and Ferrari (2018); Cordts, Omran, Ramos, Rehfeld, Enzweiler, Benenson, Franke, Roth and Schiele (2016); Zhou, Zhao, Puig, Fidler, Barriuso and Torralba (2017). However, even when background is treated as an evaluation category, methods may use different rules to decide which pixels belong to background, despite being evaluated under the same standard and mIoU metric. In official implementations, background may be determined through foreground confidence thresholds or predefined lists of background terms. Foreground category names may also be rephrased, and these choices are not always documented in detail. Among the 13 methods reviewed in our literature audit, 9 used a list of words rather than the single word "background" to represent background on VOC21 (Table 1). Such variation makes it difficult to determine whether reported performance improvements arise from the methods themselves or from their background settings based on reported mIoU scores alone. To our knowledge, no study has quantified this contribution. Moreover, these variations can affect comparisons between methods: 6 of the 21 method pairs reversed their relative rankings on VOC21 when their official settings were replaced by a unified protocol (this result reflects the overall impact of protocol differences, rather than background processing alone). These choices also present challenges in practical applications. In new domains without annotated validation data, foreground confidence thresholds cannot be selected through validation. Background word lists may also conflict with target categories. Among the 7 reproduced official configurations, 6 included "bed" in their COCO-Object background word lists, even though "bed" is itself a target category in COCO-Object (Section 4.4). Such conflicts already occur on the original benchmark and may become more likely when users change the set of target categories. Therefore, controlling and fully disclosing background settings would help distinguish methodological contributions from background-related effects, enable comparisons under a common protocol, and provide a reproducible, tuning-free starting point for new domains lacking annotations. This motivates our research question: How much do dataset-specific background settings contribute to reported mIoU on benchmarks with a background class, and can they be replaced by rules requiring no per-dataset tuning? To answer this question, we re-run seven representative training-free OVSS methods using their official code, covering four technical routes: last-layer attention modification (SCLIP, NACLIP), residual removal (ClearCLIP), intermediate-layer feature utilization (ResCLIP, SC-CLIP), and external vision foundation models (ProxyCLIP, Trident). First, we compare the reproduced results with the numbers published by the authors to verify the reliability of our experiments. We then compare each method’s official setting (O) with a strictly unified protocol (P*) based on Text-grounded Contrastive Learning (TCL) Cha et al. (2023). Compared with TCL, P* is stricter in three aspects: it uses only the word "background" with a threshold of zero, ignores dataset-specific hyperparameter settings, and disables optional pixel-level post-processing such as PAMR, while retaining method-specific components. Based on P*, we restore the official numerical settings (THR), followed by the official class-name files (BGB), and then remove background words that collide with target categories (NC). This allows us to examine how different settings contribute to the reported benchmark results. Besides that, we construct derived benchmarks from PASCAL Context and COCO-Stuff with different proportions of background pixels to examine how protocol differences vary with background coverage. Finally, we define and freeze the research hypotheses, decision thresholds, and decision rules before each experiment, with any subsequent deviations appended to the contract.

Our evaluation reveals three main findings. First, the performance gap between official settings and the unified protocol varies considerably across benchmark datasets. Averaged over the seven methods, mIoU increases by 22.8 points on VOC21 when moving from P* to official settings, compared with only 2.0 points on Context-60. The gains from official background settings also increase with the proportion of background pixels, although background coverage alone cannot explain their magnitude. Second, different components dominate the gains from restoring official background settings across benchmarks. On VOC21, about 77% of the gain comes from class-name files, while on COCO-Object, 77% comes from numeric settings, such as probability thresholds, logit scales, and area thresholds. Third, on two derived benchmarks based on COCO-Stuff, removing background words that conflict with target categories improves average mIoU by 1.3 points. A smaller but consistent gain is also observed on the original COCO-Object benchmark. However, the tuning-free rules and LLM-generated vocabularies we test cannot yet consistently replace settings tuned for each dataset. Importantly, for the evaluated later CLIP-only methods, most of their reported improvements over SCLIP are retained under the unified protocol. Our main contributions are as follows: 1. We audit the background configurations of 13 training-free OVSS methods and reproduce seven of them using their official code. Based on this, we compare their official settings with a strictly unified benchmark protocol (P ∗) derived from TCL Cha et al. (2023), providing a consistent evaluation condition for fair comparison. We also release the code and configurations to support reproducibility (Section 2.2). 2. We use controlled experiments to separate the contributions of numeric background settings and class-name files to segmentation performance. We further investigate the effects of background pixel proportion, conflicts between background words and target categories, and tuning-free replacement rules to identify the sources of performance variation across benchmark settings (Section 4.4). 3. We develop conflict-checking rules and their implementation, which we will release as a standalone tool to identify overlaps between background words and target categories. We also provide a benchmark reporting checklist to help researchers disclose key background settings and report experimental results in a reproducible manner (Section 5.2). The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work on OVSS, benchmark protocols, and background modeling. Section 3 introduces the scope of the audit, experimental settings, benchmark protocols, preregistered analysis, and reproduction procedures. Section 4 presents the experimental results, focusing on how variations in benchmark protocols and background processing settings affect segmentation performance. Section 5 discusses the transferability of background handling, a recommended reporting protocol, and the limitations of this study. Section 6 concludes the paper. The appendices provide supplementary results, details of the derived benchmarks, and the full preregistration contracts and their corresponding decisions. 

## 2. Related work 

2.1. Training-free open-vocabulary segmentation 

Training-based OVSS method can utilize pixel-level or masked supervision to learn mapping relationship in dense vision-language, such as LSeg, OpenSeg and CAT-Seg Li et al. (2022); Ghiasi et al. (2022); Cho et al. (2024); and is also able to run weakly supervised learning by only depending on image-text pair, such as GroupViT and TCL Xu et al. (2022); Cha et al. (2023). In this paper, we define training-free method as method without the need of extra parameter optimization for segmentation tasks after pre-training; It is not equal when comparing training-free in this paper with not using pixel-annotation or freezing the backbone itself. For example, CLIP-DINOiser needs to Train the spatial refinement module while CLIP is frozen Wysoczańska, Siméoni, Ramamonjisoa, Bursuc, Trzciński and Pérez (2024); Talk2DINO also needs to training the mapping from text feature to DINO feature space Barsellotti, Bianchi, Messina, Carrara, Cornia, Baraldi, Falchi and Cucchiara (2025). For training-free OVSS, one type of method reuses the internal visual representations of frozen CLIP models to generate spatial predictions. For example, MaskCLIP utilizes value features to extract dense predictions, while CLIP Surgery improves spatial localization through a dual-path architecture and value–value attention correlations Zhou et al. (2022); Li, Wang, Duan, Zhang and Li (2025b). GEM takes a step further by extending self-self attention to query-query, key-key, and value-value interactions, and facilitates token clustering through iterative self-attention and regularization during inference Bousselham, Petersen, Ferrari and Kuehne (2024). Other methods optimize attention or residual connections in the final layer, or utilize intermediate-layer features to recover spatial structure. Another

(2024b); Shi et al. (2025); Zhang, Liu and Tang (2025). Complementary to these visual modifications, ITACLIP adjusts the input through image enhancement and category descriptions generated by language models, while FreeCP focuses on reducing redundant candidate categories and visual-language ambiguity Aydın, Cırpar, Abdinli, Unal and Sahin (2025); Chen, Yang, Chen, Zhao, Lai, Shao and Xie (2025). The mechanisms illustrated mainly make optimization on model structure, feature processing and input, while the technical configurations, compared with the benchmark settings when the methods are actually applied, are from the different phase. In addition, training-free does not mean that the methods are independent from offline resources, or there is no need for configuration on datasets during inferencing. Because of this, section 2.2 audits benchmark configuration OVSS implemented; and section 3.1 introduces reproductions based on the 7 typical training-free methods’ official codes. 

2.2. Evaluation protocols and fair comparison 

When making comparisons among open-vocabulary segmentation methods, TCL established a standardized benchmark protocol Cha et al. (2023). To be specific, TCL applies MMSegmentation’s default category names, not allowing extensions or modifications depending on the target category, and resizes the shorter side of images while preserving the aspect ratio. TCL excludes dense CRF, which incurs high computational overhead, while maintaining PAMR, and analyzes its impact specifically. For datasets containing a background category, it also notes that a probability threshold can be considered. Because of this, mentioning following TCL does not mean that all methods follow the same rule for deciding the background category. In subsequent works, including SCLIP and ResCLIP, computationally expensive post-processing is not implemented in the main evaluations, while SCLIP still uses its own image resizing and sliding-window settings Wang et al. (2024); Yang et al. (2025). Subsequent studies have controlled method comparisons in different aspects. NACLIP distinguished whether external pre-training knowledge is applied from whether post-processing is applied, and pointed out that some methods’ hyperparameters would vary by dataset Hajimiri et al. (2025). ClearCLIP made a comparison on benchmarks where background existed and did not exist, and offered a SCLIP comparison using fixed, unmodified class names; SC-CLIP re-evaluated baselines under its stated protocol Lan et al. (2024a); Bai et al. (2025). These studies show that how we interpret comparisons between methods would be affected by additional pre-training priors, class names, and benchmark reproduction setups. In addition to model realization and inference settings, existing studies also analyzed the impact of datasets, annotations, and vocabulary on benchmark performance. MESS assesses trained zero-shot segmentation models across various domains and points out that its PASCAL VOC figures vary from previous reports because it includes a background class. It also notes that associating extra Context labels with the background, as done in some studies, might produce better results than simply using a uniform ’background’ label Blumenstiel, Jakubik, Kühne and Vössing (2023). RENOVATE focused on assigning more specific, fine-grained names to annotated visual regions rather than simply modifying the vocabulary of the entire dataset Huang, Peng, Zhang and Geiger (2024). OpenBench reduced semantic overlap between training and testing categories, and discovered that there were already fine-tuned CLIP models whose advantages failed to transfer to the new setting under its investigations Liu, Wu, Bai, Wang, Wang and Tang (2025). Šarić et al. distinguished the bottlenecks of mask generation, selection, and classification through oracle experiments, and pointed out that a substantial number of valid masks may be discarded because they are evaluated as no-object, a mask-level decision that differs from assigning a pixel-level background class Šarić, Martinović, Kristan and Šegvić (2025). In Vocabulary-Free Semantic Segmentation, the predicted class names need to align with the reference labels, while Reichard et al. found that there is no universally optimal matching threshold across datasets Reichard, Rizzoli, Gasperini, Hoyer, Zanuttigh, Navab and Tombari (2025). Kombol et al. pointed out that the definition of background varies across different methods, and an overlap may occur between background vocabulary and target classes, such as sky Kombol et al. (2025). Similar evaluation principles have been embraced in other areas: Musgrave et al. established standardized compar-isons for metric learning; Oliver et al. managed implementations and data conditions in semi-supervised learning; Gulrajani & Lopez-Paz focused on cross-domain evaluation and model selection; and Sincan et al. synchronized preprocessing, encoders, and training settings in sign-language translation Musgrave, Belongie and Lim (2020); Oliver,

2.3. Background modeling and vocabularies 

For benchmarks containing a background category, models must decide how to handle pixels that do not belong to any target class. One approach is to classify a pixel as background when the prediction confidence is low across all foreground categories, as adopted by GEM on PASCAL VOC Bousselham et al. (2024). Another approach is to leverage background-related textual concepts; for example, SC-CLIP employs stuff classes (such as sky and river) to represent the background Bai et al. (2025). As summarized in Table 1, these strategies can also be combined. Consequently, merely detecting background words in text prompts is insufficient to identify a method’s background handling mechanism. Furthermore, background can be inferred directly from visual responses. For example, FSeg-LLaVA activates regions based on detected foreground objects and derives the background response map from them without requiring any explicit background class names Zhang, Yu, Li, Lin, Wang and Xiao (2026). Predefining a background vocabulary becomes challenging when target categories are dynamically specified by users. To address this, Contrastive Concepts generates a group of competing concepts for each target query, which are sourced from contextual word co-occurrences in image captions and scene-related concepts prompted from language models Wysoczańska, Vobecky, Cardiel, Trzcinski, Marlet, Bursuc and Siméoni (2025). Consequently, the model not only predicts whether a pixel belongs to the target query, but also determines if it corresponds to visually or semantically confusable objects. When handling multiple query categories, the method aggregates competing concepts across all queries and filters out suggested terms that are overly similar to the target queries based on CLIP text embedding

similarity. However, competing concepts can still introduce ambiguity in target regions due to spatial relationships such as occlusion and co-occurrence: for instance, the authors report a failure case where ’blanket’ is introduced as a competing concept against ’bed’, even though a blanket naturally covers and coexists with a bed. Contrastive Concepts doesn’t just overlook synonyms and parts; it also filters concepts by applying a CLIP similarity threshold of 0.9. However, it focuses specifically on a single query and earlier base methods. We examine if these collisions remain in the official class-name files of seven recent methods, as well as in the generated lists following both the similarity filter and an extra LLM conflict check (Sections 4.4 and 4.5). Related work also shows that it is worth paying attention to the expression of class names and the redundant or ambiguous candidate classes: RENOVATE, ITACLIP, and FreeCP addressed regional naming, class descriptions, and ambiguous candidates separately Huang et al. (2024); Aydın et al. (2025); Chen et al. (2025). Our analysis focuses on 2 types of vocabulary interactions that can be tested through controlled experiments, which differ from the broader class semantic processing illustrated earlier. For official class name files, we delete terms from the background vocabulary that overlap with current target names based on predefined lexical rules, and observe the segmentation results. For automatically generated background vocabulary, we compare the performance of the list filtered by CLIP similarity before and after language model conflict checking is applied. 

## 3. Audit setup

We evaluate on five benchmarks: VOC21 (VOC 2012 val Everingham et al. (2010), 1,449 images, 20 fore-ground classes plus background), VOC20 (same 1,449 images, 20 classes, no background), COCO-Object (COCO

Annotation maps were verified file-by-file against our earlier stage with zero mismatches (5,000 COCO-Stuff label maps via an MD5 manifest, and 1,449 VOC2012 and 2,000 ADE20K validation masks against the earlier copies); for images, we verified the COCO val2017.zip archive MD5 and the ADE20K archive byte size. This file holds 5,104 images—one fewer than the 5,105 count commonly cited—which cannot be supplemented without the official source. ## 4. Results 

4.1. Protocol gap across datasets 

The performance gap between official configurations (O) and the strict unified protocol (P *) varies strongly across benchmarks, ranging from 22.8 mIoU points on VOC21 to 0.1 points on ADE20K. Averaged over the seven methods, O exceeds P * by 22.81 points on VOC21 (95% CI [21.90, 23.75]; per-method range 14.03–27.27 points), whereas on ADE20K the mean gap is 0.10 points (95% CI [ −0 .05 , 0.25]; range −0 .57 to +1 .21 ), which is not distinguishable from zero (Fig. 2 and Table 4). Across the remaining three benchmarks, O exceeds P * by 13.14 points on COCO-Object (95% CI [12.47, 13.70]; range 9.72–16.17), 5.18 points on VOC20 (95% CI [4.49, 5.90]; range 0.00–12.59), and 2.01 points on Context-60 (95% CI [1.81, 2.22]; range 0.82–4.34). On Context-60, the pre-registered V5 generalization gate ( ≥ 5.0 points) was therefore not met ( STOP ), showing that large protocol gaps are not uniform across all benchmarks with a background class. Transplanting official background handling alone ( BGB ) recovers most of the gap on the three benchmarks that include a background class, lifting mean mIoU over P * by 21.83 points on VOC21, 12.24 points on COCO-Object, and 1.49 points on Context-60 (Table 4). For ClearCLIP, ProxyCLIP, and Trident on VOC21, BGB matches O almost exactly (51.80 vs. 51.80, 61.27 vs. 61.24, and 67.08 vs. 67.08), indicating that their gap arises almost exclusively from background handling. By contrast, NACLIP remains below O under BGB by 7.04 points on VOC21 (57.02 vs. 64.06) and 3.10 points on Context-60 (31.89 vs. 34.99), indicating that its official configuration also relies on non-background settings (in V2 single-factor runs on VOC21, removing its default 10-step PAMR accounts for roughly 5.2 points; PAMR was not isolated separately on Context-60). Crucially, VOC20 exhibits a mean gap of 5.18 points despite having no background class, concentrated in NACLIP (12.59 points) and ResCLIP (10.79 points). Earlier single-factor substitutions on three methods (SCLIP, ClearCLIP, NACLIP in V2) attribute roughly 3.6–3.9 points on VOC20 to class-name rewrites and 3.3 points to NACLIP’s PAMR, with resolution and sliding-window changes shifting scores by at most about 2.4 points. 4 Thus, O and P * differ in non-background choices as well, which motivates isolating background handling explicitly via BGB , THR , and NC in Section 4.4. Protocol differences also affect relative method orderings in a dataset-dependent manner. Out of 21 method pairs, pairwise rankings reverse between O and P * for 6 pairs on VOC21, 5 on COCO-Object, and 2 on Context-60 (Fig. 3; ADE20K likewise shows 2 swaps). On Context-60, the pre-registered V5 ranking gate ( ≥ 3 discordant pairs) was not met, and the top three methods (Trident, SC-CLIP, ProxyCLIP) retain identical orderings. Conversely, VOC20 exhibits 12 discordant pairs out of 21, underscoring that non-background configuration choices such as class-name rewrites and PAMR can reshape rankings even without a background class.

4.3. Effect of background share 

The mIoU gain from transplanted official background handling increases with background pixel share, yet background share alone does not explain the size of the gain. Across the eight evaluation splits with a background class (VOC21, COCO-Object, Context-60, and the five derived variants in Fig. 4), the pre-registered S1 test (H1) was met ( SUPPORTED ): (a) background share and mean BGB − 𝑃 * exhibit a Spearman correlation of 𝜌 = 0 .857 (gate ≥ 0.8); (b) the least-squares slope is 24.6 mIoU points per unit share with a 90% method-level bootstrap confidence interval of [22.4, 26.9] (excluding zero; unlike the image-level 95% intervals elsewhere, this interval resamples across the seven methods); and (c) within the Context sequence, six of the seven methods increase monotonically from Context-60 through Ctx 35%, Ctx 53%, and Ctx 71% (gate ≥ 5), with ResCLIP as the sole exception (3.82 on Ctx 35%, 3.74 on Ctx 53%, and 3.44 on Ctx 71%). Nevertheless, matched background coverage yields widely different gains across benchmarks. Around 70–73% background share, BGB −𝑃 * averages only 5.35 points on Ctx 71% (70.7% background), compared with 12.24 points on COCO-Object (70.5%) and 21.83 points on VOC21 (73.3%). Within the Context sequence, gains rise from 1.49 points on Context-60 to 4.43 points on Ctx 35% once high-frequency categories ( sky , person , wall ) are merged, and then level off (5.03 points on Ctx 53%, 5.35 points on Ctx 71%; per-method scores appear in Appendix Table A.2, and recall from Section 3.2 that merging also reduces query-set size). At the per-image level within single datasets (H2, which tests O − 𝑃 * rather than BGB − 𝑃 * across five equal-count image quintiles of background share), the pre-registered monotonicity gate ( 𝜌 ≥ 0.9 on at least two of the three original datasets) was not met ( NOT_SUPPORTED ): only VOC21 reached 𝜌 = 0 .9, while COCO-Object and Context-60 both yielded 𝜌 = 0 .7.Conversely, on the COCO-Stuff variants that retain stuff classes as queries, transplanting official background handling is harmful or indistinguishable from zero. On COCO 31%, BGB − 𝑃 * averages −2 .59 points (95% CI [ −2 .91 ,

−2 .27 ]), with all seven methods negative; on COCO 51%, the mean is −0 .18 points (95% CI [ −0 .52 , 0.16]), whose interval includes zero. A plausible explanation, not tested within S1 itself, is that official COCO-Object background lists contain terms such as wall , road , floor , mountain , wood , and bed that collide with retained query categories; we test this hypothesis directly in Section 4.4.

## 5. Discussion 

5.1. Transferability of background handling 

Across OVSS benchmarks, “background” does not correspond to a single visual concept; rather, it is a dataset-defined residual class whose semantic scope varies with the foreground label set. On VOC21, background covers everything outside 20 object categories, including stuff regions such as sky, wall, and ground. On COCO-Object, background absorbs all 91 stuff categories and unlabeled pixels, leaving only the 80 thing categories as queries. On Context-60, by contrast, common stuff categories are foreground queries themselves, so background narrows to unlabeled and infrequent residual pixels covering just 12.6% of the split (Section 3.2). A background prompt or word list set to match the residual class on one benchmark can therefore overlap directly with foreground queries on another. 

W. Yao et al.: Preprint submitted to Elsevier Page 18 of 32 Background handling in training-free OVSS 

A plausible hypothesis—consistent with Sections 4.1 and 4.3, though not isolated across all factors—is that thing-only benchmarks require a strong background prior, whereas stuff-rich benchmarks do not. Official background handling adds 21.8 mIoU points on VOC21 and 12.2 points on COCO-Object, compared with only 1.5 points on Context-60 (Section 4.1); within the Context sequence, gains jump by roughly 3 points only once high-frequency classes such as sky , person , and wall are merged into background (Section 4.3). This hypothesis is consistent with 

> SEL

26 failing on Context-60 ( −7 .9 points below P *; Section 4.5), as would occur if a rule selected on background-heavy splits over-predicted background on a stuff-rich split. It also offers a plausible, untested explanation for why the same official numeric settings ( THR ) hurt on COCO 31% (165 classes) yet help on COCO 51% (151 classes; Section 4.4). Controlled ablations that transplant Context-60 thresholds separately from background prompts, or sweep thresholds across COCO-Stuff variants, would directly test this mechanism. These findings limit plug-and-play transfer to annotation-scarce domains Li et al. (2025a). In our literature audit, 11 of 13 methods rely on dataset-specific background rules (Table 1), the primary driver reverses between VOC21 and COCO-Object (Section 4.4), and no tested tuning-free rule transfers reliably across benchmarks (Section 4.5). Moreover, official repositories do not always match paper descriptions—for instance, whole-image VOC inference in NACLIP and ResCLIP, or per-dataset hyperparameters in SC-CLIP despite text stating consistent values. 6 Practitioners applying training-free OVSS to new domains therefore have no universally transferable background setting; at minimum, background vocabularies should be checked against target queries and paired with an untuned P *-style baseline. 

5.2. Recommended reporting protocol 

Based on Sections 4.1–4.5 and following reporting checklists in machine learning evaluation Dodge, Gururangan, Card, Schwartz and Smith (2019); Pineau, Vincent-Lamarre, Sinha, Larivière, Beygelzimer, d’Alché Buc, Fox and Larochelle (2021), we recommend six practices for OVSS benchmarks: 

R1. Report official configurations (O), a strict unified protocol (P *), and transplanted background han-dling ( BGB ) together. Official-versus-unified gaps range from 0.1 to 22.8 points and reverse method rankings (Section 4.1), while 11/13 audited methods specify per-dataset background decisions (Table 1). 

R2. Report numeric background parameters ( 𝜏 , 𝑠 , 𝑎 ) separately from class-name files. Their relative contribu-tions reverse across benchmarks—class-name files supply 77% of the gain on VOC21 whereas numeric settings supply 77% on COCO-Object (Section 4.4)—yet only 2/13 audited papers fully document background choices (Table 1). 

R3. Run a collision check against the target class list and disclose all removed terms. Colliding background words appear in 6/7 reproduced official COCO-Object configs (“ bed ”), cost 1.30 points on average across COCO-Stuff variants (Section 4.4), and coincide with class-specific drops in LLM vocabularies (Section 4.5); our collision tool is released for this step. 

R4. Report benchmarks with a background class separately from background-free benchmarks, avoiding cross-type averages. Gaps reach 13–23 points on VOC21 and COCO-Object versus 0.1 points on ADE20K and 5.2 points on VOC20 (Section 4.1), so eight-benchmark averages (e.g., Wang et al. (2024)) conflate background priors with dense alignment. 7

R5. Select background settings on a training split and document the selection rule, rather than tuning on the evaluation split. In Section 4.5, SEL 26 was chosen strictly on training splits and transferred to the validation splits without apparent overfitting, whereas 6 of 13 audited papers omit background choices altogether and 4 document them only partially (Table 1). 

R6. Release per-dataset configurations and class-name files, and specify post-processing and inference win-dows. Even without a background class, VOC20 shows a 5.2-point gap and 12/21 rank swaps; in V2 single-factor runs on three methods, class-name rewrites, PAMR, and window or resolution differences each shifted VOC20 scores (Section 4.1; Table 1).

Derived-variant confounds. Because the eight S1 splits share underlying images and methods, H1’s correlation and slope should be read descriptively rather than as independent-sample inference. 4. Decomposition and collision rules. Our string-matching rule ignores synonyms and part-whole relations, so measured collision losses may underestimate true lexical interference (we do not claim a formal lower bound, as removing synonyms was not tested). In addition, BGB −THR mixes background terms with foreground renamings (Section 4.4), and the S2 Ctx 71% control completed only 3/7 methods (reported descriptively). 5. Generated vocabularies. Adaptive study sequence and audit snapshot. Each experimental stage was frozen under its own pre-registered contract prior to inference, yet the choice of each subsequent stage was adapted after inspecting earlier results (such as pivoting to an audit-focused paper before V5, and reframing our claim from universal protocol gaps to dataset-dependent gaps after V5’s STOP ). Individual tests are pre-registered, whereas the overall sequence is adaptive. Finally, our literature audit reflects repositories and papers checked in November 2026. 

## 6. Conclusion 

This paper audited the configurations and documentation of 13 methods from the training-free OVSS literature, re-ran seven from their official codebases, and compared their official settings against a strict unified protocol derived from TCL under pre-registered analysis contracts. We found that the protocol gap depends strongly on the benchmark— ranging from large shifts on VOC21 and COCO-Object to a modest gap on Context-60 and a near-zero difference on ADE20K—and can alter pairwise method rankings. Decomposing official background handling showed that it combines numeric settings and class-name files whose dominant component reverses across benchmarks, while none of the tested tuning-free rules transfers reliably across all datasets. We also showed that collisions between background words and target category names cause measurable accuracy losses in the tested official COCO configurations, with exact collisions making the affected class unpredictable, and that class-specific drops observed on VOC21 with LLM-generated vocabularies are largely attributable to a few generated terms that name subtypes, parts, or co-occurring objects. Still, despite substantial shifts in absolute mIoU, most of the reported improvement of later CLIP-only methods over SCLIP remains under the unified protocol. To keep methodological progress separable from background priors, we encourage the research community to adopt the six reporting recommendations (R1–R6) in Section 5.2. Our evaluation code, official and derived configurations, collision-check tool, frozen contracts, and per-image statistics are released with this paper (see Data and code availability). Extending this audit to additional backbones and trained models while treating background handling as an explicit design choice remains worthwhile future work.

## E. Collision mechanism 

This appendix details the descriptive mechanistic follow-up (S5) referenced in Sections 4.4 and 4.5; it was initiated after an observation in S4, with all readouts fixed prior to computation, and is reported descriptively without altering any pre-registered gate from V2–S4. 

Proposition (structural zeroing under exact sole-name collisions). Suppose foreground class 𝑐 ≥ 1 has a single query string 𝑞 (𝑐 = { 𝑞 }) that also appears verbatim in the background vocabulary ( 𝑞 ∈ 0). By Eq. (3), 

𝑃 0(𝑥 ) = max 

> 𝑞 ′∈0

𝑝 𝑞 ′ (𝑥 ) ≥ 𝑝 𝑞 (𝑥 ) = 𝑃 𝑐 (𝑥 ) (E.1) holds at every pixel 𝑥 . Because PyTorch’s arg max breaks ties in favor of the smallest class index ( 0 for background), class 𝑐 is never selected prior to thresholding; applying 𝜏 or 𝑎 can only reassign pixels to class 0 and preserves zero predictions. For methods with post-arg max refinement (such as Trident’s SAM refinement), Eq. (E.1) serves as an empirical expectation tested below. 

Layer 1: verification on frozen outputs. Across the frozen S2 and S3 collision manifests, we classify each (method, dataset, background word, query) collision as exact sole-name (E-type : verbatim string equality with |𝑐 | = 1 ;92 instances across 17 dataset–class pairs and 18 cells, involving bed , curtain , fence , hill , mountain , river ,

road , shelf , and wood ) or other (T-type : normalized/token-level match or multi-synonym 𝑐 ; 188 instances). Exact collisions make a class unpredictable under the official post-processing: all 92/92 E-type instances receive 0 predicted pixels under BGB (and 6/6 receive 0 under O on original COCO-Object), including Trident with SAM refinement, whereas 0/92 remain zero under NC . By contrast, T-type collisions are not structurally zeroed, retaining a median predicted pixel count of approximately 0.75 of NC .

E-type classes (which have IoU 0 under BGB and recover to 0.2–56.0 under NC , e.g., 45.0–56.0 for bed 

on COCO-Object) account for 97% of Δvoc on COCO-Object and 70–71% on the COCO-Stuff variants.

## F. Implementation and numerical reproducibility 

This appendix documents hardware allocation and pixel-level cross-GPU reproducibility supporting Section 3.5. All experiments from V2-P2 through S5 ran under PyTorch 2.1.2, CUDA 12.1, mmcv 2.1.0, mmengine 0.10.1, and mmsegmentation 1.2.2 (V2 ran on an RTX 3090 Ti with PyTorch 2.6.0+cu124); V2-P2, V4, and S1 used RTX 4080 SUPER (32 GB) GPUs, whereas V3, V5, S2, S3, and S5 ran on RTX 3090 (24 GB) GPUs, and S4 rendered on both (RTX 4080 SUPER, then RTX 3090). Repository commits and SHA-256 checksums for weights and frozen files are recorded in the released archive. In S4, re-rendering 39 SCLIP THR and NC image–setting pairs on the original RTX 3090 reproduced all 39 per-image class intersection and union counts bit-for-bit (0.000 difference). Rendering the same pairs on an RTX 4080 SUPER produced small numerical pixel flips—specifically 349 of 230,400 pixels and 204 of 307,200 pixels on two COCO-Object THR images—of which 1 and 8 pixels fell on classes absent from ground truth, shifting single-image mIoU by −0 .96 and −1 .83 points. Such flips affect only borderline pixels; consistent with Section 3.5, we do not interpret dataset-level differences below 0.15 mIoU.
