Abstract
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) are widely
used for image segmentation. However, existing methods
either use a decoder or use patch-based approach which
trades boundary precision against the number of decisions
as coarse grid cells mix foreground and background pix-
els, while finer grids require many more predictions, and
autoregressive mask generation adds sequential decoding
overhead. We propose SPiXSeg, which shifts the output
from per-patch to per-superpixel label assignment, using
superpixel tokens formed by aggregating patch-level visual
features. An off-the-shelf model partitions each image into
task-agnostic superpixels, so the partition is computed once
and reused across tasks.
A Spix-to-Mask (S2M) module
scores each superpixel token in a single MLLM forward
pass by directly comparing the MLLM’s hidden representa-
tions, without autoregressive generation or a separate scor-
ing network, and the selected superpixels are mapped di-
rectly to pixels without refinement. Without any trainable
decoder head or post-hoc refinement, SPiXSeg achieves an
average cIoU of 80.5 with LLaVA-1.5-7B and 81.9 with
InternVL2.5-8B on RefCOCO, RefCOCO+ and RefCOCOg
and, when trained only on COCO-Stuff, extends to open-
vocabulary semantic segmentation with mIoU of 29.0, 59.5
and 95.4 on ADE20K, PASCAL Context and PASCAL VOC,
outperforming several SoTA MLLM baselines. Our results
suggest that a fixed, query-agnostic superpixel partition is
enough for accurate language-guided segmentation.

Existing vocabularies and methods can be broadly
grouped into four types. First, methods requiring a decoder
after the MLLM, where a special token (e.g., <seg>) is
generated, and its hidden state is converted into a mask by
a separate decoder [21, 32–34, 39]. This adds additional
decoder overhead for every query. Second, methods with
learned mask tokens, where masks are compressed into dis-
crete tokens by a trained tokenizer [37, 55]. These meth-

ods usually require multi-stage training where a tokenizer
is first trained using VQ-VAE [36], then the tokenizer is
aligned with the MLLM and then the whole pipeline is
trained end-to-end. Third, methods making patch-level pre-
dictions, where a label is assigned for every patch of a reg-
ular grid [22, 26, 35]. Here, the patch size sets a trade-off
between mask quality and sequence length. Large patches
produce coarse masks that straddle object boundaries and
mix foreground with background, while small patches re-
cover finer detail but require substantially more decisions.
Lastly, methods relying on object proposals, where one
or more proposals from a detector are selected [6, 52].
Proposal-based methods can only output whole detected
objects, so they cannot support sub-object segmentation.
Moreover, the actual segmentation is done by the detector,
and the MLLM only assigns a label, a task that a simple
CLIP-style model [31] could handle.
From the above discussion, we emphasize on four prop-
erties that are desired from a vocabulary: (i) it should re-
quire fewer decisions/predictions per mask/query, (ii) en-
ables precise and accurate masks/boundaries, (iii) should
be able to recover mask directly from MLLM, without a
decoder, and (iv) manifests a form that can be natively in-
tegrated into MLLM and can be reused across queries and
various tasks. In this work, we observe that superpixels [2],
which partition an image into non-overlapping regions, sat-
isfy these properties. Our core idea is to use off-the-shelf
models to obtain high-quality superpixels for an image and
to represent any mask as a union of superpixels. The prob-
lem then reduces to integrating superpixels into an MLLM
and classifying each superpixel as inside or outside the
mask. Since each superpixel groups several pixels into one,
the MLLM makes one prediction per superpixel instead of
one per pixel. This reduces the number of decisions while
retaining accuracy. No additional decoder is needed as the
superpixel classifications directly select the relevant super-
pixels, which are assembled into the final mask. Because
superpixels are task-agnostic, they can serve as a general
vocabulary for various segmentation tasks, as illustrated in
Fig. 1. Current literature uses superpixels as input tokens,
but are limited to perception tasks like VQA [8] or fail to na-
tively integrate them for dense tasks like segmentation [44].
In this work, we integrate superpixels natively into MLLM
for general-purpose dense segmentation tasks.
Based on this, we propose SPiXSeg. Here, we first ex-
tract superpixels from an off-the-shelf model.
Next, we
compute a token for each superpixel by pooling the patch-
level embeddings of the MLLM vision tower over the super-
pixel region. The superpixel tokens, together with the text
and vision tokens, are processed by the transformer back-
bone of the MLLM in a single forward pass. Then, a Spix-
to-Mask (S2M) module classifies each superpixel as inside
or outside the mask. S2M does this by comparing the hid-
den state of the superpixel token with the hidden states of
the visual tokens inside that superpixel. This is done with-
out any additional layer on top of the MLLM. Lastly, the
mask is obtained as the union of the superpixels classified
as inside. An overview of our method is presented in Fig. 2.
We evaluate SPiXSeg on dense tasks like referring seg-
mentation (RefCOCO/+/g) and open-vocabulary semantic
segmentation. With LLaVA-1.5-7B as the backbone, our
method achieves a mean cIoU of 80.5 on referring segmen-
tation, which is 3.5 to 5.6 points above the state-of-the-art
(SoTA) baselines. Our method also beats baselines with
multi-stage training and those that use heavier models like
RAS [6] by significant margin. Further, on semantic seg-
mentation evals, SPiXSeg obtains mIoU of 95.4 and 59.5 on
PASCAL VOC-20 and PASCAL Context-59 respectively.
In summary, the main contributions are:
• We propose SPiXSeg which uses superpixels as the vo-
cabulary for segmentation. Particularly, we recast MLLM
segmentation as classification of superpixels. The intu-
ition being that any mask can be represented as union of
superpixels, hence segmentation can be boiled down to
classification of superpixels as inside or outside the mask.
This way, our method is decoder free, precise, and doesn’t
require any multi-stage training.
• Our method first tokenizes each superpixel by aggregat-
ing MLLM’s own patch-embeddings (Section 3.2), the
transformer backbone then processes the text, vision and
superpixel tokens in a single forward pass. A Spix-to-
Mask (S2M) module is introduced, which classifies su-
perpixels by comparing hidden states of the MLLM, with
no additional layer added on top (Section 3.3).
• We obtain SoTA results on RefCOCO/+/g with a mean
cIoU of 80.5 with LLaVA-1.5-7B which is an im-
provement over existing baselines of about 3.5 to 5.6
points. We also show competitive performance on open-
vocabulary semantic segmentation and generalized refer-
ring segmentation.

pixels into compact regions that follow image bound-
aries and partition a given image.
Recent off-the-shelf
segmenters can produce such partitions at the sub-object
level [7, 8, 19]. Superpixel-like tokens have been used as
inputs in several works. E.g., SPiT [1] replaces the patch
grid of vision transformers with superpixels, EPOC [8] to-
kenizes images at the sub-object level for perception tasks
such as VQA, and SVP [44] repurposes SAM segments as
visual projectors for referring segmentation. These works
either target perception or still obtain the mask from a sepa-
rate decoder. In contrast, our method uses superpixels as the
primary vocabulary of the MLLM for segmentation. This
allows us to re-cast segmentation as a binary classification
over superpixels as ‘inside’ or ‘outside’ the mask.
3. Methodology
3.1. Preliminaries and Notation

4. Results and Experiments
In this section, we present the performance of our pro-
posed method across tasks such as referring segmentation
(RES), referring comprehension (REC), and semantic seg-
mentation.
We follow same benchmarking protocols as
Text4Seg [22] for each task, and compare against both spe-
cialized segmentation models and generalist segmentation
models with comparable parameter counts.
Our method
demonstrates competitive performance across all bench-
marks, validating the effectiveness of our approach. We
use LLaVA-1.5-7B as the main MLLM with Vicuna-7B
as the transformer backbone and CLIP vision tower for
fair comparisons; however we also show results with with
InternVL2.5-8B (InternLM2.5-7B [5]). Further, we use off-
the-shelf SAM3.1 [7] to obtain superpixels. More details
regarding superpixel extraction, training and additional ex-
periments are provided in Supplementary material.
Baselines. We compare our method against a range of
baselines, categorised into two groups: (i) specialised seg-
mentation models, which are designed explicitly for seg-
mentation tasks and often rely on task-specific architectures
or training recipes; and (ii) generalist segmentation models,
which are built on top of LLMs and aim to perform segmen-
tation through in-context learning, dedicated decoder heads,
or other architectural adaptations. For semantic segmenta-
tion, we include CLIP-based specialists [10, 16, 23, 40, 41,
49] and the MLLM-based LaSagnA [38], HiMTok [37] and
Text4Seg [22].
For fair comparison, we mainly include generalist mod-
els with 8B parameters or fewer. The exception is RAS,
which we also report with its 13B model, since its 7B model
reports only the average cIoU.
Datasets. For semantic segmentation, we evaluate on three
datasets:
ADE20K [53, 54] (150 classes), Pascal Con-
text [30] (59 classes) and Pascal VOC [13] (20 classes). For
RES, we evaluate on the three standard benchmarks: Re-
fCOCO, RefCOCO+, and RefCOCOg [48], which contain
80 object categories and varying numbers of referring ex-
pressions per image.
4.1. Referring Expression Segmentation
Referring Expression Segmentation (RES) requires the
model to segment an object based on the given image and
a natural language description. We evaluate RES perfor-
mance using class-aware Intersection-over-Union (cIoU)
across the three standard benchmarks: RefCOCO, Ref-
COCO+, and RefCOCOg. Our results are presented in Ta-
ble 1.
With InternVL2.5, SPiXSeg reaches 81.9, and with
LLaVA-1.5 80.5, both above the best baselines SAMTok

4.4. Additional Results
In this section, we study how the choice of superpixels
affects SPiXSeg, compare its inference, time with other
paradigms, and present qualitative results on out-of-domain
images.
Choice of Superpixels. Since SPiXSeg segments by se-
lecting superpixels, its accuracy is bounded by how well
the superpixels follow the object boundaries. We call the
best cIoU reachable by selecting superpixels the ‘ceiling’.
Replacing SAM 3.1 with EPOC superpixels [8] lowers the
ceiling by about 9 points as shown in Table 4, yet SPiXSeg
reaches a similar share of it (92.4 vs. 93.8%) and selects
nearly the same superpixels as the ceiling with either set as
shown in Fig. 5.

5. Conclusion
In this work, we revisited the vocabulary in which MLLMs
express masks for segmentation. Existing vocabularies each
have a limitation. Decoder-based methods add per-query
overhead, learned mask tokens require multi-stage train-
ing, grid-based methods trade boundary precision against
the number of decisions, and proposal-based methods only
8


output whole objects. We showed that superpixels satisfy
the four properties desired from a vocabulary and proposed
SPiXSeg, which recasts MLLM segmentation as superpixel
classification. Superpixels are computed once per image
and classified by S2M in a single forward pass, without
any decoder or refinement. SPiXSeg achieves state-of-the-
art results among LLaVA-1.5-7B methods, with a mean
cIoU of 80.5 on RefCOCO/+/g, and extends naturally to
open-vocabulary semantic segmentation. One limitation is
that every mask is a union of superpixels, so accuracy is
bounded by the superpixel extraction model and boundaries
it misses cannot be recovered. Since SPiXSeg reaches a
similar share of this bound with different extractors, we ex-
pect better extraction models to act as drop-in replacements
that improve masks without architectural changes.

We used LLMs, particularly through the coding agents, in
three parts of this work. Coding: we used it to write and
debug parts of the training, evaluation and analysis code.
Paraphrasing: we used it to rephrase and edit text that we
wrote, for clarity and grammar. TikZ diagrams: we used
it to write the TikZ code for the method and overview di-
agrams, following layouts that we designed. All research
ideas, experimental design, results and conclusions are our

ber in the paper comes from our own experiments; and ev-
ery qualitative result is an actual output of our model or of
a baseline, shown on benchmark images or on openly li-
censed photographs and artworks. No image or mask was
generated or edited with generative AI. We take full respon-

we study (LLaVA-1.5 and InternVL2.5) are the subject of
the research, not writing or coding tools, and are described
in the main text.

6. Implementation details
In this section, we provide the settings and implementation
details of the proposed method as described in Sec. 3 of the
main paper.
Superpixel Extraction. As described in the main text,
SPiXSeg method relies on superpixels and treats it as the
fundamental unit for segmentation. However, SPiXSeg is
not tied to a specific superpixel extractor. In our experi-
ments, we use SAM 3.1 [7] by default and EPOC [8] as
an alternative.
With SAM 3.1, we resize the image to
1024×1024 and prompt the model with a regular (uncondi-
tional) 32×32 grid of points. However, in such regular grid
points, neighboring points often produce the same mask,
so we remove the near-duplicates with non-maximum sup-
pression at a box IoU of 0.7, and the top 64 masks with the
highest predicted IoU are retained. Since SAM masks are
nested, for example, a face lies inside a person; we assign
each pixel to exactly one mask going through the masks
from the largest to the smallest, so that each part remains a
separate superpixel and the larger mask containing it covers
only the remaining area. Small uncovered areas and super-
pixels below 1024 pixels are merged into a neighboring su-
perpixel. This gives at most N=64 superpixels per image,
on RefCOCO we observe the average number of superpix-
els per image to be 50. For EPOC, the superpixels are deter-
mined by the threshold on its predicted boundary map, we
choose this threshold to be 0.1.
Model. For LLaVA-1.5-7B, we take visual features from
the penultimate CLIP layer at a resolution of 3362. For
InternVL2.5-8B, the input is 8962, split into four tiles of
4482. In both cases, the vision encoder and the projector
are frozen, and the LLM is fine-tuned with LoRA of rank
64. The positional MLP and espix in Eq. (3) and the scalars
α and β in Eq. (4) are trained from scratch.
Training. We train the pipeline using losses mentioned in
Eq. (5) and Eq. (6). One of the edge cases while train-
ing is when none of the superpixels have more than half
of its area inside the ground truth, in that case, we use the
one with the largest overlap as the positive. The losses of
Eq. (5) and Eq. (6) have equal weight of 1.0. We train with
AdamW at a learning rate of 10−4 without weight decay,
an effective batch of 64, linear warm-up over the first 3%
of steps and cosine decay after it, gradient clipping at 0.3
and bf16. Since many expressions refer to spatial loca-
tions as ‘left’ and ‘right’, the images are resized to a square
without flips or crops. For RES, we train on the RefCLEF
and RefCOCO/+/g train splits (430K expressions similar to
Text4Seg [22]) for 10 epochs with LLaVA-1.5-7B and for 5
epochs with InternVL2.5-8B. During inference, if none of
the superpixel reaches pi > 0.5, we keep the one with the
highest score.
Other tasks. For Generalized RES (gRES), we train from
the base MLLM for 5 epochs on gRefCOCO and add a bi-
nary cross-entropy term on maxi si, which tells whether the
expression has a target at all. Unlike in RES, an empty
selection is kept and read as ‘no target’. For ReasonSeg,
we start from the RES-finetuned model model and fine-tune
it for 2,000 steps on batches drawn from data-mix of RES
(35%), VQA on the COCO images of LLaVA-665K (25%,
one 4482 tile, text loss), LISA++ (25%) and the ReasonSeg
train split (15%). For semantic segmentation, we train the
model for 5 epochs with up to 63 class names per prompt.
7. Additional experiments
7.1. Generalized Referring Segmentation
Generalized referring segmentation (gRES) refers to task
where the expression points to multiple or none of the sub-
jects present in the image. gRefCOCO [24] contains such
expressions that refer to several objects or to none. We train
SPiXSeg on its train split for 5 epochs, with LLaVA-1.5-7B
and InternVL2.5-8B. An extra loss on the highest super-
pixel score teaches the model whether the target exists; if
no superpixel is selected, the predicted mask is empty. Ta-
ble 5 presents our results. With InternVL2.5-8B, SPiXSeg
performs the best in every split, and with LLaVA-1.5 our
method is second best. We present and compare qualitative
examples in Fig. 8. It can be seen that our method is able to
segment out precise objects and is able to avoid segmenta-
tion where the referred object is not present in challenging
scenarios.

8. Error Analysis
We present an error analysis of our method in Table 7 to
illustrate where does our method lose the maximum achiev-
able accuracy. Table 7 splits the cIoU that SPiXSeg (with
InternVL2.5-8B) loses on RefCOCO/+/g into errors of the
model and the limit set by the superpixels (ceiling). The
ceiling is the cIoU of the training targets i.e., the highest
achievable cIoU. The ceiling cIoU is 88.9 on average, so
11.1 cIoU is lost to the superpixel segmentation inaccuracy
irrespective of our method. It can be seen that on top of that,
our method loses 7.0 points, of which 3.9 comes from pick-
ing the wrong object or missing it, and 2.2 from picking the
right object with missing or extra pieces. It can be seen that
picking wrong objects costs the most on RefCOCO+ testB
(10.8 cIoU), where expressions do not use location words.
We provide visualization of each of these errors in Fig. 10.

in Fig. 13 for ADE20K-150.
9.4. Region Budget
SPiXSeg is trained with at most K=64 superpixels per im-
age, but K can be changed at test time by running the super-
pixel extraction with a smaller limit, without retraining the
model. Fig. 14 shows the predictions for K = 16, 32, 48
and 64, together with the ceiling, i.e. the best performance
that any selection of the superpixels can reach. With few
superpixels, the target is merged with its surroundings, re-
sulting in coarser superpixels, so even the best selection is
poor; at K=16 the ceiling is at most 10 IoU in all four ex-
amples. As K grows, the target is covered by its own super-
pixels and the ceiling rises. From K=32 on, our prediction
reaches the ceiling in every example, so the remaining er-
rors at small K come from the superpixels and not from
their classification.
