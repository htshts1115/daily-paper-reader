# Abstract 

Pre-trained generative models offer rich visual priors for image matting, but their adaptation often relies on varia-tional autoencoders (VAEs), whose spatial compression can degrade fine alpha details and introduce additional infer-ence overhead. To address these limitations, we propose X-Matte, a VAE-free pixel-space flow matching framework for single-step image matting. By eliminating latent compres-sion, X-Matte formulates the RGB-to-alpha transition as a continuous flow directly in pixel space. We adopt trajectory-wide x-prediction with transition-aware alpha supervision, enabling the model to learn from intermediate states while directly predicting the target alpha matte in a single for-ward pass. This design avoids both VAE encoding and decoding and iterative sampling. Extensive experiments demonstrate performance competitive with state-of-the-art trimap-free matting methods on P3M benchmarks, together with promising zero-shot generalization to RWP-636 and AIM-500. Moreover, X-Matte achieves up to a 9.4× infer-ence speedup over a constructed single-step latent coun-terpart at high resolution, demonstrating the effectiveness of VAE-free pixel-space modeling for accurate and efficient image matting. 

# 1. Introduction 

Image matting estimates the continuous per-pixel opacity of foreground objects, known as the alpha matte [3, 14, 15]. Unlike binary segmentation, matting requires accurate re-covery of fine structures and semi-transparent boundaries, such as hair and fur, where small prediction errors can pro-duce visible artifacts in image compositing [5, 28]. Recent discriminative methods, ranging from specialized CNNs to Vision Transformers (ViTs), commonly formulate mat-ting as dense pixel-wise regression [6, 12, 45]. Despite 1arXiv:2611.33874v1 [cs.CV] 25 Nov 2026 their strong performance, generalization to unseen fore-ground objects and imaging conditions remains challeng-ing [16, 46]. Pre-trained generative models offer a promis-ing alternative by providing rich visual priors learned from large-scale image data. The key challenge is therefore how to adapt these priors to pixel-accurate alpha estimation without sacrificing fine structures or inference efficiency. Existing generative matting methods, many of which build on Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs) [31], largely fall into two paradigms. Iterative latent generation en-codes alpha mattes into the latent space of a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) [8, 38] and commonly recovers them through iterative noise-to-alpha sampling [33, 38]. How-ever, such VAEs are designed for natural RGB images rather than pixel-accurate alpha estimation, and their compres-sion can discard spatial information to which fine struc-tures and semi-transparent boundaries are particularly sen-sitive, while iterative sampling adds substantial overhead to what is ultimately a deterministic prediction. Fixed-timestep regression instead applies a pre-trained diffusion backbone at a fixed timestep for efficient dense prediction [34, 41, 42, 52], often with task-specific decoders that re-cover fine details from latent features [41, 42]. However, this paradigm still relies on compressed representations, in-curs VAE overhead at high resolutions, and does not explic-itly exploit supervision along a task-specific RGB-to-alpha trajectory. Both paradigms further face a resolution gap, as generative backbones are typically pre-trained at fixed res-olutions far below those required for high-fidelity matting. These limitations raise a natural question: Can a pre-trained generative model predict alpha mattes directly in pixel space, without a VAE interface or iterative sam-pling? We address this question with X-Matte , which builds on a pre-trained pixel-space generative backbone and formulates the RGB-to-alpha transition as a continu-ous flow, as illustrated in Figure 1. Rather than encoding alpha into an RGB-oriented latent space or regressing from a single fixed state, X-Matte learns across the RGB-to-alpha trajectory and operates directly at native image resolution. This provides a direct path from generative priors to alpha estimation, avoiding VAE compression and reconstruction while retaining single-step inference. Specifically, X-Matte addresses the above limitations in three ways. First, we directly adapt the pre-trained pixel-space backbone for RGB-to-alpha prediction, removing the dependence on compressed representations and the associ-ated encoding and decoding overhead. Second, we adopt 

x-prediction to estimate the target alpha at intermediate timesteps, enabling trajectory-wide training with transition-aware alpha supervision. At inference, the original RGB image serves as the starting state, allowing the model to pre-dict the alpha matte in a single forward pass. Thus, unlike fixed-timestep regression, single-step inference does not re-strict training to a single state (Table 4). Third, we adapt po-sitional embeddings during fine-tuning and interpolate them for different inference resolutions, bridging the gap between generative pre-training and high-resolution matting. Experiments on P3M benchmarks demonstrate competi-tive matting accuracy, while zero-shot evaluations on RWP-636 and AIM-500 show cross-domain generalization. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • VAE-Free Pixel-Space Matting. We introduce X-Matte, a generative matting framework that directly adapts pre-trained pixel-space models for RGB-to-alpha prediction, eliminating VAE compression and reconstruction. • Trajectory-Wide Alpha Prediction. We formulate a task-specific RGB-to-alpha flow with x-prediction and transition-aware supervision, enabling training across in-termediate states and single-step inference. • Accurate and Efficient Matting. X-Matte achieves competitive accuracy on P3M benchmarks, demonstrates zero-shot generalization to RWP-636 and AIM-500, and delivers up to a 9.4× inference speedup over a con-structed single-step latent counterpart. 

# 2. Related Work 

2.1. Discriminative Image Matting 

Image matting has predominantly been formulated as dis-criminative pixel-wise regression. Early CNN-based meth-ods, including Deep Image Matting [43] and FBA Mat-ting [7], focus on recovering high-frequency alpha details. Trimap-free methods such as MODNet [12] and Back-ground Matting [21, 32] further remove manual guidance for portrait or fixed-background settings. More recently, Vision Transformers have been introduced to matting to ex-ploit global context and pre-trained visual representations [2, 27, 45, 47]. State-of-the-art discriminative systems also employ substantial task-specific design; for example, P3M-Net [16] combines dual decoders with domain-specific aug-mentation for privacy-preserving portrait matting. 

2.2. Generative Priors and the Latent Bottleneck 

Recent methods adapt pre-trained LDMs [31] to image mat-ting, but two mismatches remain. 

Stochasticity–determinism conflict. Natural image synthesis models a distribution, whereas supervised matting associates each input with a deterministic target. Multi-step diffusion methods [10, 38, 44] therefore require ad-ditional mechanisms, including trajectory correction [44], customized decoding [38], or iterative refinement [10]. Latent representation paradox. LDM-based genera-tive matting methods typically rely on VAEs [13, 31] pre-trained on natural RGB images, whose perceptually ori-ented latent representations are not explicitly designed to preserve continuous alpha values and fine transition bound-aries. Existing approaches either encode alpha mattes into RGB-oriented latent spaces [8, 11, 38] or employ task-specific decoders to recover fine details from compressed features [41, 42, 44]. To better accommodate alpha in-formation, recent studies explore transparency-aware rep-resentations, alpha-aware VAEs, and layered RGBA gen-eration [40, 48, 50], while other work leverages diffusion priors for joint foreground and alpha prediction [20] or reads alpha mattes directly from text-to-image DiT tokens while keeping the VAE encoder [37]. Rather than adapting RGB-oriented latent representations for alpha modeling, X-Matte removes the VAE interface entirely and formulates the RGB-to-alpha mapping directly in pixel space, avoiding latent compression while preserving access to fine-grained spatial information for alpha prediction. 

2.3. Pixel-Space Generation and Flow Matching 

To circumvent the semantic misalignment inherent in VAE-based latent spaces, the generative frontier has recently piv-oted back to native pixel-space modeling [4, 19, 39]. Pixel-space flow matching formulates the generative process di-rectly at the original image resolution, establishing a strict end-to-end mapping paradigm that has demonstrated re-markable success in high-fidelity RGB image synthesis [4]. This naturally raises a compelling question: how can pre-trained pixel-space generative priors be effectively adapted to high-fidelity image matting, while preserving fine alpha details and avoiding the limitations of VAE-based latent representations? However, adapting a pre-trained pixel-space backbone to a deterministic task like matting presents a fundamen-tal challenge: modeling continuous generative trajectories in high-dimensional pixel space remains difficult. Recent pixel-space generative modeling shows that x-prediction stabilizes raw-pixel learning by anchoring predictions to the data manifold [19]. Motivated by this finding, we adopt the time-conditioned Transformer architecture of SiT [26] with a VAE-free pixel interface, replacing latent encoding and decoding with direct pixel patch embedding and pre-diction. Building on pixel-space generative pre-training, we further introduce a matting-specific RGB-to-alpha flow with trajectory-wide x-prediction and task-aware supervi-sion, enabling direct alpha estimation without iterative sam-pling (Section 3). 

# 3. Method 

We present X-Matte, a VAE-free pixel-space flow match-ing framework for image matting, as illustrated in Fig-ure 2. X-Matte formulates the RGB-to-alpha transition as 3a continuous flow in pixel space, using trajectory-wide x-prediction during training and direct endpoint prediction for single-step inference. We describe the pixel-space flow for-mulation (Section 3.1), transition-aware supervision (Sec-tion 3.2), and resolution-adaptive positional transfer (Sec-tion 3.3) in detail. 

3.1. Pixel-Space Flow Matching with x-prediction 

Flow formulation. Given an RGB image x ∈ RH×W ×3

and its alpha matte α ∈ [0 , 1] H×W , we replicate α to three channels ( α3c) and define an RGB-to-alpha interpolation di-rectly in pixel space: 

zt = t α 3c + (1 − t) x, t ∈ [0 , 1] , (1) where z0 = x is the observed RGB image and z1 = α3c is the target matte. Rather than explicitly predicting the veloc-ity, the time-conditioned network fθ is trained to estimate the alpha endpoint at each sampled timestep: 

Lgen = Et∼U (0 ,1) ∥fθ (zt, t ) − α3c∥1 . (2) Unlike conventional noise-to-alpha generation, the ob-served RGB image serves as the source of the interpola-tion rather than an auxiliary conditioning signal. Sampling t

across the interpolation path exposes the model to interme-diate RGB-to-alpha states, providing trajectory-wide end-point supervision instead of restricting training to the RGB starting state. 

Backbone and pixel interface. We adopt the time-conditioned Transformer architecture of SiT [26], equipped with a convolutional pixel patch embedding and a lin-ear prediction head. Unlike the original latent-space SiT pipeline, our backbone is pre-trained directly in pixel space on ImageNet, without VAE encoding or decoding. The same pixel-space interface is retained during matting fine-tuning, allowing the pre-trained generative weights to be transferred to RGB-to-alpha prediction. Since the flow starts from the observed RGB image, X-Matte can lever-age the visual priors learned during pixel-space pre-training without relying on latent representations. 

Why x-prediction. Recent pixel-space generative model-ing has demonstrated the effectiveness of direct clean-data prediction [19]. For image matting, we adopt x-prediction to directly estimate the alpha endpoint ˆαt = fθ (zt, t ) at each sampled timestep. This provides an interpretable pre-diction in the alpha domain, allowing task-specific recon-struction and boundary losses to supervise fine structures and semi-transparent regions throughout the RGB-to-alpha trajectory. While ϵ- or v-prediction can also recover the alpha endpoint through an additional transformation, x-prediction offers a more direct interface for transition-aware supervision, as detailed in Section 3.2. 

Single-step endpoint prediction. The RGB-to-alpha in-terpolation in Eq. (1) defines a linear path with constant tar-get velocity v∗ = α3c − x. Given the predicted endpoint 

ˆαt = fθ (zt, t ), the corresponding velocity can be parame-terized as

Although inference uses only t=0 , we view the interpola-tion path as structured supervision rather than a sampling trajectory, consistent with Table 6, where additional Eu-ler steps do not help. Intermediate states reveal the alpha only partially, so predicting the endpoint from them forms a range of easier, related subproblems that share one network with the t=0 task; we hypothesize that this regularizes the single-step prediction. Under the same backbone, it reduces SAD from 36.87 to 22.16 relative to fixed-timestep regres-sion (Table 4). 

3.2. Transition-Aware Trajectory Supervision 

Direct endpoint prediction provides an interpretable al-pha estimate at each sampled timestep, allowing matting-specific reconstruction and boundary losses [7, 43] to su-pervise the RGB-to-alpha trajectory. 

Loss formulation. We combine a trajectory-wide alpha regression objective with a transition-aware auxiliary loss to jointly supervise alpha accuracy and fine boundary struc-tures: 

L = Lreg + Lta , (4) where Lreg applies spatial weighting to the endpoint regres-sion objective in Eq. (2), encouraging accurate alpha pre-diction across the sampled RGB-to-alpha trajectory. Mean-while, Lta combines an additional weighted alpha recon-struction term with gradient supervision that emphasizes fine boundaries [43]. These complementary objectives en-courage the model to maintain overall alpha accuracy while recovering subtle local structures, particularly around hair strands and semi-transparent boundaries. 

Transition-aware weighting. To emphasize semi-transparent regions and fine boundaries, we apply an alpha-dependent spatial weighting scheme to the recon-struction terms, together with gradient supervision [30, 43]. Most pixels belong to fully opaque foreground or trans-parent background regions, while sparse transition regions contain fine structures and fractional alpha values that require precise estimation. We therefore assign greater importance to intermediate alpha values while retaining supervision over opaque and transparent pixels. Applied 4throughout the RGB-to-alpha trajectory, this weighting encourages fine-detail recovery at different timesteps. Meanwhile, gradient supervision complements pixel-wise reconstruction by penalizing local discrepancies along object boundaries and thin hair structures. Both objectives operate directly in the alpha domain without additional inference-time processing. Table 5 and Figure 4 demonstrate the effectiveness of this design. 

3.3. Resolution-Adaptive Positional Embeddings 

The backbone is pre-trained at 256 × 256 , while image matting benefits from higher-resolution inputs for recover-ing fine spatial details [22]. However, increasing the in-put resolution produces a denser token grid, creating a mis-match with the positional representations learned during pre-training. To bridge this resolution gap, we adopt learn-able positional embeddings during fine-tuning at 768 px [6, 24]. This allows the positional representations to adapt to the finer spatial layout required for alpha prediction while retaining the pre-trained pixel-space backbone. At infer-ence, the learned embeddings are bicubically interpolated to match the input token grid, allowing the same model to process different resolutions and aspect ratios without ad-ditional fine-tuning [1]. This resolution-adaptive strategy enables flexible inference without modifying the backbone architecture or introducing additional refinement modules. The effects of positional adaptation are quantitatively eval-uated in Table 5, while Fig. 5 illustrates the recovery of fine boundary structures at different inference resolutions. 

# 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Dataset. We train and evaluate on P3M-10k [16], the largest privacy-preserving portrait matting benchmark. The training set contains 9,421 high-resolution face-blurred por-trait images with annotated alpha mattes. Evaluation is conducted on two test sets: P3M-500-P, comprising 500 face-blurred images following the privacy-preserving train-ing (PPT) setting, and P3M-500-NP, comprising 500 nor-mal celebrity portraits without face obfuscation. Evaluat-ing on both sets measures in-distribution accuracy (P3M-500-P) and cross-domain generalization (P3M-500-NP) si-multaneously. To further evaluate cross-dataset generaliza-tion, we additionally test on RWP-636 [49], a real-world portrait matting benchmark containing 636 high-resolution images collected in diverse real-world conditions, with-out fine-tuning on this dataset. We further evaluate zero-shot generalization on AIM-500 [17], a natural image mat-ting benchmark of 500 images spanning portraits, animals, plants, and transparent objects, most of which are absent from P3M training. 

Evaluation metrics. We report four standard matting evaluation metrics [30] (lower is better): SAD and MSE as-sess pixel-level alpha prediction accuracy, while Grad and Conn evaluate the preservation of fine boundary details and structural connectivity, respectively. 

Implementation details. We pre-train X-Matte-B (140M) and X-Matte-H (∼0.9B) on ImageNet-1k at 

256 ×256 following the x-prediction recipe of [19]. Both are fine-tuned at 768 px with learnable positional em-beddings (Section 3.3). Full hyperparameters are in the supplementary material. 

Inference. We bicubically interpolate positional embed-dings on the fly for resolution-free inference. All results use single-step endpoint prediction at native aspect ratios.

4.3. Ablation Study 

Paradigm analysis. First, generative pre-training plays an important role in our framework. Without pre-training, both discriminative and pixel-space generative variants ex-hibit substantially higher errors (SAD 81.67 and 54.50, re-spectively). With ImageNet pre-training, their SAD scores decrease to 36.87 and 22.16, respectively, demonstrating the clear benefit of transferring pre-trained visual representa-tions to image matting. Second, trajectory-wide training substantially outper-forms fixed-timestep regression under the evaluated setting (SAD 22.16 vs. 36.87). Both variants use the same back-bone architecture and single-step inference, suggesting that supervision across intermediate RGB-to-alpha states pro-vides an effective training strategy beyond direct regression at t = 0 .Third, the pixel-space x-prediction variant achieves lower SAD than the latent-space v-prediction variant (22.16 vs. 25.17). Since this comparison changes both the repre-sentation space and the prediction target, we attribute the gain to their combination and leave disentangling the two factors to future work. 4.4. Computational Efficiency of VAE-Free Design

# 5. Conclusion 

We presented X-Matte, a VAE-free pixel-space flow match-ing framework for single-step image matting. By replac-ing the latent diffusion interface with a direct RGB-to-alpha flow and trajectory-wide x-prediction, X-Matte avoids VAE compression and enables direct matting-aware supervision throughout training. It achieves accuracy competitive with state-of-the-art trimap-free matting methods on P3M bench-marks, promising zero-shot generalization to RWP-636 and AIM-500, and up to a 9.4× inference speedup for X-Matte-B over a constructed single-step latent counterpart at high resolution. The improvements from X-Matte-B to X-Matte-H suggest that the framework can benefit from larger back-bones. As an automatic trimap-free method, X-Matte may struggle when foreground selection is ambiguous. As il-lustrated in the supplementary material, the model may in-clude additional instances of the same category or absorb adjacent dark objects and shadows into the predicted matte, particularly when foreground and background appearances are similar. These errors arise partly from foreground selec-tion ambiguity rather than boundary reconstruction alone, due to the lack of instance-level guidance. Incorporating lightweight spatial guidance, such as points or bounding boxes, could help resolve these ambiguities more effec-tively in challenging scenes. Future work includes extend-ing the RGB-to-alpha flow to video matting with tempo-ral consistency, and to other dense prediction tasks such as depth and surface normal estimation. 8References
