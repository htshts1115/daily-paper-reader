# CIRKDV2: Cross-Image Relational Knowledge Distillation with Contextual Modeling for Efficient Semantic Segmentation 

Chuanguang Yang, Yu Wang, Chengqing Yu, Xinqiang Yu, Weilun Feng, Yuqi Li, Zhulin An, Libo Huang, Boyu Diao, Fei Wang, Fuzhen Zhuang, Senior Member, IEEE , Yongjun Xu, Yingli Tian, Fellow, IEEE , Tingwen Huang, Fellow, IEEE , and Yongduan Song, Fellow, IEEE 

Abstract —Existing Knowledge Distillation (KD) methods for semantic segmentation often guide the student to mimic the teacher’s structured information generated from an individ-ual image. However, they ignore the global semantic relations across various images that are valuable for KD. This paper proposes Cross-Image Relational KD V2 (CIRKDV2), which focuses on transferring structured pixel-to-pixel, pixel-to-region, and channel-to-channel relations modeled from global images in the dataset. A large teacher network often constructs awell-structured feature space in terms of global contextual dependencies. CIRKDV2 makes the student mimic the better structured semantic relations of the teacher, thus improving the feature representational capability. Beyond relational distillation, we further propose contextual feature distillation by using context aggregation to augment student pixel embeddings to match the semantic level with the teacher. This avoids the semantic gap problem between the teacher and student occurred in traditional feature distillation studies. Experimental results on Cityscapes, ADE20K, Pascal VOC, and COCO-Stuff-164K demonstrate that CIRKDV2 outperforms the state-of-the-art semantic segmenta-tion distillation methods by large margins across various network architectures. Our code and pre-trained models are publicly available at https://github.com/winycg/CIRKD. 

Index Terms —Knowledge distillation, semantic segmentation, cross-image relations, context aggregation.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Semantic segmentation is a crucial and challenging task in computer vision. It aims to classify each pixel in the input image with an individual category label. The applications of segmentation often focus on autonomous driving, virtual reality, and robot navigation. Although popular state-of-the-art segmentation networks, such as DeepLab [1], [2], PSPNet [3], and SAM [4], achieve remarkable performance, they are often equipped with large image encoders, leading to high computational costs. This weakness makes them difficult to deploy for real-world scenarios on resource-limited mobile devices. Model compression is an effective way to pursue compact networks, mainly divided into quantization [5]–[7], pruning [8], [9], and knowledge distillation (KD) [10]–[12]. This paper investigates KD to improve the performance of a compact student network under the guidance of a high-capacity teacher network for semantic segmentation. A broad range of KD approaches [10], [13], [14] have been well studied but mostly for image classification tasks. Unlike image-level recognition, the segmentation task aims at dense pixel pre-dictions, which is more challenging. Previous researches [15] have found that directly utilizing classification-based KD methods to deal with dense prediction tasks may not achieve desirable performance. This is because strictly aligning the coarse feature maps between the teacher and student networks arXiv:2611.34795v1 [cs.CV] 25 Nov 2026 2

may lead to negative constraints and ignore the structured context among pixels. Recent studies attempt to propose specialized KD meth-ods [16]–[23] for semantic segmentation, mainly divided into relation- and feature-based distillation methods. Most relation-based distillation studies focus on mining correlations or dependencies among spatial pixel locations because segmen-tation needs a structured output. Typical knowledge can be local pixel affinity [16], [21], global pairwise relations [17], [18], [22] and intra-class pixel variation [19]. Such methods often perform better than traditional point-wise alignment in capturing structured spatial knowledge. However, previous segmentation KD methods often guide a student to mimic the teacher’s structured information generated from an individual image sample . They ignore cross-image semantic relations among pixels for knowledge transfer, as shown in Figure 1. Therefore, they only construct narrow context but cannot capture wide contextual dependencies across global im-ages. 

To address this problem, we propose Cross-Image Rela-tional Knowledge Distillation V2 (CIRKDV2) for semantic segmentation. To capture wide contextual dependencies, we aim to construct global relations across the whole training images from both the teacher and student. A good pre-trained teacher network with high performance could often generate a well-structured embedding space and capture better correlations than a student network. Based on this property, we distill pixel-to-pixel, pixel-to-region, and channel-to-channel relations across various images from the teacher to the student. This helps the student fully explore multi-dimensional struc-tured relations to learn long-range contextual dependencies. The pixel-to-pixel and channel-to-channel distillation aims to transfer similarity distributions among pixel and channel embeddings, respectively. The channel embedding means a semantic map along the channel dimension of a feature map. The pixel-to-region distillation focuses on transferring pixel-to-region similarity distributions between pixel and region embeddings. The region embedding is generated by averagely pooling pixel embeddings from the same class and represents the feature center of that class. The pixel-to-region relations indicate the relative similarities between pixels and class-wise prototypes. A naive way for constructing cross-image relations is to derive embeddings from the current mini-batch. However, the batch size of the segmentation task is often small, limiting the network to capture broader pixel dependencies. Moti-vated by the memory mechanism in previous self-supervised learning [24], we introduce a pixel queue, a region queue, and a channel queue in the memory bank to store abundant embeddings for modeling long-range relations. The embed-dings in the queue are consistent in distribution during the distillation process, since they are generated from the pre-trained and frozen teacher network. We regard the teacher and student pixel embeddings from the current mini-batch as anchors, and then randomly sample contrastive pixel and region embeddings from queues to model pixel-to-pixel as well as pixel-to-region similarity distributions. In a similar manner, we regard channel embeddings from the current mini-batch as anchors, and then randomly sample channel embeddings from the channel queue to construct channel-to-channel similarity distributions. Then we align such similarity distributions via KL-divergence from the student to the teacher. This helps the student learn comprehensive structured relation knowledge from the teacher. Beyond popular relation-based distillation, feature-based distillation is also demonstrated as an important technique. Shu 

et al. [20] revealed that each channel represents a category-specific mask and thus proposed Channel-Wise Distillation (CWD). CWD demonstrates the importance of channel-level information for dense prediction tasks. More recently, LAD [23] transfers the classical FitNet idea to semantic segmentation for raw feature distillation. However, previous methods often perform direct alignment without contextual modeling for student pixels, leading to possible mismatch-ing semantics between the teacher and student. This is because the teacher is often equipped with a larger kernel than the student, which means that the teacher pixel extracts semantic information from a wider receptive field. To mitigate the semantic gap, we propose context aggregation over the spatial dimension of the student feature map to augment pixel embeddings with wider contextual information. And then we perform feature distillation of pixel embeddings between the student and teacher. This helps the student learn meaningful pixel semantics from the teacher without negative receptive field gaps. In summary, CIRKDV2 leverages pixel-to-pixel, pixel-to-region, and channel-to-channel dimensions for cross-image relational distillation, and contextual feature distillation to improve the student’s segmentation performance under the guidance of a strong teacher. We evaluate our method over popular DeepLabV3 [2], PSPNet [3], UperNet [25], and SegFormer [26] architectures with various backbones on four challenging semantic segmentation benchmark datasets: Cityscapes [27], ADE20K [28], Pascal VOC [29], and COCO-Stuff-164K [30]. Experimental results indicate that CIRKDV2 achieves the best performance among the state-of-the-art dis-tillation approaches, demonstrating the effectiveness of cross-image relational distillation and contextual feature distillation in semantic segmentation. Parts of this paper, including pixel-to-pixel and pixel-to-region distillation, were published originally in our conference version [31]. This paper extends our earlier work in several valuable aspects for relation and feature distillation. The main contributions are summarized as follows:  

> •

We propose cross-image relational KD by modeling pixel-to-pixel, pixel-to-region, and channel-to-channel re-lations assisted by a memory bank mechanism for struc-tured knowledge transfer. This helps the student learn comprehensive long-range semantic dependencies of var-ious object segments across the whole images.  

> •

We propose contextual feature distillation by using con-text aggregation to augment student pixel embeddings with wider contextual information and then performing feature alignment between the teacher and student. This also avoids the semantic mismatching problem occurred in traditional feature distillation studies. 3 

> •

CIRKDV2 achieves the best performance among the state-of-the-art distillation methods across various ar-chitectures on four challenging semantic segmentation datasets. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Knowledge Distillation for Image Classification 

The core idea of KD [10] is to transfer meaningful knowl-edge from a cumbersome teacher into a smaller and faster student. Most current KD methods deal with image clas-sification networks, mainly divided into probability-based, feature-based, and relation-based approaches. Probability-based KD [10] transfers the class probabilities produced by the teacher as soft labels to supervise the student. Feature-based KD focuses on intermediate feature maps [32] or their refined information [33] as knowledge. Relation-based KD [34]–[36] aligns correlations or dependencies among multiple instances between the student and teacher networks. However, these image-level KD methods are often unsuitable for pixel-wise semantic segmentation [15], because the latter needs more fine-grained structured knowledge. 

B. Relation-based Distillation for Semantic Segmentation 

Existing KD methods for semantic segmentation can be di-vided into relation-based and feature-based approaches, where relation KD has become a mainstream paradigm. The core idea of relation-based KD is to encode contextual pixel or channel affinity as valuable knowledge for transfer. The main differences among existing work lie in how to construct relationships. Xie et al. [16] align local similarity maps constructed from 8 neighborhood pixels between the student and teacher networks. He et al. [18] transfer non-local pairwise affinity maps using an autoencoder to minimize the discrep-ancy of features. SKD [15], [17] performs pairwise similarity distillation among pixels and an adversarial distillation of score maps. IFVD [19] distills intra-class feature variation to learn more robust relations with class-wise prototypes. DSD [37] constructs pixel-wise and category-wise similarity distillation, where the former uses residual attention maps to strengthen fine-grained spatial relations, and the latter aims to learn category correlation. APD [21] mines sample-wise contextual information by an adaptive local perspective. Af-DCD [22] distills dense structured relations from channel and spatial dimensions primarily within the current image or mini-batch feature structure . CIRKDV2 changes the knowledge be-ing transferred: it models channel-to-channel relations across global images by using teacher channel embeddings stored in an online memory queue. Thus, each current student channel is not only compared with channels from the same image, but also with a broader set of teacher channel embeddings collected from previous images. This transfers dataset-level channel correlation patterns that are not available from an individual image alone. Although the above relation-based distillation methods achieve good performance, their common weakness is that the relational modeling is only limited in an individual image, which cannot capture long-range pixel dependencies. By con-trast, CIRKDV2 proposes pixel-to-pixel and pixel-to-region distillation by sampling pixels from an online memory bank. Compared to the mini-batch, the memory bank significantly increases the number of available pixels for affinity modeling. Therefore, the student can learn longer-range pixel dependen-cies. 

C. Feature-based Distillation for Semantic Segmentation 

Beyond relation-based distillation, some studies examine feature-based distillation to improve the representational capa-bility of the student network. Different from affinity modeling along the spatial dimension, CWD [20] conducts channel-wise distillation to guide each student channel to mimic the corre-sponding teacher channel, where a channel often represents a semantic mask. SSTKD [38], [39] distills structural and statistical texture features to let the student learn low-level detail information from the teacher. Attention distillation [33] has been widely used for image classification. The attention map [40] often emphasizes meaningful semantic information while ignoring meaningless regions. The core idea of attention distillation is to make the student learn critical semantic information by mimicking the teacher’s attention maps. This idea is also extended to semantic segmentation. CAT [41] distills class attention maps to guide the student to focus on class-discriminative regions of the teacher. AttnFD [42] introduces CBAM [40] to extract channel and spatial attention maps and performs alignment between teacher and student. LAD [23] revisits the seminal work FitNet [32] and conducts feature distillation from l2-normalized raw features. Being orthogonal to relation-based distillation, feature-based distillation is also a complementary way to improve the student network. Previous feature-based methods often perform simple alignment between feature maps without high-level feature modeling. By contrast, we propose contex-tual modeling to augment feature representations, facilitating knowledge transfer of valuable high-level semantic informa-tion. III. M ETHODOLOGY

B. Overview of CIRKDV2 

A unified framework is shown in Figure 2, which illustrates the complete pipeline of CIRKDV2. Compared to the previous CIRKD, CIRKDV2 is an omni-dimensional distillation frame-work, including not only pixel-to-pixel distillation and pixel-to-region distillation, but also channel-to-channel distillation, channel feature distillation and contextual feature distillation. Semantic segmentation requires both spatially structured prediction and channel-wise semantic discrimination. Pixel-to-pixel distillation captures long-range spatial dependencies among pixels. Pixel-to-region distillation captures the relation between pixel embeddings and class-wise semantic proto-types. Channel-to-channel distillation captures the correlations among semantic response maps. These three dimensions are complementary: the first two focus on spatial/category-level structure, while the third focuses on semantic-channel interac-tion. Cross-image relational distillation transfers structural knowledge by aligning similarity distributions. However, re-lation losses mainly constrain relative similarities, not the absolute semantic content of each pixel embedding. Contextual feature distillation complements this by providing a direct teacher-guided semantic alignment after context aggregation. Therefore, it serves as a semantic anchor for the relational framework. 

C. Pixel-wise Cross-Image Relational Knowledge Distillation 

Motivation. Although the training objectives of Ltask and 

Lkd are widely used in semantic segmentation, they only deal with pixel-wise predictions independently but neglect seman-tic relations between pixels. Some segmentation KD meth-ods [17]–[19] attempt to capture spatial relational knowledge by modeling pixel affinity. Nevertheless, these KD methods only construct the relationships among pixels within a single image , regardless of the semantic dependencies among pixels across global images. This paper demonstrates that cross-image relational knowledge is also valuable for conducting teacher-student-based KD. Our CIRKD makes use of pixel embeddings beyond a single image. Inspired by the recent memory-based contrastive learning [24], [43], we may retrieve pixel embeddings of other images from the current mini-batch or an online memory bank . This paper considers both of two manners to model relationships among pixels, the details of which are shown as follows.

2) Memory-based Pixel-to-Pixel Distillation: Although mini-batch-based distillation could capture cross-image rela-tions to some extent, it is difficult to model dependencies among pixels from global images, since the batch size per GPU of segmentation task is often small, e.g. 1 or 2. To address this problem, we introduce an online pixel queue that can store massive pixel embeddings in the memory bank generated from the past mini-batches. It allows us to retrieve abundant embeddings efficiently. The usage of memory bank dates back to self-supervised learning [24]. This is because a large number of negative samples are pivotal for unsupervised contrastive learning, and the mini-batch size limits available contrastive samples. In the context of the dense segmentation task, each image would contain a vast number of pixel samples, and most pixels in the same object region are often homogeneous. Therefore, storing all pixel embeddings may learn redundant relational knowledge and slow down the distillation process. Moreover, saving several last batches to the queue may also damage the diversity of pixel embeddings. Thus we maintain a class-aware pixel queue Qp ∈ RC×Np×D , where Np is the number of pixel embeddings per class and D is the embedding size. E. Feature Distillation with Context Aggregation 

Cross-image relational modeling often captures struc-tured knowledge. Beyond relation distillation, feature dis-tillation [32], [47] has been demonstrated to reduce the performance gap between teacher and student. We further explore feature distillation of pixel embeddings to increase the similarity of pixel-wise representations between teacher and student. The traditional feature distillation methods often directly align pixel-wise embeddings from the original teacher and student feature maps. However, the pixels over the same spatial location from teacher and student may not have consis-tent semantics, since the teacher is often equipped with a larger kernel size than the student, which means that the teacher pixel extracts semantic information from a wider receptive field. To mitigate the receptive field gap between teacher and student, we utilize context aggregation over the student fea-ture map to augment student pixel-wise representations using wider contextual semantic information. Given the feature map 

F ∈ RA×D , where A is the number of pixels and D is the pixel embedding dimension, we linearly transform it to Q, K,and V as the concepts of query, key and value. We use the self-attention mechanism [48] to model context aggregation for pixel representations:

Benefiting from the contextual feature distillation, the student would learn better pixel representations with meaningful con-text semantics from the teacher, leading to improved semantic segmentation capability. The context aggregation module can indeed be viewed as an auxiliary distillation head during training. However, it is not used to enhance the inference-time capacity of the student. Instead, it provides a teacher-compatible feature transformation for the distillation objective. It is a training-only semantic adapter that improves the quality of feature distillation without introducing any inference overhead. This is consistent with many distillation methods that use auxiliary heads or projection layers during training and remove them during inference.

B. Ablation of context aggregation for feature distillation 

As shown in Table XII, we conduct an ablation study of various context aggregation methods for feature distillation. The feature distillation without contextual aggregation only achieves a 0.41% mIoU gain over baseline. This is because the traditional feature distillation ignores that the teacher pixel has a wider receptive field than the student pixel. Although contex-tual aggregation with equal weights improves the traditional feature distillation by 0.32%, it neglects the correlation among contextual pixels. The object contextual aggregation [57] ab-sorbs object region representations to augment student pixels and boosts the equal contextual aggregation by 0.17%. We think the reason why object region representations lead to a marginal improvement is that the object-level coarse-grained representations do not contribute to the optimization of pixel-wise feature distillation. Our method introduces the attention mechanism for weighted pixel-level contextual aggregation and achieves the best 1.61% improvement over baseline. The results demonstrate that our method makes the student pixels learn better semantic information from the teacher. This is because our method widens the student receptive field and leads the student to learn matched pixel semantic information from the teacher. 

C. Impact of the queue size 

We investigate the impact of memory sizes of the pixel queue, region queue, and channel queue. As shown in Ap-pendix Figure R.1, distillation performance increases as the sizes of the pixel queue, region queue, and channel queue grow. This is because a larger queue could provide more abundant and diverse embeddings for capturing long-range dependencies. The results also show that the distillation per-formance saturates at a certain memory capacity. In summary, the pixel, region, and channel queues occupy 368MB, 40MB, and 92MB memory costs, which only account for <1% of the whole memory. In our design, the region queue stores features generated 

by the frozen teacher network . Since the teacher is fixed, the stored region embeddings remain semantically invariant throughout training, therefore the “staleness” problem does not exist. In addition, we use a class-wise queue and class-balanced sampling. This prevents frequent categories from dominating the relation construction and ensures that rare classes can still be sampled once their region embeddings are stored. As shown in Figure 6 of our manuscript, the Cityscapes class-wise IoU analysis also shows gains than SOTA dis-tillation method LAD on rare classes like wall, fence, and truck by 2.08%, 5.18% and 5.81% mIoUs, respectively. This indicates that the class-wise region queue does not harm rare classes. Instead, it helps preserve teacher-provided prototypes and makes them available to provide stable information for later iterations. We investigate whether a large memory queue is more effective than a smaller but more frequently refreshed queue. In Table XIV and XV, the experiments are conducted on both CNN-based (DeepLabV3-ResNet-18) and Transformer-based (SegFormer-MiT-B0) architectures across Cityscapes and ADE20K. For DeepLabV3-ResNet-18, the large queue (20K, 16) achieves 75.26% and 38.77% mIoU on Cityscapes and ADE20K, respectively, outperforming the smaller queue (5K, 40) by 0.22% and 0.42% mIoU. Similarly, for SegFormer-MiT-B0, the large queue achieves 75.35% and 36.52% mIoU, exceeding the smaller queue by 0.17% and 0.25% mIoU. The intermediate configurations also exhibit a consistent trend of improved performance with larger queue capacities. These results suggest that preserving diverse historical embeddings 15 

is more beneficial than increasing the queue refresh rate in our cross-image relational distillation framework.

D. Impact of sampling methods 

As shown in Table XIII, we examine the impact of vari-ous sampling methods for memory-based distillation. “W/O sampling” means that the queue size is equal to the number of contrastive samples. “Hardest sampling” represents that we sample the hardest contrastive instances from the queues. Some previous studies [58], [59] found that the hardest nega-tives may lead to bad local minima. To alleviate this problem, we attempt a semi-hard sampling strategy. We first collect the top 20% hardest negatives from the queues and then randomly sample contrastive instances from them. We found that “W/O sampling” is harmful to the performance gain, since this way decreases the queue size and range for modeling dependencies. Other advanced sampling methods, like hardest and semi-hard sampling, also do not produce improvements but introduce extra computation complexity. Finally, we choose random sampling, a simple yet effective way. In dense semantic segmentation, the memory bank is used to approximate the teacher’s dataset-level relational distribution, not only to mine the hardest negatives. A large queue provides diverse cross-image semantic contexts, while random class-balanced sampling gives a stable estimate of this global distribution. By contrast, hardest mining can overemphasize outliers, boundary pixels, mislabeled regions, or visually similar classes that are not reliable negatives. This may explain why hardest and semi-hard sampling introduce extra computation but do not improve performance.

G. Sensitivity analyses of loss weights 

As shown in Figure 13, we analyze the sensitivity of the loss weights α, β, γ, and λ, where we set α ∈ [0 .1, 5] ,

β ∈ [0 .001 , 2] , γ ∈ [1 , 500] , and λ ∈ [1 , 100] . For relational 16 

distillation weights α and β, we find that performance in-creases from α = 0 .1 to α = 1 , and from β = 0 .001 to 

β = 0 .1, reaching the best results when α = 1 and β = 0 .1.When α > 1 and β > 0.1, the performance becomes subopti-mal. We think that smaller α and β reduce the contributions of relational distillation, while larger counterparts may lead to over-optimization and negative regularization. Moreover, we observe that γ ∈ [50 , 200] and λ ∈ [1 , 10] work well for feature distillation. Overall, loss weights are not sensitive within our recommended ranges. VI. C ONCLUSION 

This paper presents CIRKDV2, an improved cross-image relational KD method for efficient semantic segmentation. We construct pixel-to-pixel, pixel-to-region, and channel-to-channel relation modeling to transfer multi-dimensional struc-tured knowledge for learning long-range pixel and chan-nel dependencies. Moreover, we propose contextual feature distillation that first performs spatial context aggregation to augment the student pixels with richer contextual information and then aligns the augmented student pixels with the teacher pixels. Experiments on four challenging semantic segmen-tation datasets demonstrate that CIRKDV2 outperforms the state-of-the-art segmentation distillation methods. We hope our work can inspire future research to explore more advanced segmentation KD works towards global relational modeling and contextual feature distillation. ACKNOWLEDGMENT
