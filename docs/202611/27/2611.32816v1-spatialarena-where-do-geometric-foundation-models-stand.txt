Abstract
3D perception is shifting from task-specific pipelines to geometric foundation models: monocular depth
networks, feed-forward 3D/4D reconstruction models, and vision–language models that predict geometry.
However, their progress cannot be measured reliably. Published numbers are not comparable, because every
paper samples frames, sets resolutions, and selects test splits differently. They are not robust because the
alignment required for every up-to-scale prediction can outweigh the gap between models: fitting depth in
the wrong space nearly triples its error, and standard trajectory alignment understates the error of failing
methods by up to 2.6×. And they rarely cover long recordings, dynamic scenes, or aerial views. We present
SpatialArena, an open arena that scores geometric models with the same metrics and rules, with robust and
deterministic alignment. SpatialArena spans five tasks, from monocular depth to SLAM. It covers 23 public
datasets with 418,929 images across indoor, outdoor, aerial, object-centric, and dynamic scenes, together
with Spatial Arena, our own captures with accurate poses and depth. Evaluating 52 models, we find that
single-image depth is close to saturation on public benchmarks but not on large, uncontrolled scenes, and
that progress now comes from consistency across frames and views, where feed-forward 3D/4D models match
or surpass monocular specialists and overtake video depth models. The benchmark, toolbox, and leaderboard
will be publicly released.

1 Introduction
Perceiving the 3D world from images is one of the oldest goals of computer vision, and it is undergoing
the shift that natural language processing went through a few years ago. Depth, camera pose, and scene
geometry, traditionally estimated by separate hand-engineered pipelines [58, 71, 72], are now predicted
by large networks trained on massive data: monocular depth models generalize to unseen scenes without
further finetuning [6, 44, 93, 102], feed-forward 3D/4D models predict cameras, depth, and point maps
for thousands of frames in one pass [15, 55, 88, 91, 94, 95], and vision–language models begin to predict
metric geometry [12, 14, 107]. These geometric foundation models raise questions beyond any single task.
Will general models absorb the specialized ones, as large language models (LLMs) did in NLP? Where do

Answering them requires measurement. Shared benchmarks have shaped both vision and language: Ima-
geNet [23] made progress in recognition visible and comparable, and holistic evaluations and open arenas [19, 54]
track how LLMs evolve across many abilities at once. Geometric foundation models have no such common
ground, and three problems make their results hard to compare. First, every work evaluates with its own
tools. Frame sampling, input resolution, test splits, and evaluation code differ from paper to paper [44, 55, 91],
so a reported gap between two models may come from evaluation rather than from the models. Second,
metric computation is neither consistent nor robust. Most models predict geometry up to scale, so every
score passes through an alignment to the ground truth, and that alignment can matter more than the model.
Fitting a disparity model in depth space nearly triples its AbsRel, a least-squares fit raises the AbsRel of
strong multi-view models by more than 50% (Sec. D), and the closed-form fit on camera centers used for
trajectories [30, 84] understates the error of failing methods by up to 2.6× (Sec. D). Third, coverage is partial.
Most papers report results on a subset of public datasets, rarely on the regimes that matter in practice:
dynamic scenes, aerial views, weakly textured objects, or recordings of thousands of frames. Some test scenes
are even part of the evaluated models’ training data (Sec. A). As a result, it is unclear how far geometric
foundation models have actually come, and in which direction.
We argue that the field needs an arena: an open platform where geometric models are evaluated with the
same metrics, under the same rules. SpatialArena is built on three principles. Fairness. One evaluation suite
scores every model from its outputs using the same inputs, alignment, and metrics, and excludes the datasets
models were trained on from the ranking. Robust measurement. Depth is aligned by a robust fit in each
model’s native output space on pixels chosen by the ground truth alone, and trajectories by a pose-aware,
outlier-robust similarity estimator. Breadth. SpatialArena spans five tasks, from monocular and video depth

We evaluate 52 depth, feed-forward 3D/4D, long-sequence SLAM, and open and commercial vision–language

our large, uncontrolled captures the errors are three to four times higher; the remaining headroom lies in fine
accuracy and in such scenes. Second, general models are absorbing specialized ones: feed-forward 3D/4D
models match the monocular specialists on public data, surpass them once our captures are scored, and, on
video depth, halve the error of the best dedicated model. Third, vision–language models improve fastest on
single-image depth but remain far behind, lacking metric precision and consistency across frames. No model
leads on every dataset, so the field has not yet converged on one model of 3D structure. In summary, our
contributions are:
• SpatialArena, an open arena that evaluates geometric foundation models on five tasks, 23 public datasets,
and our captures under one protocol;
• robust, deterministic alignment for depth and trajectories, with a comparison of six depth alignments and
a controlled study showing that the standard trajectory alignment hides failures;
• an evaluation of 52 models across specialist, feed-forward, and vision–language families that traces how
geometric foundation models are evolving.
2 Related Work
Depth foundation models. Affine-invariant training on mixed data [68, 69] made monocular depth
zero-shot. Later work adds unlabeled and synthetic data [101, 102], diffusion priors [34, 44], metric depth,
cameras, or point maps [6, 37, 67, 92, 93], and arbitrary output resolution [106], mostly on self-supervised
backbones [61]; video models add temporal consistency [16, 38, 108]. Each reports results under its own
alignment and test split [27].
Feed-forward multi-view geometry. DUSt3R [94] and MASt3R [51] regress point maps from uncalibrated
image pairs, and successors scale to many views with spatial memory [11, 86, 91] or a single forward
pass [99]. VGGT [88] predicts cameras, depth, and point maps from hundreds of frames, and was refined for
permutation equivariance [95], depth–ray outputs [55], accuracy and metric scale [9, 87, 89], efficiency [18, 76],
robustness [31], and semantics [52, 110]; others accept cameras or depth as priors [40, 46, 66]. They now
drive SfM [25] and SLAM [56, 59], competing with classical pipelines [58, 63, 71, 72, 83], and reach thousands
of frames through chunk alignment [24, 96], test-time training [41], compact memories [15, 32, 109], or local
pose regression [97], each evaluated on its own sequences and trajectory alignment.
Geometry from general-purpose models. Vision–language models answer spatial questions [14, 100] or
predict metric depth [12, 82, 107], and video generators are repurposed as geometry estimators [90]. Probing
studies ask how much 3D structure such models encode [4, 7]. Commercial VLMs, such as Claude [2],
GPT [60], and Gemini [29], are available only through APIs and do not disclose their training data. Neither
open nor commercial models are routinely evaluated alongside dedicated 3D models.
Benchmarks. Most papers reuse the protocols of Marigold [44], π3 [95], and DA3-BENCH [55], with differing
sampling, resolution, and alignment, and trajectories rely on an Umeyama fit on camera centers [30, 84].
RobustMVD [74] evaluates multi-view depth, GeoBench [27] retrains monocular models under controlled
settings, and E3D-Bench [20] evaluates 16 geometric foundation models. Concurrent SpatialBench [65]
evaluates 41 model variants on 19 datasets, and UAVFF3D [103] targets UAV imagery.

3 Method
SpatialArena evaluates spatial foundation models on five geometric tasks. We first define the tasks and input
tracks (Sec. 3.1), then introduce the Spatial Arena dataset (Sec. 3.2) and the depth and trajectory alignment
shared by all tasks (Sec. 3.3). We then summarize the datasets and metrics of each task (Sec. 3.4) and the
evaluated models (Sec. 3.5). The data leakage audit is described in Sec. A.

3.2 Spatial Arena Dataset
Data capture. Public benchmarks rarely provide metric ground truth for large, uncontrolled scenes captured
by handheld devices. We therefore collect Spatial Arena with a handheld scanner that records LiDAR point
clouds and three synchronized cameras; the front camera faces the direction of walking, and the two side
cameras look up and sideways. The benchmark uses 30 scenes, 13 indoor and 17 outdoor: museum halls,
station concourses, a library, an office floor, and a stairwell; streets, alleys, gardens, markets, plazas, and
monuments (Fig. 3). Walks range from 18 m to over 1 km, and LiDAR depth from below 1 m to over 100 m.
In total, Spatial Arena contributes 32,655 unique images, selected per task as described in Sec. A.
Processing. The final LiDAR points and camera poses are obtained by LiDAR SLAM and post-processed
by iterative global bundle adjustment [71]. Projecting the LiDAR map into every camera with a z-buffer
gives sparse metric depth, and SAM 3.1 [13] masks people, vehicles, and sky, which are excluded from the
valid pixels. To densify the sparse depth, we fit 3D Gaussian Splats (3DGS) [47] to each scene, supervised
by LiDAR depth and regularized by depth–normal and multi-view geometric consistency, and render dense
depths for images as pseudo-labels; the projected LiDAR depth remains the sparse metric reference. We
validate every rendered depth map against the sparse LiDAR depth: over all MonoDepth and VideoDepth
frames, 98.3% and 98.2% of the LiDAR pixels agree within ±25% (AbsRel 0.036 and 0.030). Since LiDAR has
no returns where the rendering fails most often, e.g., near-camera floaters, reflections, and weakly textured
surfaces, MonoDepth also selects its images automatically: it keeps only images whose render PSNR exceeds
the scene median, so ground truth comes from well-reconstructed views, and samples 10% of each scene’s

3.3 Geometry Alignment
Most methods predict geometry only up to an unknown scale, and depth also up to an unknown shift, so
every metric is computed after aligning the prediction to the ground truth: one depth alignment for both
depth tasks and one trajectory alignment for all pose-based metrics.
3.3.1 Depth Alignment
Depth alignment estimates an unknown scale and shift between prediction and ground-truth depth. Models
predict in different spaces, i.e., affine-invariant disparity [34, 69], affine-invariant depth [44], or depth up to
scale [55, 88], and existing protocols align them differently: by least squares in depth [44] or by ℓ1 with a fixed
number of gradient steps [91, 95], some only on pixels where the prediction is valid. These choices are not
neutral: on identical predictions (Sec. D), least squares in depth raises the AbsRel of VGGT-Ωand DA3-G by
55–59% over a robust fit, and a depth-space fit nearly triples the AbsRel of Lotus-D. We therefore align every
model with the same robust, deterministic estimator on pixels chosen by the ground truth alone, and fit it in
the space in which the model predicts: in depth for depth outputs and in inverse depth for disparity outputs.
Ground-truth-only pixel domain. For frame i, Ωi contains the pixels whose ground-truth depth is finite,
inside the dataset’s valid range, and inside its mask. The prediction plays no role in this choice. A non-finite
prediction on Ωi fails the case instead of silently shrinking the set, so every method is fitted and scored on
the same pixels.

truth in the same space: ˆx = ˆD and x = D for models that predict depth, and ˆx = ˆq and x = 1/D for

e.g., disparity for MiDaS-style models [69, 101] and depth for Marigold [44], so an affine map matches its
ambiguity only in that space. We estimate the scale a and shift b that map the prediction onto the ground

Reconstruction and poses. MVRecon turns depths and poses into a reconstruction exactly as a downstream
user would: the poses are aligned with the Sim(3) estimator of Sec. 3.3.2, the recovered scale rescales the
depths, and all views are fused into a TSDF volume [21] (on Spatial Arena, back-projected directly into
one point cloud), which is compared with a laser scan, a mesh, or our LiDAR map. SfM tests camera
recovery from unordered images, including Spatial Arena scenes that mix three rig cameras looking in different
directions. SLAM instead evaluates tracking over continuous video: each case is one complete recording, fed
in a single pass at a fixed stride. Because sequence length dominates both difficulty and memory, we split
SLAM into short, long, and large-range tracks (up to 364, 1,211, and 18,846 input frames), and within each
track and dataset, we rank methods by the mean of their ATE and drift ranks.
3.5 Evaluated Models
We evaluate 52 models in five families (Tab. 2). Feed-forward 3D/4D models predict cameras, depth, and
point maps; they enter the depth, MVRecon, and SfM tasks, and most of them also SLAM; four of them also
accept geometric priors and run the rgbc or rgbd tracks. Monocular depth models, both affine-invariant and
metric, enter MonoDepth. Video generative models are built on video diffusion or generation backbones and
predict video depth, camera poses, or both. Long-sequence and SLAM systems chunk, stream, or compress
input to process thousands of frames and enter SLAM only. Finally, vision–language models are queried
for depth, which places them on the same scale as dedicated geometry models; besides open models, we
query three commercial models (Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6 Astra, and Gemini 4 Argon) through APIs on
MonoDepth. Variants of models, such as the depth and pose variants of GenCeption, count as separate
models. We list the input resolution and preprocessing of every model in Sec. D.3.1.

Progress over time. Do newer models perform better? Figure 4 and the VideoDepth plot in Fig. 9 answer
this for the two depth tasks, and the answer differs between model families. On MonoDepth, the depth
specialists have stalled: their fitted trend falls by 2.8 points per year, and the best of them has improved its
AbsRel only from 12.3% (MoGe, 2024) to 11.6% (MoGe-3, 2026). The 3D/4D models rise by 8.4 points per
year, and since mid-2025 the best of them has been more accurate than every depth specialist evaluated on all
datasets (π3-X, 11.4%; VGGT-Ω, 10.8%). Their advantage comes from Spatial Arena, the only MonoDepth
dataset on which the 3D/4D models are clearly more accurate than the specialists (Sec. 5.1); on the public
datasets alone, the two families rise by only 1.9–2.8 points per year and remain on par (Sec. B.3). On
VideoDepth, which requires depth that is consistent across frames, the 3D/4D models improve by 28.4 points
per year; their best AbsRel fell from 45% (DUSt3R) to 6.5% (VGGT-Ω) in two and a half years and has been
below that of the best video depth model (GenCeption-Depth, 14.0%) since mid-2025, while depth models
improve by 8.7 points per year. VLMs improve fastest on MonoDepth (31.5 points per year) from a low base,
but on VideoDepth more slowly than the 3D/4D models (14.3), and they still trail the other two families
on both tasks. Progress comes mainly from multi-view consistency rather than from single-image accuracy;

5 Discussion
The central finding of our evaluation is that models which are accurate on the public benchmarks fail in
systematic ways on large, uncontrolled scenes. In this section, we analyze why, along the three axes of Sec. 4:
generality (Sec. 5.1), robustness (Sec. 5.2), and accuracy (Sec. 5.3). We then summarize the strengths and
weaknesses of each model family (Sec. 5.4) and their implications for future work (Sec. 5.5). Throughout, ρ
denotes Spearman’s rank correlation, and “public” denotes a model’s mean over the ranked public datasets of
a task, which exclude ScanNet++ and Spring (Sec. A).
5.1 Generality
Across tasks. Single-image accuracy is a poor predictor of multi-view performance. The task scores are
strongly correlated among VideoDepth and the multi-view tasks (ρ = 0.88 between VideoDepth and MVRecon,
0.77 between VideoDepth and SLAM, and 0.74 between MVRecon and SLAM), but only weakly between
MonoDepth and the pose-dependent tasks (0.52 with SfM, 0.47 with SLAM); DA3-G, for instance, leads
MVRecon but ranks eighth in MonoDepth. We attribute this to the different cues that the tasks reward. A
single image can only be resolved through learned priors about scene layout, whereas consistent depth and
poses across views require correspondence, which the multi-view models learn and the monocular ones do not.

Why models fail on Spatial Arena: VideoDepth. A video provides temporal context and, to the
multi-view models, parallax, which could in principle resolve both failures. We test this on the 15 Spatial

the nine side-camera clips than on the six front-camera clips, by 1.1–2.7× (median 1.7×). The gap is smallest
for DVD (1.1×) and SenseNova-Vision (1.3×), but only because they are inaccurate already on the front clips

scene content adds to this gap, but its size and consistency across models agree with the MonoDepth analysis.

−0.45 against −0.59), which indicates that parallax helps, but not enough to recover distant structure.
 Key finding
Temporal context does not remove either failure. Video models inherit the gravity prior and the depth
compression of single-image models; parallax from multi-view models reduces the compression but does not
remove it, and diffusion-based video depth flattens the whole depth range.
Why models fail on Spatial Arena: SfM. Each Spatial Arena SfM scene mixes the upright front camera
with the two rolled side cameras, whose views point in different directions and overlap little with each other.
We therefore split the image pairs of the seven scenes by camera and compute the AUC@5◦separately for
each kind of pair (eight models, Fig. 8(c)). For every model, pairs of two front images are the most accurate
and pairs from the two different side cameras the least accurate, by 1.6–7.4× (VGGT-Ω: 0.250 against 0.124;
MapAnything: 0.071 against 0.010). Pairs from the same side camera, which share the roll but view similar
content, lie in between. Two factors therefore compound: the roll of the side cameras lowers accuracy even
when the views overlap, and the small overlap between differently oriented cameras lowers it further. A third
factor limits all pairs alike. The models process the 1600×1296 images at a width of 504–518 pixels (Tab. 29),
so that one 14-pixel patch spans about 2.5◦of the 90◦field of view, half of the 5◦threshold. The coarse loop
is therefore recovered (median AUC@30◦0.404), but fine accuracy is not (median AUC@5◦0.043). Finally,
because the models regress poses directly from image tokens, without the geometric verification by which
COLMAP rejects matches between similar-looking but distinct places, repeated structure produces spurious
jumps (Fig. 25).
 Key finding
Feed-forward pose estimation is reliable only for upright views that overlap strongly. Rolled views, small
overlap between differently oriented cameras, a working resolution of about 2.5◦per token, and the absence
of geometric verification together explain the scattered cameras and spurious jumps on Spatial Arena.

at most 18.2% on 7-Scenes drift by 42–221% on Spatial Arena, although the sequences have a similar number
of input frames (325 on median). The difference lies in the camera motion: the short Spatial Arena sequences
cover 179 m, so the camera moves 0.55 m between input frames, against 0.10 m on 7-Scenes and 0.05 m on
TUM RGB-D. Over all short-range sequences of all datasets, the drift of every model except Pi-Long and
ABot-Recon increases with this motion (Fig. 8(d); median ρ = 0.48 over the feed-forward models). Below

 Key finding
Drift grows with the camera motion between frames and, once the walk leaves the local context of the
model, with the length of the walk; the number of frames alone does not matter. Feed-forward models
track well only on short, slow, indoor paths. Only long-sequence systems that bound the error per chunk,
most consistently Pi-Long, keep the drift low on long outdoor walks, which Spatial Arena provides on every
range.
Why models fail on Spatial Arena: MVRecon. MVRecon combines the two previous mechanisms.
It aligns the predicted cameras to the ground truth, recovers the scale from the camera centers, and fuses
the depth maps with it. Depth that is compressed at long range then places distant surfaces too close to
the cameras, and pose errors among the rolled side views misplace whole depth maps; both move points
beyond the 25 cm threshold. This is consistent with the reconstructions of Figs. 19 and 20, whose shapes are
recognizable but whose scale and placement are wrong. We did not measure the error of the fused points per
distance, so this explanation rests on the depth and pose analyses above rather than on a direct measurement.
 Key finding across tasks
The failures on Spatial Arena trace to three properties that the public benchmarks lack: rolled views,
which break the gravity prior of depth and pose models; a wide depth range within one image, which every
model compresses at long range; and large camera motion between frames over long walks, along which
feed-forward models accumulate drift. None of these properties depends on the scale of a scene alone.

rankings transfer only partly. The rank correlation between public and Spatial Arena results is high for depth
(ρ = 0.73–0.91) and SfM (0.87–0.88), low for MVRecon (0.40), and absent for short-range SLAM (0.07), and
the leader changes in every task except VideoDepth. In MonoDepth, the three most accurate public models
are monocular specialists (MoGe-3, MoGe-2, MoGe), whereas on Spatial Arena the three most accurate are

5.2 Robustness
Failure cases on Spatial Arena: depth. The qualitative results show the failures analyzed in Sec. 5.1.
On the Spatial Arena frame of Fig. 11, VGGT-Ωhas an AbsRel of 13.2% and VGGT of 35.6%, although the
two models differ by only 1.4 points on the public datasets. In VideoDepth (Fig. 17), ChronoDepth misses
the far opening of a corridor (0.171, against 0.044 for Déjà View and π3-X), an instance of the compressed far
range. On a close-up of a nearly planar museum surface, every model fails to some degree (0.117–0.375), and
VGGT-Ωand DA3-NGL, first and third on the public VideoDepth datasets, are among the least accurate.
Such a view contains no horizon, ground plane, or familiar object that would anchor a learned layout prior.
The VLMs fail even on the coarse layout: in the library corridor (Fig. 14), Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra
return nearly planar depth (0.399 and 0.392).
Failure cases on Spatial Arena: reconstruction and poses. In MVRecon, no model exceeds an F-score
of 0.35 on the scenes of Fig. 19, and on the roof (Fig. 20), most models recover the shape but at the wrong
scale and position. In SfM, all models recover the overall loop around the Dugald Stewart Monument (Fig. 24),
but their cameras scatter around it (best AUC@5◦0.230, Déjà View; MapAnything 0.005). Around the St
Giles’ statue (Fig. 25), whose sides look alike, DA3-NGL and VGGT add spurious jumps across the loop.
These are the effects of camera roll, small overlap, low working resolution, and missing geometric verification
analyzed in Sec. 5.1.
Failure cases on Spatial Arena: long recordings. The 14 feed-forward models that drift by at most
18.2% on the short 7-Scenes sequences drift by 42–221% on those of Spatial Arena. As analyzed in Sec. 5.1,
the cause is the camera motion between frames. On the large range (Fig. 21b), only Pi-Long keeps the drift
below 50% (27.0%, ATE 13.1 m); the other systems drift by 51–155% or collapse onto a small region, and
VGGT-SLAM returns poses for only 10% of the frames. Pi-Long chunks the input of a strong feed-forward
model and aligns overlapping chunks, which bounds both memory and the error per chunk. Unlike the other
tasks, the large range of Spatial Arena is about as difficult as the public long recordings (median drift 80.1%
against 83.4%); for SLAM, Spatial Arena adds most on the short and long ranges.
Input length and memory. On the public datasets, most failures stem from input length rather than from
prediction errors. Global attention over all frames requires memory that grows quadratically with the number
of tokens, and pairwise models must process a number of pairs that grows quadratically with the number of
frames. Accordingly, DUSt3R and Pow3R exhaust memory on all but two short-range 7-Scenes sequences and
on every other short- and long-range SLAM sequence, ZipMap on every large-range Oxford Spires and VBR
sequence, and Scal3R on six of the seven VBR sequences (Tab. 15). GenCeption (pose) accepts at most 81

5.3 Accuracy
Coarse versus fine accuracy. On the public datasets, coarse accuracy is close to saturation, but fine
accuracy is not. The best δ1.25 is 98.2–99.5% on the Marigold suite, HAMMER, iBims-1, and AerialMetric,
and the best SfM AUC@30◦is 0.89–0.98 on DTU, ETH3D, HiRoom, IMC, and Tanks and Temples, whereas
the best δ1.03 is only 70.7–82.1% and the median AUC@5◦is 0.36–0.74. Fine thresholds also separate the
models better: on HAMMER and iBims-1, the median model is within 2.1 points of the best δ1.25 but 14–23
points behind the best δ1.03. We see two limits on fine accuracy. The models predict at a fraction of the
input resolution, and their training objectives are dominated by the coarse structure of a scene, which a
δ1.25-level prediction already captures. On Spatial Arena, fine accuracy is the first to break, with a best δ1.03
of 35.9% (Tab. 5).
Drift and global consistency. Errors grow rapidly with trajectory length: the best short-range ATE is
1.0 cm on TUM and 4.0 cm on 7-Scenes, whereas the best large-range drift is 9.8–27% of the path length
(Tab. 15). Low drift does not imply global consistency, nor does a low ATE imply small local errors. ABot-
Recon has the lowest drift on KITTI, VBR, and the short Spatial Arena range, but on the large range about
three times the ATE of Pi-Long on Oxford Spires and Spatial Arena (16.0 and 36.8 m against 5.5 and 13.1 m),
although it has the lowest ATE on VBR (16.5 m), while LingBot-Map shows the opposite pattern. Locally
accurate tracking and globally consistent maps are thus different capabilities, and only Pi-Long ranks among
the three best systems in both drift and ATE on every large-range dataset.
5.4 Strengths and weaknesses of the evaluated models
Table 7 summarizes the strengths and weaknesses of each model family. We draw five conclusions from it.
(1) Multi-view 3D/4D models are the strongest general-purpose geometric models today, but they are not
yet reliable. They enter all five tasks, lead VideoDepth, MVRecon, and SfM, and are the most accurate
single-image depth models on Spatial Arena, where VGGT-Ωis also the least sensitive to camera roll. Their
weaknesses are long inputs, on which window-wise alignment accumulates drift (42–221% even on short
Spatial Arena sequences), fine pose accuracy, which their low working resolution and the absence of geometric
verification limit, and priors, which they follow even when the priors conflict with the image.
(2) Monocular specialists remain the most accurate single-image models on upright views of controlled scenes,
but they depend on that setting. They have the lowest AbsRel on every public MonoDepth dataset, yet their
error rises by 2.7–4.2× on Spatial Arena and more than doubles under strong camera roll, which indicates a
learned gravity prior rather than an understanding of geometry that is independent of orientation.
(3) Dedicated video depth models have been overtaken. The best 3D/4D model is more accurate on every
VideoDepth dataset, and on Spatial Arena the video depth models have 3.4–4.6× the error of VGGT-Ω;
temporal smoothness without multi-view geometry does not resolve the depth of distant structure.
(4) Long recordings require dedicated long-sequence machinery, and its robustness is the bottleneck. Only the
long-sequence systems run the large range, and they also lead the short Spatial Arena range, on which the
feed-forward models drift. Chunking a strong feed-forward model with overlapping alignment, as Pi-Long
does, is currently the most reliable design; the other systems fail through memory exhaustion, incomplete
trajectories, or drift above 50%.
(5) VLMs recover the coarse metric layout but are not yet geometric models. They improve fastest on
MonoDepth and approach the median model on some public datasets, but on Spatial Arena their best AbsRel
is 28.2% and their δ1.03 is at most 13.9%. We query the commercial VLMs at only 16×16 points (Sec. D.3.2),
so their scores bound only their coarse accuracy.
Overall, no family combines breadth, fine accuracy, and robustness to viewpoint, depth range, and input
length. The public benchmarks share the upright viewpoints, narrow depth ranges, and short inputs of the

5.5 Implications for future work
These observations suggest six directions for the design and evaluation of geometric foundation models.
1. Remove the dependence on upright views. The error more than doubles under camera roll. Training with
in-plane rotation and with views that look up or down, or conditioning on the gravity direction where a
device provides it, should close much of this gap; evaluation should include such views.
2. Cover near and far structure in one image. All models compress depth beyond 20 m. Training data in
which near and far structure appear together, and losses that weight relative error uniformly over depth,
address the failure that a single scale and shift cannot correct.
3. Verify geometry and raise working resolution. Spurious jumps on repeated structure and the gap between
AUC@30◦and AUC@5◦call for geometric consistency checks inside or after the network and for pose
estimation at higher resolution.
4. Handle large motion, bound memory, and correct drift. Training sequences with large camera motion
between frames should accompany the small-motion video clips that dominate current training data.
20

Models should run within bounded memory, through streaming, chunking, or a compressed state, and
correct accumulated scale drift, e.g., through loop closure or global optimization.
5. Detect failures and handle priors. Incomplete trajectories, constant predictions, and far outliers produce
plausible-looking outputs; per-frame and per-point confidence is needed to expose them. Prior conditioning
should be trained on noisy, sparse, and inconsistently scaled inputs, so that a model given cameras or
depth is never less accurate than without them.
6. Evaluate fine accuracy on uncontrolled data. δ1.25 and AUC@30◦are saturated on most public datasets,
whereas δ1.03 and AUC@5◦on Spatial Arena still separate the models, under a common robust alignment
on audited test data.
6 Conclusion
We presented SpatialArena, an open arena that evaluates geometric foundation models on five tasks, 23
public datasets, and our Spatial Arena captures under one protocol, with robust and deterministic alignment
of depth and trajectories, and with leaked test data excluded from the ranking. Evaluating 52 models shows
that single-image depth is close to saturation on public benchmarks but not on large, uncontrolled scenes,
that progress now comes from consistency across frames and views, where feed-forward 3D/4D models match
or surpass the monocular specialists and overtake dedicated video depth models, and that vision–language
models improve fastest on single-image depth but still lack metric precision. No model leads on every dataset,
and long recordings remain out of reach for feed-forward models on their own.
Limitations. We measure the effect of camera roll by comparing images, not by rotating them, so content
that differs between front and side views, such as more sky and facades in the side views, contributes to the
gap; the same-scene comparison and the monotonic trend with roll make roll the most likely main factor, but
not the only one. The trends rest on one to three years of releases per model family and are indicative rather
than forecasts. Several comparisons involve few models (four accept auxiliary inputs, eight run the large
SLAM range), some models cover Spatial Arena only partially (marked ◦), and we evaluate the commercial
VLMs only on MonoDepth and through a 16×16 query grid. We will keep the leaderboard open to new
models and datasets.

Evaluation environment. We run all evaluations on a cluster of NVIDIA H200 GPUs (141 GB of memory

Spatial Arena pipeline. We refine the poses with five rounds of global bundle adjustment with track
completion, track merging, and outlier filtering [71]; all cameras of a frame share one rig pose, and each
camera keeps its fixed rig-to-camera transform. The final Sim(3) fit to the scanner poses corrects the scale by
0.99–1.16 and leaves a median camera difference of 2.5 cm. For LiDAR depth, we keep the nearest point per
pixel without a depth cutoff and mark pixels without a return as invalid. We prompt SAM 3.1 separately with
person, vehicle, license plate, and sky, and dilate every mask by 8 pixels; we never modify the source images.
We initialize the Gaussian Splatting model from the refined sparse points and train it for 30k iterations
with LiDAR depth supervision (log-ℓ1, weight 0.05), depth–normal consistency (weight 0.05), and multi-view
geometric consistency (weight 1). We mark rendered non-finite, non-positive, and sky pixels as invalid.

SpatialArena: Where Do Geometric Foundation Models Stand?
Spatial Arena selection. We rate every scene by the share of pixels masked as people or vehicles: static
(below 1%), light (1–3%), or crowded (above 3%, or people in over 60% of frames). For the depth tasks, we
use the 19 static or lightly populated scenes; for SfM and RGB-only SLAM, which we score on poses only,
we also admit crowded scenes. For MonoDepth, we draw images from all three cameras of the 19 depth
scenes: among the images whose render PSNR exceeds the scene median, we sample 10% of the scene’s
images, spread over time. For VideoDepth, we split seven walks into consecutive, non-overlapping clips with
lengths drawn uniformly from 100–500 frames, each on one randomly chosen camera (fixed seed), and keep
one 1,021-frame recording whole. For MVRecon, we use six compact scenes (extent at most 36 m) with every
image of the three cameras (5,295 in total). For SfM, we use every image of the three cameras of seven scenes
(321 to 1,947 images), from which we draw a fixed-seed subset of up to 300. For SLAM, we use one camera
per sequence and size each sequence close to the longest input of its range track. In the depth tasks, we
black out people and vehicles in the images and remove them from the ground truth; in SfM and SLAM,
we keep the images unmodified. We checked every frame of MonoDepth and VideoDepth by eye next to its
LiDAR and dense depth, and every MVRecon, SfM, and SLAM scene in a 3D viewer. This review removed
203 MonoDepth images and the ground truth of 26 VideoDepth frames. In total, Spatial Arena comprises
32,655 unique images (34,994 over all tasks, since we sample the MonoDepth images from the VideoDepth
and MVRecon scenes).
Data leakage audit. A benchmark rewards memorization when its test scenes are part of a model’s training
data. For every evaluated method, we searched its paper’s list of training data and, where released, its
training configurations and data loaders for each evaluated dataset (Tab. 9). We exclude a dataset from the
aggregate ranking when several evaluated methods train on its test scenes, or on scenes not shown to be
disjoint from them; we still keep it in every per-dataset table as a diagnostic. We record overlap in domain,
such as synthetic re-renders of the same streets, but do not exclude a dataset for it.
We exclude two public datasets.
Most methods disclose training on ScanNet++ [105] without a split
shown to be disjoint from our test scenes, and six methods train on the Spring [57] split that we evaluate.
TexturePoorSfM [35] leaks within itself: its 1,020 bags are re-samplings of 17 object captures, and bags of
one object share identical frames and poses, so a split by bag puts test frames into training. We therefore
split by object: 12 objects for training and five held-out objects that supply all 30 test bags; none of the
training images is identical to a test image. For the other public datasets, we found no evidence of training
overlap, although this does not prove that there is none. Since we have not released Spatial Arena before, no
evaluated method can have been trained on it.

B.3 Scores on the public datasets
All tables and trends of this paper score and average Spatial Arena like a public dataset. Existing benchmarks
contain public data only; for comparison with them, Tabs. 17 and 20 give the leaderboard and the MonoDepth
table with Spatial Arena left out of the scores and averages. Here we analyze how scoring Spatial Arena
changes the ranking.
The top of the ranking is stable. VGGT-Ω, π3-X, Déjà View, and IGGT4D keep the first four places, and
the first 20 places remain feed-forward 3D/4D models. Below them, the models that transfer well to Spatial
Arena move up: DA3-G and DA3-NGL pass DA3-L, IGGT rises from 16th to 15th (score 53.1 to 59.2), and
FastVGGT and MapAnything gain one place each, whereas AMB3R falls from 11th to 13th and CUT3R
from 15th to 16th. GeoNeXt, which has no Spatial Arena MonoDepth result, falls from 32nd to 50th, since
its missing cells count as 0.
MonoDepth changes most, because Spatial Arena is the only MonoDepth dataset on which the 3D/4D models
are clearly more accurate than the depth specialists (Sec. 5.1). VGGT-Ωreplaces MoGe-3 as the leader (score
96.7 against 92.1; mean AbsRel 10.76% against 11.57%), π3-X and AMB3R rise from 6th and 8th to 3rd and
4th, and MoGe-2 falls from 3rd to 7th. The mean AbsRel more than doubles for the leading models (MoGe-3:
4.94% to 11.57%), since Spatial Arena adds errors three to four times those of the public datasets. The trends
change accordingly (Fig. 4): with Spatial Arena, the MonoDepth score of the depth models decreases by 2.8
points per year instead of increasing by 1.9, whereas that of the 3D/4D models increases by 8.4 instead of 2.8
points per year (VLMs: 31.5 instead of 33.5). The newest specialists degrade most on Spatial Arena (MoGe-3
and InfiniDepth reach 4.2× their public AbsRel, Marigold 2.9×), so their gains on the public datasets do not
carry over to large, uncontrolled scenes.
B.4 Qualitative results
MonoDepth. On the Spring and Spatial Arena frames of Fig. 11, VGGT-Ω, first in MonoDepth, attains
the lowest error, and most models smooth away thin structures such as the trees in Spring. VGGT, ranked
15th, has the highest error on the Spatial Arena frame.
On iBims-1 (Fig. 12), the depth specialists are within 0.016–0.034 AbsRel of one another on a simple room,
whereas foliage and railings increase every error by a factor of 2.5–4.6; MoGe-3 is among the best on both
frames.
On the two Spatial Arena frames of Fig. 13, no model is consistently best: MoGe-2 leads in the garden (0.186)
and MoGe-3 on the canal (0.193), although MoGe-3 is least accurate in the garden (0.271). In the garden, the
largest errors lie on the thin pergola beams near the camera, which VGGT-Ωmerges with the background.
On a Spatial Arena frame (Fig. 14), Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra produce nearly planar depth, and
only Gemini 4 Argon recovers the layout of the corridor (AbsRel 0.153), still behind VGGT-Ω(0.112).

HAMMER and DepthLM on NYUv2.
VideoDepth. On the dynamic Bonn and DyCheck sequences (Fig. 16), the feed-forward 3D/4D models are
more accurate than the video depth models DVD, ChronoDepth, and DepthCrafter, consistent with Tab. 11a.
On Spatial Arena (Fig. 17), ChronoDepth misses the far opening of the library corridor, and a close-up of a
nearly planar museum surface degrades every model; VGGT-Ωand DA3-NGL, among the strongest models
on public data, are among the weakest on this frame.
MVRecon. On HiRoom (Fig. 18), DA3-NGL, VGGT-Ω, and VGGT reconstruct the room almost exactly
(F-score 0.875–0.943), whereas the errors of OmniVGGT and Pow3R extend over entire walls rather than

C Metric Definitions
Valid depth ranges. In MonoDepth and VideoDepth, we resize the prediction bilinearly to ground-truth
resolution, align it as in Sec. 3.3.1 on the set Ωof valid ground-truth pixels, and clip the aligned depth ˜D to the
valid range of each dataset. In MonoDepth, these ranges are [10−3, 10] m for NYUv2, [10−5, 80] m for KITTI,
(3, 150) m for Synth4K, (10−3, 400) m for AerialMetric, and [0.1, 100] m for Spatial Arena; HAMMER, iBims-1,

D.2.2 Choice of the estimator
On real predictions, the correct alignment is unknown, so we cannot validate an alignment estimator on
benchmark outputs. We therefore simulate predictions whose true alignment is known and compare our
estimator (Sec. 3.3.2) with five alternatives: evo, the Umeyama Sim(3) on all camera centers used by most
published SLAM tables [30, 84]; R-DAGSfM, LMedS RANSAC over the DAGSfM pose fit [17] with an

GTSAM followed by the robust refit; and R-GTSAM+refit (ours), R-GTSAM followed by the robust refit.

D.3 Model inputs and commercial VLM queries
D.3.1 Input preprocessing
Per-model preprocessing. We run each method at its own native input resolution and record this resolution
profile with the results. Our adapters record the exact resize, padding, and crop applied to each image and
invert them on the output: they return depth and confidence maps on the source pixel grid, mark border
pixels that the model never saw as invalid, and export intrinsics that describe the source image. Where a
model’s own loader would crop image content, our adapter pads instead, wherever the model allows it. We
list the result for every model in Tab. 29. We resize predictions that are not on the source grid bilinearly to
the ground truth before scoring. In MonoDepth, we give each image to multi-view models as a single-view
input and to pairwise models as the pair (image, image).
SLAM inputs. In SLAM, all methods see identical pixels. We write every sequence once at a width of 504
pixels (36 patches of 14 px), close to the dataset’s own aspect ratio: 504×378 for 7-Scenes, TUM RGB-D, and
Oxford Spires, 504×252 for VBR, 504×154 for KITTI Odometry, and 504×406 for Spatial Arena. We resize
each frame to fit and zero-pad it by at most a few pixels, never crop it, and map the ground-truth intrinsics
and the rgbd priors in the same way. The methods keep these frames unrescaled: models that require a
square canvas pad them, models with 16-pixel patches crop a border of at most 8 px, and for methods whose
code fixes another size, we set it to the input size (Tab. 29).

D.3.2 Querying commercial VLMs
Claude Opus 5.5 [2], GPT-6 Astra [60], and Gemini 4 Argon [29] return depth only as text, and every request
re-sends the whole image, so asking for one pixel per request would multiply the cost by the number of queried
points. We therefore ask for a whole grid of points in a single request per image, through the OpenRouter
API, on MonoDepth only.
Query. We resize each image to a long side of 1,024 pixels and send it twice as JPEG: the clean photo, and
a copy with a 16×16 grid of numbered red dots. The dots are spaced uniformly, with a margin of half a cell
from the image border, and each carries its number, from 1 to 256 in row-major order, on a white tag. We
ask the following user message (no system prompt):
The first image is a photo. The second image is the same photo with 256 red dots, each labelled with a number from 1 to
256; use the first image to see the scene the markers cover. For every dot, estimate the metric depth of the scene point
under the dot centre: its distance from the camera along the camera’s viewing (optical) axis, in meters. If a dot lies on
the sky or on anything effectively infinitely far away, answer "sky" instead of a number. Give a numeric estimate for
every other dot even when unsure.
Return the final answer as a single JSON object mapping each dot number (as a string) to its depth in meters (or "sky"),
wrapped in <answer> </answer> tags, e.g. <answer>{"1": 2.4, "2": "sky", "3": 3.1, ...}</answer>.
We query all three models with temperature 0, reasoning effort low (the reasoning text is not returned), and
at most 8,192 output tokens, and only through providers that do not store or train on the prompts.
Incomplete answers. Some answers stop after the first rows of dots. In this case, we send up to two
follow-up messages in the same conversation that ask for exactly the missing dots, without re-sending the
images:
Your answer did not include dots <missing ranges, e.g. 97-256>. Give the metric depth (or "sky") for exactly these dots,
estimated the same way and consistent with your previous estimates. Return one JSON object mapping each of these dot
numbers (as a string) to its depth, wrapped in <answer> </answer> tags.
The follow-ups ask for the rest of the answer rather than re-sampling it, and we stop as soon as one adds no
new dot.
From answers to a dense depth map. We clamp each answered depth to the depth range declared for
the scene, so that, e.g., a sky point answered at thousands of meters does not spread into its neighbors. We
fill grid cells answered “sky” or left unparsed from their answered 8-neighbors with the mean log depth, from
the nearest answers inward; a single scene-median value per gap would leave flat blocks. We then upsample
the 16×16 grid bilinearly to the resized image and invert the resize, so that we score the map at ground-truth
resolution with the per-image alignment of Sec. 3.3.1. An image without any parseable answer receives the
scene’s median depth, and we log it as an imputation; we score a constant answer with the offset-only fit of
Sec. D.1.1.
Protocol selection. We chose this protocol on a pilot of 14 images (two per MonoDepth dataset). Adding
the clean photo and the “sky” answer lowered the aligned AbsRel by about 9% compared to a single marked
image with an 8×8 grid, and the 16×16 grid lowered it a little further. Neither an RGB-guided joint bilateral
fill, a second request for extra dots around depth edges, nor a system prompt that frames the task for a depth
expert improved the aligned scores, so we use none of them.
Cost. We record the billed cost of every request with its answer. Running the full MonoDepth suite, including
the pilot and the follow-up requests, cost $510 for Claude Opus 5.5 (11,820 requests), $1,122 for GPT-6 Astra
(11,804), and $364 for Gemini 4 Argon (11,706), $1,996 in total (OpenRouter prices of November 2026), i.e.,
about 4.3, 9.5, and 3.1 US cents per request. We cache the answers by model, prompt, settings, and image,
so re-scoring a model costs nothing.
