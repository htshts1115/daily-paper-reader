Abstract 

We present the Segment Anything Model via Visual Demonstrations (SAYD), a foundation model for in-context segmentation. Given visual demos comprising prompt images and their segmentation masks, SAYD segments coherent regions (e.g., objects or their parts) in a query image by establishing visual correspondences between the prompts and the query. SAYD generalizes to previously-unseen segmentation tasks, including those requiring tacit visual knowledge or involving concepts that are difficult to articulate unambiguously in language. We train SAYD on 12 public datasets originally curated for panoptic segmentation using our proposed techniques, including a lightweight mask decoder for prompt-agnostic mask generation and a learning mechanism for recognizing novel query classes absent in the prompts. These innovations enhance both computational efficiency and out-of-domain generalization. Consequently, SAYD significantly outperforms existing methods of in-context segmentation and open-vocabulary segmentation across 24 out-of-domain benchmarks, spanning natural, biological, ecological, cosmological, medical, pathological, and satellite imagery. in-domain test examples

This capability emerges prior to the acquisition of high-level semantic knowledge, which is typically conveyed through language [52, 46]. Unlike the language-based prompts used by prevailing foundational segmentation models, such as SAM3 [11], visual demos can naturally encapsulate concepts rooted in tacit visual knowledge [21, 42, 76]. ICS is therefore particularly valuable for domains constrained by the knowledge acquisition bottleneck [36, 54], where relevant concepts may be difficult to articulate unambiguously. To solve ICS, we present a foundation model termed Segment Anything Model via Visual Demonstrations (SAYD) , as illustrated in Fig. 1. Early ICS methods do not directly solve ICS but train proxy models, such as pixel inpainting [5] and pixel coloring [96, 97], and subsequently post-process their outputs into segmentation masks. Most recent approaches exploit frozen pretrained image encoders [3, 63, 69, 62, 23], focusing instead on matching off-the-shelf features between the prompt and query images toward ICS. As the encoders are not pretrained specifically for ICS, these methods are limited in their matching ability required for effective ICS (Fig. 2). DINOv [55] is a universal visual in-context prompting model trained on 11M images, supporting not only ICS but also other prompts (e.g., strokes, boxes, and points). However, its focus on universal prompts hinders its optimization for broad ICS deployment. In sum, existing ICS methods struggle to generalize to diverse applications, particularly in scientific imaging and are thus ill-equipped to serve as foundation models (FMs) for these domains (Fig. 3). To address these limitations, we introduce the foundation model SAYD. First, we exploit diverse public datasets curated for various panoptic segmentation tasks, which provide class labels and masks for objects and/or parts. Using them not only enriches training but also supports a controllable reproducible evaluation on out-of-domain (OOD) generalization [87] (§3). Second, to enable effective training across heterogeneous datasets, we propose several simple and novel techniques (§4) regarding architecture design, training data sampling, and loss formulation. Architecturally, we design a mask decoder to generate class-agnostic masks directly from query images. This contrasts with existing methods [55, 11] that rely on prompt images, which overemphasize prompt-query semantic alignment at the expense of mask quality. Furthermore, our training loss explicitly accounts for novel query classes absent from the prompts. This design simplifies training data sampling, promotes diverse training combinations, and crucially, equips SAYD to recognize novel classes during inference. Finally, we evaluate SAYD on 24 OOD benchmarks featuring distribution shifts, spanning natural, biological, ecological, cosmological, medical, and pathological imagery. Extensive experiments and analyses confirm SAYD’s robust performance and its significantly superior performance over existing methods (§5). We summarize three key contributions as follows: 1. We introduce simple techniques, including architecture design, training batch sampling strategy, and loss formulation, that enable training over heterogeneous datasets and efficient inference. 2. With these techniques, we train the foundation model SAYD and release it alongside source code to foster future research. 3. # 2 Related work 

Vision Foundation Models (VFMs) are trained on massive images, optionally along with other data modalities. Some VFMs serve as backbones for encoding images. For example, MoCo models [40, 15] learn transferable features via contrastive learning over unlabeled images; MAE [39] and PixIO [108] train VFMs with an objective of randomly masked region reconstruction; DINO models [74, 75] are trained further with self-distillation [13]. Some others are trained over multimodal data. For example, CLIP [78], MetaCLIP [105, 19], SigLiP [111], and PE [7] train image and text encoders via multimodal contrastive learning on massive image-text pairs [85, 105, 92], producing visual-textual aligned features that allow open-vocabulary visual recognition. In this work, we focus on training a VFM for in-context segmentation, a more concrete application than learning image encoders and a mid-level vision problem compared with the high-level open-vocabulary recognition. 

VFMs for image segmentation can be broadly categorized by the type of prompt they accept. First, high-level text prompts (e.g., class names or descriptions [31]) enable open-vocabulary segmentation, allowing users to segment images based on texts [99, 66]. Representative models include X-Decoder [113], OpenSeeD [37], and SAM3 [11]. However, they struggle in highly-specialized domains (Fig. 3), where target concepts are often associated with domain-specific terminology sparsely represented in the models’ pretraining data or rely on tacit visual knowledge difficult to articulate unambiguously in language. Second, 

low-level visual prompts (e.g., points, boxes and masks) specify spatial locations, as in SAM [49]. While well suited to interactive segmentation, they require manual prompts for each query image and are thus limited in high-throughput applications. Third, mid-level visual prompts (i.e., images and their segmentation masks) specify the target visual concepts and instruct models to segment analogous regions in query images. This ICS paradigm is particularly valuable for applications that rely on tacit visual knowledge, such as clinical judgement and medical practice [76, 93] and astronomical discovery [6]. We train a VFM with ICS generalization across diverse scientific domains. 

In-Context Segmentation (ICS) adapts in-context learning from natural language processing [9] to computer vision [5] and has gained increasing attention. Early proxy-model approaches predict colored masks for query images [96, 97] and thus are not optimized for ICS. Other methods, such as Hummingbird [3] and INSID3 [23], use similarity metrics to associate off-the-shelf features between prompt and query images extracted by pretrained image encoders. However, they are limited as the encoders are not optimized for ICS (Fig. 2). Furthermore, DINOv [55] is a universal visual in-context prompting model trained on 11M images sourced from the COCO [60] and SA-1B [49] datasets. It supports not only ICS but also other prompt types (e.g., strokes, boxes, and points). However, its universality comes at the expense of limited ICS ability. Other 3Mask Decoder

# 3 Problem Definition and Evaluation Protocol 

Problem definition. A VFM designed for ICS aims to segment coherent regions in a query image, where the regions are specified by visual demos consisting of prompt images and their corresponding segmentation masks (Fig. 1). This VFM should generalize to diverse and previously unseen domains without task-specific adaptation. Therefore, the core problem is to develop such a VFM that enables robust, cross-domain generalizable, efficient learning-free ICS. 

Evaluation protocol. To comprehensively evaluate the foundational capability of the designed models, we evaluate them on test data sourced from diverse unseen domains, e.g., from scientific imagery. We further include a broad range of segmentation tasks, including instance, semantic, and panoptic segmentation, across multiple levels of granularity, from object- to part-level segmentation. 

# 4 Methodology 

Training a foundation model for ICS should be done over large-scale, diverse data with segmentation annotations spanning multiple levels of granularity. Such data can be easily sourced from diverse public datasets originally curated for various segmentation tasks. However, training over such heterogeneous datasets has been underexplored and requires effective techniques. We introduce such techniques regarding architecture design, training data sampling, and loss formulation. 

## 4.1 Model Architecture 

From first principles, we design the architecture of SAYD (Fig. 4) to achieve inference efficiency, efficacy to support multiple prompt images, and ability to produce high-quality masks. SAYD comprises two main components, an image encoder and a lightweight mask decoder. We describe them below, deferring implementation details to the Appendix §B and the supplemented code. 

An image encoder extracts feature maps for any input images. For a query image in resolution H × W ,it uses a convnet to spatially upsample the feature map and outputs E ∈ Rh×w×d. The encoder architecture 4follows Vision Transformer (ViT) [29] and is initialized from pretrained weights (Table 5). We further incorporate window attention to improve computational efficiency [57] and apply a linear layer to reduce the feature dimension to d. For visual demos supposedly defining K classes, the encoder extracts the prompt images’ feature maps, which are then spatially averaged for each of the K classes, within relevant regions according to the prompt masks, resulting in K prototypes P ∈ RK×d (with L2 normalization). The query’s feature map E and the prototypes P are used for mask prediction and classification by the following decoder. 

A lightweight mask decoder maps the query’s feature map E to pairs of segmentation masks and classes [18]. Concretely, with q learnable mask queries, it first maps E to q tokens, which are then transformed by two MLPs to two sets of L2-normalized tokens, denoted as Qmap ∈ Rq×d and Qcls ∈ Rq×d. They are used for mask prediction and classification, respectively: • Mask prediction. With the query feature map E and tokens Qmap , it computes q masks M = E ×3 QTmap ∈

Rh×w×q , which are then converted into binary masks. These masks are class-agnostic [50] that depend solely on the query (Fig. 4a). In contrast, existing methods [55, 11] additionally require prompt image features (Fig. 4b) and generate class-aware masks. Consequently, they overemphasize prompt-query semantic alignment and degrade mask quality. Our design not only improves inference efficiency even with multiple prompts, but also facilitates the training of high-quality mask generation, thereby improving ICS (Tables 3&4). • Mask classification. With q tokens representing q segment masks, we use prototypes P to compute per-token logits S = Qcls PT ∈ Rq×K . Straightforwardly, one could apply argmax to obtain the classification results for these tokens. However, the query image likely contains novel classes absent from the prompt images (and therefore not represented by the prototypes). We address this by training SAYD to identify the novel classes, detailed in the following two subsections. 

## 4.2 Training Data Sampling 

We repurpose 12 publicly-available datasets (listed in Appendix Table 9), originally curated for panoptic segmentation on diverse images, for training SAYD. As different datasets name visual concepts differently, we adopt a within-dataset training data sampling strategy. 

Prompt-query sampling . We sample training data within each dataset. Specifically, for a given dataset, we randomly sample one image as the query and N additional images, along with their ground-truth masks, as visual demos. The ground-truth masks of both query and prompt images help identify regions that share class labels to compute training loss (detailed in §4.3). Despite the simplicity of this sampling strategy, a recurring issue arise: the query image contains novel classes absent from the prompt images. We use the term ignota to denote these novel classes present in the query but missing from the visual demos. 

The ignota . Existing ICS methods that train models circumvent ignota in various ways. Some train regression models to color and group pixels for segmentation [97, 96]. This sidesteps the problem entirely but the resulting models are not optimized for ICS. [69] design a model to handle only one class per run. This simplifies the sampling of query and prompt images (requiring only one shared class) but renders training inefficiency as each iteration ignores all other labeled classes. [62] restrict their sampling to cases where all query classes are present in the prompts [90]. This severely limits the possible prompt-query combinations. [55] use the current training batch as prompts and treat each individual example within the batch as a query. While this eliminates the ignota issue during training, it simultaneously introduces shortcut learning and prevents learning of a model capable of identifying ignota at test time. In the real world, numerous applications solicit the ability to identify novel classes [84, 51, 1, 14]. Hence, we explicitly incorporate ignota 

into the training loss computation (detailed next). Empirically, doing so not only facilitates training but also yields superior ICS performance (Table 7). 

## 4.3 Loss Function 

By design, SAYD produces mask queries, from which it generates class-agnostic masks and class labels (Fig. 4). We train SAYD using task-specific losses. For mask prediction, we employ the widely used dice loss 

Ldice and focal loss Lfocal [18]. Our novelty of loss formulation lies in mask classification, detailed below. 5Loss for mask classification. Recall that SAYD computes q per-token logits S ∈ Rq×K (§4.1), to which a cross-entropy (CE) loss could be straightforwardly applied. However, the training data contain ignota .When using a CE loss, there are two ways to handle ignota : (1) ignoring them during loss computation, or (2) assigning them to an additional placeholder class. The former forgoes the opportunity of exploiting ignota 

to train models capable of identifying novel classes, while the latter inevitably collapses diverse ignota into a single class and thus complicates training. Hence, we instead employ the binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss 

Lbce . Under the BCE formulation, the features of ignota , as well as background regions (cf. the black regions in the fourth column of Fig. 1), serve as negative examples for all K prototypes. Furthermore, we learn a temperature τ and a bias β to scale and calibrate the logits, i.e., ˜S = S

Remarks. The adoption of BCE loss with learnable temperature and bias is a novel contribution to the ICS literature. As discussed in §4.2, prior methods circumvent ignota during training, thereby avoiding the need to compute a loss for them. That said, they assume no ignota , preventing training models to identify novel classes at test time. Instead, learning with ignota yields superior ICS models (Tables 7&8). Moreover, DINOv [55] employs a BCE focal loss without a learnable bias and eschews L2-normalization when computing similarities between prototypes and query features. Despite this alterative strategy for modulating feature similarities during loss computation, incorporating a learnable bias significantly improves model performance (Table 6). 

The final loss. Combining the aforementioned loss terms, we train SAYD using the final loss L =20 · L bce + 2 · L focal + Ldice . We set the weights of loss terms by balancing the magnitude of their produced gradient. We provide details in Appendix §B. 

## 4.4 Inference 

SAYD segments a query image conditioned on visual demos, which define target classes. It outputs class-agnostic binary masks, each capturing a potential object instance. By classifying and merging the masks according to the target classes, SAYD achieves semantic and panoptic segmentation. Finally, for each mask, if its maximum calibrated logit (in the corresponding row of ˜S) falls below zero, the region of this mask is classified as ignota ; otherwise, it is assigned to the argmax class. 

# 5 Experiments 

We validate SAYD by comparing it against recent and representative VFMs across 24 benchmarks, supported by comprehensive analyses and ablation studies. We begin by outlining the experimental setup and key implementation details. 

## 5.1 Setup and Implementation 

Training data. We combine 12 public datasets designed for panoptic segmentation, including COCO [60], ADE20K [112], and Cityscapes [22]. Together, they provide 0.91M images and 11.46M segmentation masks to train SAYD. See the “in-domain test examples” in Fig. 1 for some images from these datasets, and Appendix §A for more details. 

Benchmark datasets. We use 24 public datasets span diverse domains, including gaming scenes [82], art paintings [20], object parts [34, 71, 47], animal/plant species [94, 100, 41], remote sensing [95], medical imaging [88], manufacturing materials [2], astronomy [102], and cell biology [30]. These datasets are clearly different from the aforementioned 12 training datasets, hence serving as OOD benchmarks. Refer to the “out-of-domain test examples” in Fig. 1 for some test images and Appendix §A for more details. For each benchmark, we randomly sample ∼10 images from its official training set as visual demos, ensuring that they collectively cover all semantic classes of this benchmark, and use the official validation/test images as queries. All queries within a benchmark share the same visual demos. For each method on each benchmark, we repeat the evaluation with three sets of independently sampled visual demos and report the average metrics. We 6evaluate semantic, instance, and panoptic segmentation using mean Intersection-over-Union (mIoU), Average Precision (AP), and Panoptic Quality (PQ), respectively. 

Compared methods. We compare SAYD with methods from two prevailing paradigms: text-based and vision-based prompting. We provide their detailed specifications in Appendix Table 10 and briefly describe them below: • Text-based prompting . We compare leading open-vocabulary segmentation models, including X-Decoder (X-Dec) [113], OpenSeeD (O-SeeD) [37], APE [86], Grounded SAM (G-SAM)[81], OpenWorldSAM (O-SAM) [104], RADSeg+SAM (R-SAM) [73], and SAM3 [11]. They use textual descriptions of target concepts (e.g., semantic class names) as prompts. We also evaluate the recently released open-source Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) WeVisOne-VL-4B model [58]. Following its official pipeline, we report performance under the following two types of text prompts: (1) WeV-cls , standing for only using class names (provided by official benchmarks) as shown in Fig. 3 -left, and (2) WeV-ins , which combines both the class names and corresponding one-sentence descriptions. Each description is obtained by prompting the AI agent Qwen3.8 [77] to describe a target class name. For example, for person , the description is “ ahuman being, including any man, woman or child in the scene”. 

• Vision-based prompting . We compare against Painter [96], SegGPT [97], UniverSeg [10], Matcher [63], SINE [62], SegIC [69], DINOv [55], INSID3 [23], SAM2 [80], and SAM3-V which is a mode of SAM3 that supports images as prompts. To the best of our knowledge, INSID3 is the most recent ICS method, which exploits a frozen DINOv3 [75] as the image encoder, while DINOv represents the most recent approach to training an ICS model on large-scale data. Since Matcher, SegIC, and INSID3 can only generate a binary mask for one class per inference, we apply it sequentially to each class and combine the resulting binary masks to obtain multi-class semantic segmentation. Moreover, since the weights of SegGPT are not publicly available, we retrain it using the same data as our SAYD. Notably, while supporting visuals in prompts, SAM3-V acutally requires low-level prompts such as boxes on the query image itself. To enable SAM3-V to operate as an ICS model, we concatenate the prompt images with the query into a composite image, where the regions consisting of prompt images contain such boxes. 

Implementation. For benchmarking, we initialize the image encoder with pretrained DINOv3 ViT-L [75, 29]; we validate SAYD with other pretrained encoders in Table 5. The mask decoder has 4 Transformer blocks and processes q = 200 256d mask queries. We use random horizontal flipping and random scale jittering in the range [0.5, 2.0] for data augmentation. We train SAYD for 550k iterations using the AdamW optimizer [65] with step-wise learning rate decay. The learning rate is linearly warmed up [35] to 3e-4 in the first 10k iterations and reduced by 10 × at iteration 500k. For the image encoder, we use a layer-wise learning-rate decay [4] of 0.75 and a drop-path rate of 0.3. We apply deep supervision [18] with training losses in all decoder blocks. Each training batch contains 128 samples: each sample consists of N = 5 prompt images and 1 query image, all at 512 ×512 pixel resolution.

## 5.3 Analyses and Ablation Studies 

We analyze SAYD w.r.t inference efficiency and ablate its key components. For all analyses in this section, we train SAYD for 100k iterations and use DRAM [20], TigD [71], Ant [47], LDA [95], and Gala [102] benchmarks to report results. 

Inference efficiency. Table 3 compares the computational costs of our SAYD and representative methods. Recall that SAYD adopts a lightweight mask decoder that takes as input only the query’s features without conditioning on prompts (Fig. 4a). This supports pre-computing prototypes defined by visual demos and consequently helps SAYD maintain a constant runtime regardless of the number of classes. In contrast, existing decoders either iteratively process each prompt atop the query’s features (e.g., SAM3) or require a joint query-prompt forward pass for each individual class (e.g., DINOv and INSID3; Fig. 4b). Consequently, their inference time scales linearly with the number of classes. Next, we compare architectural designs. 

Simplified mask decoder design. SAYD’s mask decoder takes only the query as input (Fig. 4a; Table 4 design a), unlike existing decoders that condition on prompt images, by requiring either feature maps of prompt images (Table 4 design b-1 ) or class prototypes computed on the prompt images (design 

b-2 , as in DINOv). Comparing four-block decoders of these designs atop the same image encoder confirms SAYD’s computational efficiency w.r.t parameter count and FLOPs, as well as its superior performance. We conjecture that conditioning on prompts biases the decoder toward coarse semantic regions at the expense of mask boundaries, thereby degrading segmentation performance. To validate this hypothesis, we conduct a breakdown analysis by comparing these designs w.r.t metrics Recognition Quality (RQ), Segmentation Quality (SQ), and Boundary SQ on the Hypersim panoptic segmentation benchmark [83]. RQ and SQ follow the panoptic segmentation metrics of [48], while Boundary SQ denotes SQ computed under the boundary-aware IoU criterion using the Boundary IoU API [16]. # 6 Conclusions 

We presented SAYD, a foundational in-context segmentation model that learns from heterogeneous prompt-query pairs. By developing specialized architectural and training strategies, SAYD bypasses the limitations of language-dependent models to capture tacit visual knowledge that is readily recognized by human experts but impossible to articulate in text. Extensive experiments across 24 benchmarks spanning natural, medical, ecological, and remote sensing domains demonstrate that SAYD significantly outperforms existing in-context, open-vocabulary, and SAM-based image segmentation methods. SAYD establishes a robust paradigm for in-context visual learning, enabling segmenting the nuanced, unspoken visual patterns critical to specialized expert domains.

# B More Details of Model Architecture and Training 

Image encoder. Following [57], we employ a pre-trained ViT-L [29, 75, 108, 7] as the image encoder, incorporating 16 ×16 windowed attention and 4 evenly spaced global attention blocks to process input images at 512 ×512 resolution. We discard the [class] token and adopt a 16 ×16 patch size, yielding 1,024 patch tokens per image. For PE spatial , the pretrained patch-embedding filters are interpolated from 14 ×14 ×3 to 16 ×16 ×3. We retain the positional-encoding scheme associated with each pretrained encoder, either windowed absolute positional embeddings [8] or RoPE [91, 43]. The positional encoding is applied independently to patch tokens within each image and shared across the query and prompts; no positional index is constructed across images. The encoder can therefore process a variable number of prompt images at inference time. 

Mask decoder. The mask decoder follows a query-based segmentation architecture [18, 17]. It receives single-scale, 16 × downsampled features and a set of learnable mask queries, and outputs paired masks and class scores. As illustrated in Fig. 4(a), each decoder block sequentially applies: (1) masked cross attention from mask queries (as queries) to the query image feature (taken from the final layer of the image encoder), (2) self-attention among the mask queries, and (3) a pointwise MLP updates each mask query. In step (1), we adopt the masked attention mechanism proposed in Mask2Former [17]. Prompt features are used only 

for prototype-based mask classification and do not enter the mask prediction path. The decoder contains 4 Transformer blocks, with embedding dimension 256, 8 attention heads, and 200 mask queries. To preserve spatial information during decoding, positional embeddings are re-injected at every decoder block: learnable embeddings are added to the mask queries [17], while fixed sinusoidal embeddings [39] are added to the query image features only when these features serve as keys (rather than values) in the masked cross-attention. 19 GTA DRAM iShape YouT NYU Ant CUB TigD Plant LDA LIP JSRT ODM Gala Cell PhenoBench iSAID WildDash2 ScanNet BSB TrashCan PASTIS Fashionpedia Hypersim Figure 7: Random examples from the 24 evaluation datasets , featuring images from diverse domains.

Hungarian algorithm [12]. To mitigate this, we initialize the bias term β to -4.59, reducing the initial sigmoid probability to 0.01. As for the temperature τ , following standard contrastive learning practice [103, 40], we initialize it to 0.07 and clip 1/τ to [10, 20] during training. 

Loss. We supervise mask prediction using established mask loss [18], which is the combination of focal loss Lfocal and dice loss Ldice . For mask classification, we adopt a sigmoid-based binary cross-entropy loss 

Lbce , augmented with focal modulation ( α = 0 .5, γ = 1 .0) [59] to more effectively handle the imbalance between positive and negative mask queries. 

Loss weight tuning. To determine the loss weights for the loss terms Ldice , Lfocal , and Lbce , we first conduct a pilot experiment to train SAYD for 100k iterations with all the weights as 1. Empirically, the mask prediction loss terms Ldice and Lfocal converge substantially faster than the classification loss Lbce . Then, we focus on the weight for Lbce . We consider the magnitude (i.e., the L2 norm) of gradient produced by the three terms: with weight 1 for Ldice and Lfocal , we increase the weight for Lbce until it produces gradient magnitude (after 100k training iterations) that matches the summed gradient magnitude of Ldice and Lfocal .Then, we adjust the weight for Lfocal to match the gradient magnitude of Ldice . Consequently, the final weights for the loss terms Ldice , Lfocal , and Lbce are 1, 2, and 20, respectively. In other word, the final loss 

L = 20 · L bce + 2 · L focal + Ldice . The loss weights are fixed throughout training. Lastly, we apply the same loss for deep supervision after each decoder block [18, 12] during training. 

Inference calibration and rejection. For each predicted mask, we first take the maximum value from the calibrated classification logit over the K prompt-defined categories. The mask is classified as ignota if the max value is less than 0; otherwise, it is assigned to the class corresponding to this max value.

# E Limitations and Future Work 

We discuss several limitations in this work that are worthy of address in future research. First, while our vision foundation model SAYD significantly outperforms existing methods on out-of-domain benchmarks, particularly scientific imagery, its metrics remain notably lower than on benchmarks featuring common concepts. Although training SAYD on more scientific data could close this gap, we intentionally maintain a clear domain gap between training and evaluation to rigorously study out-of-domain ICS generalization. We leave domain-specific adaptation for future work. Second, while our simplified mask decoder helps SAYD achieve superior performance over other methods and improve its computational efficiency, its class-agnostic mask generation nature may struggle to segment irregular target (e.g., the failure case on the MetalDAM ODM benchmark), whereas class-aware mask generation mechanism (e.g., by conditioning on prompts) may mitigate this limitation. Future work can investigate new decoder designs. Third, each visual concept (e.g., object class) is currently represented by a single prototype feature. Real-world applications may involve complex observations that require a “bag of features” to adequately represent a single concept; developing methodologies to address this is left for future research. Lastly, while SAYD demonstrates promising segmentation results on images from scientific domains its practical deployment in these domains to assist domain experts and accelerate scientific discovery represents a valuable next step. 24
