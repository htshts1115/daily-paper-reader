From Depth to Safe Depth: Uncertainty-Aware
Monocular Perception for Mobile Robot Navigation
Saeid Firouzi Daghigh and Saeed Ayat
Abstract—Monocular depth foundation models make it tempt-
ing to replace depth sensors on mobile robots with a single RGB
camera. We show that, used off the shelf, this is unsafe: in a simu-
lated indoor navigation benchmark, a standard Nav2 stack driven
by Depth Anything V2 (metric) collides in 63% of 120 episodes,
because the model systematically over-estimates the distance to
nearby obstacles. We propose a lightweight plug-in module that
turns predicted depth into safe depth. A 0.58 M-parameter head
on top of the frozen depth model predicts, per pixel, a log-depth
correction µ and an uncertainty σ, trained with a β-weighted
Gaussian negative log-likelihood. The navigation stack consumes
the one-sided bound ˆdsafe = d eµ−kσ, where k is set by conformal
calibration on a held-out world, and uncertainty also decides
which pixels may be treated as floor. The correction halves the
depth error (AbsRel 0.262 →0.134) and reduces near-range
over-estimation from 59.8% to 12.0%. In navigation, safe depth
raises the success rate from 37% to 70% and lowers collisions
from 63% to 3%, close to the 2% of ground-truth depth. A con-
stant safety margin calibrated to the same pixel coverage reaches
only 30% success because it freezes the robot; uncertainty-aware
safety wins 52 of 56 discordant paired episodes against it. Gains
hold on glass, texture-less walls and dark regions. Source code,
pre-trained models, and test data results are publicly available
at: https://github.com/saeed5959/depth safe robot
Index Terms—Collision avoidance, monocular depth estima-
tion, uncertainty quantification, mobile robot navigation.
I. INTRODUCTION
D
EPTH perception is the basis of obstacle avoidance for
mobile robots. LiDAR and RGB-D sensors provide it
directly, but add cost, weight and power, which is why camera-
only navigation has long been attractive for small or low-cost
platforms [1], [2]. Monocular depth estimation has progressed
from early multi-scale networks [6] through training on mixed
datasets [7] to foundation models such as Depth Anything
V2 [8] and Metric3D v2 [9], which predict metric depth zero-
shot in unseen scenes [13]. Navigation systems increasingly
build on such models, from monocular reconstruction for
micro aerial vehicles [2] and risk-aware model predictive
control [4] to off-road stacks [5] and safety layers for learned
navigation policies [3], [12].
Foundation-model depth, however, is not equally reliable
everywhere. Errors concentrate on texture-less surfaces, trans-
parent objects, dark regions and, importantly, scenes outside
the training distribution. For navigation the direction of the
error matters: under-estimating depth makes a robot cautious,
over-estimating it makes obstacles appear farther than they

are and leads to collisions. A cost map built directly from
predicted depth treats every pixel as equally trustworthy, which
MonoMPC [4] identified as a main cause of collisions of
standard Nav2 pipelines on Depth Anything point clouds.
Uncertainty quantification offers a principled way out.
Aleatoric uncertainty can be learned by predicting a variance
with a likelihood loss [14]; evidential regression [15] and
post-hoc heads on frozen networks such as BayesCap [10]
avoid sampling at test time. A recent study combined several
uncertainty methods with Depth Anything V2 and concluded
that likelihood-based heads give useful pixel-wise uncertainty
at little cost [11]. What is less studied is how such uncertainty
should enter a classical navigation stack, and whether it
actually improves closed-loop safety compared with simply
being more conservative everywhere.
This paper addresses that question with a deliberately simple
design. Our contributions are:
• A plug-in safe-depth module: a small head on a frozen
depth foundation model that predicts a per-pixel bias
correction µ and uncertainty σ, and outputs the one-sided
bound d eµ−kσ for an unchanged Nav2 stack. k is set by
conformal calibration on a held-out world.
• An analysis of how depth uncertainty interacts with a
downward-looking camera: shrinking depth along rays
lifts the floor into phantom obstacles. We resolve this
with an uncertainty-aware ground test.
• A paired closed-loop evaluation with 120 episodes per
method in procedurally generated indoor worlds with
glass, blank walls and dark regions, comparing ground-
truth depth, raw monocular depth, a constant margin
with the same calibrated coverage, the corrected depth
alone, and the full method, with McNemar tests on paired
outcomes.
II. RELATED WORK
Monocular depth estimation. Eigen et al. [6] introduced
deep networks for single-image depth. MiDaS [7] showed
that mixing diverse datasets yields robust zero-shot transfer,
but predicts relative depth. Depth Anything V2 [8] trains a
large teacher on synthetic data and distils it through pseudo-
labelled real images, and provides metric variants fine-tuned
on indoor (Hypersim) and outdoor data. Metric3D v2 [9]
resolves scale ambiguity with a canonical camera space. A
recent survey [13] summarises this progress and notes that
robustness and reliability remain open problems.
Monocular navigation. Camera-only navigation has been
approached with learned traversability classifiers and gener-
ated depth [1], with monocular metric reconstruction feeding

a motion-primitive planner [2], and with end-to-end diffusion
policies [3]. MonoMPC [4] conditions a learned probabilistic
collision model on the estimated point cloud and plans with
risk-aware MPC. CARE [12] adds a repulsive-force safety
layer to visual navigation foundation models using monocular
depth. Marsal et al. [5] compare LiDAR with monocular depth
inside an open-source off-road stack. Unlike these works, we
keep the standard planner and cost map, and change only the
depth that is fed to them.
Uncertainty in depth. Kendall and Gal [14] distinguished
aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty and learned heteroscedastic
variance for depth regression. Seitzer et al. [16] showed that
the Gaussian NLL lets networks “give up” on hard samples
and proposed the β-NLL. Evidential regression [15] predicts
both uncertainty types in one pass. BayesCap [10] learns
calibrated uncertainty for frozen networks, the setting we
adopt. Landgraf et al. [11] benchmarked uncertainty methods
on Depth Anything V2. Conformal prediction [17] provides
distribution-free coverage guarantees from a calibration set;
we use it to set the safety factor k.
III. METHOD
A. Overview
Fig. 1 summarises the pipeline. An RGB frame is passed
through a frozen metric Depth Anything V2 model (ViT-S,
Hypersim checkpoint, input 518 px). Its depth dDA, decoder
features and the image feed a small head that outputs µ and
s = log σ per pixel. The resulting depth image is converted
to a point cloud and consumed by an unmodified Nav2 [18]
voxel cost map, planner and controller.
B. Correction and uncertainty head
We hook the 32-channel feature map that enters the final
convolution of the DPT decoder. Together with log dDA and
the normalised RGB image it forms a 36-channel input at
half the network resolution. The head is a small U-Net: two
convolution blocks, two stride-2 stages with residual dilated
convolutions (dilations up to 8, for context on large uniform
surfaces), two up-sampling stages with skip connections, and
a 1 × 1 output layer, 0.58 M parameters in total. The output
layer is initialised to µ = 0, σ = 0.2, so training starts exactly
from the raw model. Working in log-depth makes errors
multiplicative, which matches how monocular depth errors
scale with distance. The corrected depth and its uncertainty

C. Training objective
With the residual r = log dGT−log ˆd, the Gaussian negative
log-likelihood is

Its minimum over s lies at σ = |r|, so the head learns, without
any uncertainty labels, the typical error size of each kind of
pixel, while µ learns the systematic part of the error. Because
the gradient of µ is scaled by σ−2, plain NLL tends to inflate
σ on hard pixels instead of correcting them. We therefore
use the β-NLL [16], multiplying the loss by sg(σ2β) with
β = 0.5, where sg stops the gradient. Pixels are weighted
by w = 1/ max(dGT, 0.5 m), since nearby obstacles cause
collisions, and pixels with invalid ground truth or depth outside
[0.1, 10] m are masked.
D. Safe depth and calibration
The depth passed to navigation is the one-sided lower bound
ˆdsafe = dDA eµ−kσ.
(3)
Instead of fixing k = 2, which assumes a perfectly calibrated
Gaussian, we choose k by split-conformal calibration [17]: on
a held-out validation world we compute the normalised scores
z = (log ˆd −log dGT)/σ of pixels closer than 3 m and set k to
their 97.7% quantile, so that dGT ≥ˆdsafe holds for 97.7% of
near pixels on that world. This gave k = 3.0. For the constant-
margin baseline dDAe−c, c is calibrated the same way to the
same coverage, which gave c = 0.73 (depth scaled by 0.48).
Both baselines are therefore equally safe at the pixel level;
they differ only in where the margin is spent.
E. Integrating safe depth with the cost map
Phantom floor. The camera looks down at the floor. Shrink-
ing depth along a ray moves a floor point towards the camera
and upwards: for a camera at height H, a floor pixel whose
depth is under-estimated by a fraction ϵ is lifted by Hϵ at
any range. With H = 0.55 m, a 14% under-estimate already
exceeds the cost map’s obstacle threshold, and safe depth
turned the whole floor into lethal cells. We therefore classify
ground pixels explicitly. Each pixel’s ray meets the floor plane
at depth dfloor, obtained from the camera intrinsics and its pose
above the floor. A pixel is ground if
log dest −log dfloor ≥−τ,
(4)
where dest is the method’s best estimate (dDA for the raw
and margin variants, ˆd otherwise), and its depth is replaced
by dfloor, so it clears but never marks the cost map. We
use τ
= 0.15 for all variants and, for the safe variant,
τ = max(0.15, kσ): a pixel is treated as floor if the floor
lies within its own uncertainty band. Uncertainty thus decides
both where obstacles are and whether a pixel is an obstacle at
all.
Range and timing. Monocular depth is reliable at short
range, so for all monocular variants obstacles are marked
within 2.5 m and cleared by ray tracing up to 5 m (4 m and 5 m
for ground truth). Because inference finishes about one second
after the image was captured, the depth node republishes the
camera intrinsics with the depth image’s time stamp; otherwise
the point-cloud conversion silently drops most frames.

B. Worlds
A procedural generator builds indoor worlds from a seed: an
8 m corridor (1.8 m wide) with doors, pictures and cardboard
boxes, opening into a 7 × 8 m hall with furniture from the
AWS RoboMaker small-house set (Fig. 2). Three hard cases
can be enabled: a free-standing glass partition (tinted pane
with a frosted safety band and rails), blank texture-less white
walls, and a dark half of the hall lit at about 10%. Five
training worlds use different subsets of hard cases; one of
them (train_03) is held out for validation and calibra-
tion. Four test worlds are never used for training or tuning:
test_clean without hard cases, and test_01–03 with all
three.
C. Episodes and metrics
Each test world has 30 episodes: the four canonical goals
from the spawn pose and 11 sampled start-goal pairs, each run
twice with small start perturbations (±0.1 m, ±10◦), giving
120 paired episodes per method (Fig. 3). Starts and goals keep
at least 0.45 m clearance and are connected by an A* path
for a 0.30 m robot. An episode is labelled easy with line of
sight, hard if its path passes a hard case or a narrow gap, and
medium otherwise. It ends with success when Nav2 reaches
the goal within 0.3 m, collision on any contact of the robot
body (bumper sensor), timeout after 2.5 LA∗/vmax+20 s, stuck
after 40 s without 0.1 m progress, or failure when Nav2 aborts.
We report success rate with Wilson 95% intervals, outcome
rates, SPL, time to goal and minimum clearance. Because all
methods run the same episodes, we compare them with exact
McNemar tests on the discordant pairs.

D. Training data
RGB images and ground-truth depth were recorded on
the five training worlds while the robot executed episodes
with both ground-truth and raw monocular depth, so that
the data include the close approaches that precede monocular
collisions. A frame was stored every 0.2 m or 10◦of motion.
Training uses 7,045 frames from four worlds; train_03 is
used for validation and calibration. The head was trained for 5
epochs with AdamW (learning rate 10−3, one-cycle schedule),
batch size 6, horizontal flips and brightness augmentation, on
a single GTX 1650 (4 GB) with the depth model frozen in half
precision.

V. RESULTS
A. Depth accuracy
Table I reports depth accuracy on the test worlds. Raw
Depth Anything V2 is far from its benchmark accuracy in this
setting: the mean relative error is 26%, only 62.5% of pixels
lie within 25% of the truth, and 59.8% of pixels closer than
3 m are over-estimated by more than 0.2 m. The error is not
a constant scale factor, as the ratio of predicted to true depth
varied between roughly 0.8 and 2 across scenes. The head
halves AbsRel, raises δ1.25 to 91.9% and cuts dangerous near-
range over-estimation five-fold. On the validation world the
area under the sparsification error curve was 0.028, indicating
that σ ranks errors well.
B. Navigation
Table II summarises the 120 paired episodes. With ground-
truth depth the stack succeeds in 93% of episodes. Raw
monocular depth collides in 63%; it never times out or gets
stuck, which is the signature of over-confidence: obstacles
are placed too far away and the robot drives into them. Its
minimum clearance of 0.25 m is exactly the contact distance
of the body.
The constant margin removes most collisions (5%) but
leaves the robot stuck or timed out in 65% of episodes.
Shrinking every depth by half narrows the 1.8 m corridor
and the gaps between furniture until no path remains. It
is less successful than raw depth. Correcting the bias (µ)
raises success to 60% and lowers collisions to 15%. Adding
uncertainty gives the best monocular result: 70% success with
3% collisions, close to the 2% of ground truth, and a minimum
clearance of 0.45 m. The safe variant takes slightly longer than
ground truth (26 s vs. 24.5 s) because it keeps more distance
where it is unsure.
Table III shows that the differences are significant on paired
outcomes. The safe variant wins 45 of 50 discordant episodes
against raw depth and 52 of 56 against the margin. Adding
σ to the correction helps on its own (20 vs. 8, p = 0.036).
Ground truth remains clearly better (30 vs. 2).
C. Hard cases
Table IV breaks success down by the hard case an episode’s
path passes; an episode can count in several groups. The
ordering raw < µ < safe holds in every group, the margin
is never better than µ, and the safe variant improves over raw
depth by 33-37 percentage points in each group, including the
scenes without hard cases. Glass remains the hardest case for
all monocular variants: the safety band and rails provide only
weak cues, and the glass groups are small (16 episodes), so
their intervals are wide. Note that the simulated depth camera
sees glass as solid, which makes the ground-truth baseline
optimistic compared with real depth sensors.
VI. DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
Where the margin goes matters. The margin and the
safe variant are calibrated to the same pixel coverage, yet
their outcomes differ by 40 percentage points. A uniform
margin spends caution everywhere, including on well-textured
walls and furniture that the model sees reliably. σ spends
it where errors actually occur. Bias correction alone is not
sufficient either: µ still collides in 15% of episodes, because
its remaining errors are not systematic and only the uncertainty
term can cover them.
Uncertainty and geometry. Lowering depth along camera
rays has side effects that a cost map does not expect: it lifts
the floor and pulls side walls inwards. The first we resolve
with the uncertainty-aware ground test. The second remains:
with large k, narrow passages shrink, and part of the remaining
27% of non-collision failures are such conservative stops. A
calibration target defined on navigation outcomes, or applying
the bound only to nearby obstacles, could trade these off better
than the pixel-level coverage used here.
Limitations. All experiments are in simulation with one
robot, one camera configuration and one depth model (ViT-
S). Ground-truth odometry isolates perception but hides local-
isation error. The ground test assumes a flat floor. The head
is trained on simulated data from the same generator family
as the test worlds, although on different layouts, so transfer to
real images needs to be verified. Monocular inference at about
1 Hz on a 4 GB GPU also limits the speed at which the robot
can safely drive.

VII. CONCLUSION
Off-the-shelf monocular depth from a foundation model is
not a safe drop-in replacement for a depth sensor: in our
benchmark it made a standard navigation stack collide in most
episodes. A small head on the frozen model, trained to predict
a correction and an uncertainty, and a conformally calibrated
lower bound turn this depth into safe depth that an unmodified
Nav2 stack can use. It nearly doubles the success rate and
brings collisions close to those of ground-truth depth, while a
constant margin with the same calibrated safety level leaves
the robot unable to move. Future work will validate the module
on real robots, use navigation-aware calibration, and combine
aleatoric with epistemic uncertainty for unseen environments.
