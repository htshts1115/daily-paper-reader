## Abstract 

Many new AI models estimate how far away things are, in metres, from a single photo. To do this they must also work out what camera took the photo. We check whether they re-ally do with a simple test: digital zoom, that is, cropping a photo and enlarging the crop back to full size. Zooming does not move anything in the scene, so a correct model should give the same distance for every point that is still in the picture. The test is free, exact and needs no ground-truth labels. We describe a model’s reaction with two numbers: how much its depth changes with the zoom factor s (written 

s−α; ideal α = 0 ) and how much its estimate of the cam-era’s focal length grows (written sβ ; ideal β = 1 ). On 800 images from KITTI, NYUv2 and DIODE, the widely used Depth Anything V2 has α = 0 .95 : it treats a zoomed photo almost exactly as if the camera had moved closer, because it judges distance mainly from how large things look. Models that also predict the camera remove only part of this error (α ≈ 0.31 –0.36), because their focal-length estimate grows too little. When we give Depth Pro and DA3-Nested the true camera, their depth stays correct ( α ≈ 0), so the error comes from their camera estimate, not from their depth es-timate. We explain why: a zoomed photo is, pixel for pixel, identical to a normal photo of a scene squashed in depth, so an object’s size alone cannot tell the two apart, and a camera estimator trained by regression plays safe and stays close to the cameras it saw in training. Undoing this “play-ing safe” needs no labels and no training, and it removes Depth Pro’s error ( α = 0 .04 ) at almost no cost on nor-mal photos. Control tests, including a real optical zoom lens, rule out image blur and resizing artefacts as the cause. Finally, fine-tuning on unlabeled photos so that the model agrees with itself across zooms lowers α to 0.05 on KITTI and 0.07 on NYUv2, once we stop the model from learning a “blurry means zoomed” shortcut; we also report what this costs on normal and unseen photos. Code: https:// github.com/rabeyanoor/zoom-equivariance .

## 1. Introduction 

The problem in plain words. Models that tell how far away things are, in metres, from one photo ( metric depth )have improved fast [3, 12, 22, 31, 35]. They now run on phones, robots and cars, often with cameras they never saw in training. But a single photo hides an old problem: a close scene taken with a wide-angle lens and a far scene taken with a telephoto lens can look the same [11]. To answer in metres, a model has to work out the camera too. Some mod-els are given the camera’s settings (its intrinsics , mainly the focal length). Some predict them. Many simply guess me-tres from the pixels, for example from the usual size of cars or doors. Benchmark scores cannot show which of these re-ally happens, because test sets come from only a few cam-eras. 

Our test: digital zoom. We use a test that needs no la-bels and no new data. Crop a photo and enlarge the crop back to full size. The result is exactly the photo a camera with a longer focal length would have taken from the same spot. Nothing in the scene has moved, so every point still in view is at the same distance as before; only the focal length has grown, by the zoom factor s (Sec. 3). A model that understands the camera should therefore keep its depth the same and multiply its focal-length estimate by s. This matters in practice: phones zoom digitally all the time, and so do gallery apps, screenshots and object-detection crops. Earlier work showed that depth maps change under crop-ping [40] and that models which are given the camera fail when a crop leaves the camera settings out of date [14]. What is still missing is a test of today’s metric models in which the cropped photo’s camera is updated correctly. We ask four questions.

What we find. Q1. No tested model that has to guess or ignores the camera passes the test. Depth Anything V2 makes the scene look closer almost in proportion to the zoom ( α = 0 .95 , where α = 1 would mean “exactly like walking closer” and α = 0 means correct; Fig. 1). Models with a camera head do better, but their focal-length esti-mate grows far less than s (Fig. 2). Q2. All failures fol-low one pattern. A zoomed photo is, pixel for pixel, the same as a normal photo of the scene squashed along the viewing direction; we call this the zoom–squash ambiguity .A model that judges distance from size cannot tell the two apart, and lands on the line α + β = 1 (with β the growth of its focal estimate), which is where Depth Pro sits im-age by image. Giving the network the true camera shows where the error lives: Depth Pro and DA3-Nested then keep their depth ( α = −0.03 and −0.04 ), so the whole error comes from their focal-length estimate (Tab. 2). The same ambiguity explains why every focal estimator we test, in-cluding a dedicated calibration network, grows too little: trained by regression, it stays close to the cameras it saw in training. Undoing this, without labels, makes Depth Pro pass the test. Q3. For models that use only pixels, the fail-ure comes from the content looking larger, not from the lost surroundings, on every dataset. Q4. Fine-tuning a model so that it agrees with itself across zooms, in the spirit of [40], first teaches it a shortcut, “blurry means zoomed”. Adding a term that makes the model ignore blur removes the shortcut and brings α close to zero on the training datasets, at a cost on other photos that we report in full.

## 2. Related work 

Metric depth and the camera. Early work gave the focal length to the network [11] or made convolutions camera-aware [6]; FS-Depth [32] augments focal lengths by crop-ping, with depth labels. Today’s models fall into three groups. Some take the camera : Metric3D [12, 36] maps ev-ery image to a canonical camera and needs K at test time, and Depth Any Camera [9] and ZeroDepth [8] are also con-ditioned on it. Some predict the camera : Depth Pro [3] estimates the field of view, UniDepth [20, 22] predicts a ray per pixel, and MoGe-2 [31] returns a metric point map with intrinsics. The rest use pixels only , such as the metric heads of Depth Anything [34, 35] and ZoeDepth [1]. Training on many cameras [17] is meant to make camera inference ro-bust; our audit tests whether it is. 

Depth cues. DepthCues [5] probes which monocular cues large vision models pick up, among them the known size of objects. Our exponent α measures one cue in particular, apparent size, and our cue intervention (Sec. 4) changes it directly while keeping everything else fixed. 

Single-image calibration. Classical methods calibrate from vanishing points and right angles [10]; learned ones regress the intrinsics [4], predict crop- and resize-invariant incidence fields (WildCamera [41]) or local perspective fields [13], or combine a network with geometric optimisa-tion (GeoCalib [28]). These are the cues the zoom–squash ambiguity says a model must use. InFlux [15] benchmarks video cameras whose intrinsics change over time, for exam-ple by zooming, and shows that most calibration methods struggle with it. We ask instead whether depth models re-spond correctly to the one camera change whose effect is known exactly. 

Crops and equivariance. Zhong et al . [40] are the closest to us. They show that supervised depth and normal predic-tors are not equivariant to crop-and-resize and fix it with test-time averaging and a self-consistency loss on unlabeled images; the output is treated as a map that should simply be cropped, and the camera does not appear. UniDepth trains a geometric invariance loss that aligns camera-conditioned depth features across rescaled and cropped views [20]. We instead measure how released models change their metric depth and focal under the exact camera change. For met-ric models a crop is a zoom, the focal length must grow by s, and a model that reads apparent size breaks the rule in a predictable way. Our fine-tuning (Sec. 6) follows the self-consistency idea of [40] with a frozen teacher; we do not claim the loss as new and compare against their stop-gradient target. Kim and Lee [14] study camera-conditioned models whose pipeline passes stale intrinsics after a crop, resize or padding, and propose a mismatch-aware camera module; our stale-K run is one case of this setting, included only to put such models on the same scale. Crops also mat-ter elsewhere: resize-and-crop augmentation breaks plane geometry in self-supervised depth [30], crops cause a per-spective ambiguity for object-level 3D [23] and human meshes [18], and NERO [39] visualises broken equivari-ance. Robustness to camera roll [25] leaves the intrinsics unchanged; a zoom does not. 

Label-free evaluation and adaptation. Self-Geometry [37] adapts 3D foundation models at test time with multi-view constraints, Re-Depth Anything [2] refines depth by self-supervised re-lighting, and test-time adaptation has been used for depth completion [19]. Amodel can be consistently wrong, so we always report the label-free exponents next to errors against ground truth.

A zoomed photo of a scene is pixel-for-pixel the unzoomed photo of the same scene squashed along the viewing axis by s.

This is the classical focal–depth ambiguity of a single view [11], written as an exact, testable statement about a zoom; it is not a new ambiguity. The squashed scene has the same lateral sizes, so every object has a plausible width; only its extent in depth is wrong (a 4.5 m car be-comes 2.25 m long at s = 2 ), and so are the angles between surfaces. Telling the two apart needs priors on 3D shape (aspect ratios in depth, right angles, the rate at which par-allel lines converge), not priors on size. One consequence is a hard limit: a fronto-parallel plane squashed along the viewing axis is still a fronto-parallel plane, so no estima-tor can be equivariant on such scenes without being told the camera. The ambiguity also explains why the third invari-ant is the natural failure mode: believing the squashed scene means α = 1 with β = 0 , a point on the line α + β = 1 .Note that the line only guarantees lateral consistency; along it, depth and shape are wrong unless β = 1 .

Why a focal head under-scales. A simple model shows why a regression-trained focal head has β < 1. Suppose the training cameras spread as log f ∼ N (μ, τ 2) and the image carries evidence x = log f + ϵ, ϵ ∼ N (0 , σ 2). The squared-error optimum is the posterior mean μ + ρ (x − μ)

with ρ = τ 2/(τ 2 + σ2). A zoom adds log s to x, so β = ρ:the focal exponent is the reliability of the camera cue, and it equals 1 only for a perfect cue. Inverting the shrinkage, 

log fc = μ + (log ˆf − μ)/β , restores β = 1 but scales the noise by 1/β , so equivariance and accuracy trade off more steeply the less reliable the cue. Both β and μ can be measured without labels (Sec. 5). 

Models that take the camera as input. Metric3D [12, 36] receives K and predicts depth in a canonical camera, so with the correct K′ it is equivariant by design. When the zoom happens after capture (a gallery crop, a screenshot) the metadata may still describe the original camera [14]; with this stale K only the final scale f / 1000 changes, by 

s, so αstale = αtrue + 1 up to output clipping. Our runs confirm this (mean difference over 800 images 0.999), so a camera-conditioned model with stale metadata behaves like a pixels-only model that reads apparent size. 

## 4. The zoom audit 

Models. We test seven models in three groups (Sec. 2): 

pixels only — Depth Anything V2 metric Small and Base [35] (indoor or outdoor head, matching the dataset) and ZoeDepth NYU+KITTI [1]; predicts camera — Depth Pro [3], MoGe-2 ViT-S [31] and UniDepthV2 ViT-S [22]; 

takes camera — Metric3D v2 ViT-S [12], run once with the correct zoomed K′ and once with the stale K. All use the official weights and the authors’ preprocessing (Hug-ging Face transformers or the official repositories). 

Data. KITTI depth val selection [7, 26] (outdoor, Li-DAR), the 654 official NYUv2 test images [24] (indoor, Kinect, Eigen crop), and the DIODE validation set [27] split into indoor and outdoor scans (laser). From each of the four sets we draw a fixed random subset of 200 images (seed 0), identical for all models. Ground-truth intrinsics come with all three datasets.

It is the camera, not the pixels. Setup. We tune Depth Anything V2 metric Small (24.8M parameters). The outdoor head is tuned on the frames of seven KITTI raw drives that do not overlap the 13 drives of the KITTI val selection (3,323 images), and the indoor head on the RGB of the 795 official NYUv2 training images. The frozen baseline is re-evaluated through the same fixed 392-px input as the tuned models, so its α and error can differ from Tab. 1. 

The resolution shortcut and EFT+B. A digital zoom also removes detail, so a student trained with Eq. (4) alone can learn to detect a zoom from sharpness instead of geom-etry. The blur control of Sec. 4 exposes exactly this (Sec. 7). EFT+B adds a third term that removes the shortcut:

## 7. Results: correcting the models 

Plain self-consistency learns a shortcut. EFT lowers α

of DA-V2-S from 1.03 to 0.18 on KITTI and from 0.98 to 0.12 on NYUv2 (Tab. 4). The blur control reveals how: the tuned model moves depth by 29.6% on KITTI when the image is only blurred, against 1.1% before tuning. It has learned that a blurry image is a zoomed one. 

EFT+B removes it. With the blur-invariance term the shortcut is gone (4.9% on KITTI, 0.4% on NYUv2) and 

α drops further, to 0.05 and 0.07. On the two datasets never used in training it is 0.37 and 0.40 (frozen: 0.70 and 0.65). Over 3 training seeds α is 0.03 ± 0.05 , 0.09 ± 0.03 ,

0.33 ± 0.03 and 0.41 ± 0.01 (mean ± sd, same order). 

The cost. AbsRel on unzoomed KITTI images rises from 0.138 to 0.175, and on DIODE-outdoor from 0.731 to 1.000 (higher for every seed), while NYUv2 is about unchanged (0.344 to 0.326 ±0.026 over seeds). A stronger anchor does not buy it back (supplement). 

What does the work. Blur augmentation alone leaves most of the effect in place ( α 0.82 on KITTI, 0.56 on NYUv2). The student’s own stop-gradient target, as in Zhong et al . [40], reaches a similar α (0.18, 0.11) but drifts: the blur shortcut returns (25.8% on KITTI) and unzoomed AbsRel degrades most (0.222 on KITTI). Teacher, zoom term and blur term are each needed. Correcting only the scale. EFT-S, which learns one num-ber per image, reaches α = 0 .04 on NYUv2 at no un-zoomed cost (AbsRel 0.346, frozen 0.344). It also trans-fers better than EFT+B to the unseen DIODE ( α = 0 .23 

and 0.27, against 0.37 and 0.40), but is weaker on KITTI (α = 0 .19 ) and costs more unzoomed accuracy there and on DIODE-outdoor. Tuning only Depth Pro’s focal head (EFT-F) helps on its training domains, not on DIODE (sup-plement). 

## 8. Limitations and conclusion 

Limitations. Real optical zoom is tested on only 50 scenes from one lens. When the camera is not given, a crop also removes surrounding context, so α = 0 is a goal, not a promise; the test measures how far a model is from the law and in which direction. Giving the model the true camera is an exact experiment only for Depth Pro and DA3-Nested. EFT+B lowers accuracy on normal and unseen photos. Fo-calUnshrink is a first-order correction; it needs unlabeled photos from the target domain to set its scale, and a reliable focal cue. 

Conclusion. In short: today’s metric depth models that are not told the camera mistake a digital zoom for moving closer, and their focal-length estimates stay too close to the cameras they were trained on. Our zoom test needs no la-bels and no new data, takes only a crop and a resize, and should be a routine check for any model that reports depth in metres. LLM Usage Statement

## F. A calibration-only reference 

GeoCalib [28] predicts the camera but no depth. Run on the same zooms of the same 800 images, its focal exponent is β = 0 .60 (median over images; 0.72 on KITTI, 0.80 on NYUv2, 0.35 on DIODE-in, 0.47 on DIODE-out), against the ideal β = 1 , and its median focal error on the unzoomed images is a factor 1.29. A model built only for calibration also under-scales the focal under zoom, so the under-scaling of the depth models’ focal heads ( β = 0 .65 for Depth Pro, 0.56 for MoGe-2-S) is not specific to depth estimation. 

A calibrator’s focal in place of the true one. The true camera is rarely available, so a natural alternative is to give a depth model the focal of a dedicated calibrator. For Depth Pro this is exact without a new run: its metres are its focal times a camera-free output, so replacing its focal by Geo-Calib’s rescales each depth map by fGeoCalib / ˆf . On the 800 images of the main sample this gives α = 0 .35 (own fo-cal 0.31, true focal −0.03 , FocalUnshrink 0.04) and an un-zoomed scale error of 0.226 (own focal 0.136). GeoCalib’s focal under-scales under zoom as well, so it does not repair the zoom response, and it is less accurate for Depth Pro’s depth than Depth Pro’s own focal.

## G. FocalUnshrink 

Table S5 gives the shrinkage inversion of Secs. 3 and 5 for every focal head. Per model, ˆβ is the least-squares slope of log ˆfs/ ˆf1 on log s pooled over images (a pooled slope is more stable than the per-image median of Tab. 1, 0.65 for Depth Pro), and μ is the mean unzoomed log ˆf of the tar-get dataset; both come from one half of each dataset’s im-ages and are applied to the other half, then the halves swap. Without labels the correction cannot remove a model’s bias on unzoomed images: it is centred on the model’s own esti-mate, so at 1× it only spreads the estimates around μ. What it removes is the error that the zoom adds. The result follows ˆβ. Depth Pro, with the most reliable focal cue, becomes equivariant and more accurate under zoom at no cost unzoomed. MoGe-2-S also gains under zoom (its depth is not exactly its focal times a camera-free output, so its depth columns are an approximation). For UniDepthV2-S and DA3-Nested, with ˆβ ≤ 0.50 , the 1/ ˆβ

amplification of per-image noise outweighs the gain, and for GeoCalib the focal error at 3× falls from 0.453 to 0.306. Equivariance obtained this way is free of training, but its accuracy is bounded by how much the camera cue actually knows, which is what β measures. 

Sensitivity. Table S6 varies the two measured quantities. 

ˆβ is known to within its scene-bootstrap interval (Depth Pro 0.70 [0.62, 0.78]); at the ends of that interval Depth Pro’s corrected α is 0.13 and −0.09 , an over- or under-correction of the size the interval implies. The prior centre μ cancels in the ratio of two zooms, so it cannot affect α; taking it from the other datasets instead of the target one changes only the unzoomed scale (Depth Pro 0.245, against 0.138 with the target’s μ). Unlabeled target images matter for scale, not for equivariance. 

Non-Gaussian priors. The inversion is exact when log f

and the cue are Gaussian. Real camera distributions are not (phones cluster at a few focal lengths). Table S7 simulates an exact posterior-mean focal head under Laplace, uniform and bimodal priors of the same variance. The measured 

ˆβ stays close to the Gaussian reliability ρ, but the head is no longer linear in the cue, so a single ˆβ cannot undo it image by image: the per-image zoom error falls from 0.54 to 0.48 for the bimodal prior at ρ = 0 .65 , against 0 for the Gaussian one. In every case the error at 1× grows, by the 

1/ ˆβ amplification of Sec. 3. FocalUnshrink is therefore a first-order correction; a non-linear inverse would need the prior’s shape, which self-zoom alone does not give. 

## H. Objects of familiar size and surfaces 

If a model fixed scale from the usual size of known objects, its depth on those objects should follow the zoom less than its depth on walls, floors and roads. We split each image with SegFormer-B5 trained on ADE20K [33] into object classes (people, cars, chairs, beds, etc .) and surface classes (wall, floor, road, ceiling, etc .), and fit α per region from the 2× and 3× zooms on the 200 images per set. On the 429 images with both kinds of region, the median α on ob-jects and surfaces is 1.07 and 1.01 for DA-V2-S, 0.38 and 0.36 for Depth Pro, and 0.41 and 0.39 for MoGe-2-S. Ob-jects follow the zoom slightly more , not less (median paired difference 0.03, 0.02 and 0.03; larger on objects in 55%, 65% and 69% of images; Wilcoxon p = 0 .0088 , 2.5e-13 and 2.9e-12). We find no sign that familiar objects anchor the scale; the difference is small and the response is nearly uniform over the image.

Flagging images to distrust. The same score also works per image. For each model and dataset we rank the images by their own inconsistency at 2× and keep only the most consistent ones. So a user can run the zoom check on their own photos and set aside the images where the model is least sure of the scale, without any labels. 

A training-free fix when the zoom is known (ZoomFix). 

Phones record a digital zoom ratio, and apps know their own crops. Since α needs no labels, a user can measure it on their own photos and multiply the depth of zoomed images by sˆα. We evaluate this cross-fitted: ˆα is measured with-out labels on half of a dataset’s images and the correction scored on the other half. For DA-V2-S at 3× it removes 61%, 38% and 70% of the error the zoom adds on KITTI (AbsRel 0.758 to 0.384, against 0.149 for the unzoomed prediction on the same pixels), NYUv2 (0.675 to 0.495) and DIODE-indoor (0.598 to 0.365). On DIODE-outdoor the mean error rises (0.558 to 0.661). The cause is a heavy tail: even unzoomed, the mean AbsRel there (0.605) is far above the median (0.430), and scaling depth up amplifies the images that are already over-estimated, while the typi-cal image improves. Depth Pro, whose α is already small, barely changes. A single global factor cannot undo a zoom that also distorts shape (Sec. 4); it is a stop-gap for deployed pixels-only models, not a replacement for a camera-aware one.

## N. EFT-S per domain 

Head. c is a two-layer MLP (198,657 parameters) on the last-layer class token and the mean patch token of the frozen ViT. Its last layer starts at zero, so training starts from the frozen model, and the depth shape is the frozen model’s by construction. 

Training from a cache. Because c is one number per view, each term of the loss depends only on the distribution of the residual log ˆD0(view ) − log ˆD0(I) over the crop. We store 100 quantiles of that residual and the token features for 4,000 random zoom and blur views per domain, drawn as for EFT+B from the same training images, from a sin-gle pass of the frozen model. The head is then fitted on this cache (60 epochs, AdamW, learning rate 10 −3, batch 64) on a CPU. 

Results. Table S10 gives the per-dataset numbers of the scale-only variant EFT-S discussed in Sec. 7, next to the frozen model and EFT+B. Without the blur term, blur alone moves its KITTI depth by 23.6% (7.3% with it): EFT-S finds the same blur shortcut as EFT.

The audit itself never compresses a zoomed image, so the zoom response of Tab. 1 is not caused by compression; the question is whether compression changes it. For four of the five models, compressing after the zoom moves depth by a factor of 1.00 to 1.05 relative to the plain zoom, about the size of the JPEG control itself. ## R. Real optical zoom 

SR-RAW [38] holds static scenes shot from a tripod with a Sony FE 24–240 mm zoom lens at seven focal lengths. The camera does not move, so the true depth of every point is the same in every shot and no depth labels are needed. We use the camera JPEGs of the test set at a long side of 1024 px and the EXIF focal length; the shots with s ≤ 3.2 against the widest one are compared, 150 zoomed shots in all 50 test sequences. As SR-RAW assumes, a zoomed shot shows the centred 1/s box of the wide shot; we compare the median log depth over the inner 80% of the zoomed shot with the median over the matching box of the wide shot’s prediction, which leaves a margin for the small shift of the lens centre. The same crop of the wide shot, done digitally, is scored on the same scenes. Models that take the camera get K from the EXIF 35 mm-equivalent focal. Table S15 gives the result. For DA-V2-S a real lens and a digital zoom are the same to the model ( α = 0 .76 and 0.76, Wilcoxon p = 0 .45 over sequences): the error is not an artefact of resampling. Depth Pro keeps its depth under both ( −0.08 , −0.08 ) and Metric3D v2 with the EXIF cam-era does too ( −0.04 ). MoGe-2-S and UniDepthV2-S shrink 

more under the real lens than under the digital zoom (0.38 against 0.26, and 0.62 against 0.39). EFT+B lowers α of DA-V2-S on this unseen data to 0.59, less than on its train-ing domains. Figure S6 shows the sequence whose DA-V2-S α is the median over all sequences.
