## Abstract 

Self-supervised monocular depth networks can predict a per-pixel uncertainty, which is mainly evaluated by how well it ranks pixels according to their error. Such metrics are insensitive to the magnitude of the uncertainty, so an un-certainty half as large as the actual error obtains the same score. In this paper, we investigate whether the predicted uncertainty is as large as the depth error, both on average (scale ) and across confident and uncertain, near and far pix-els ( shape ). On KITTI, the scale is miscalibrated for every uncertainty estimate we evaluate, and the error of Mono-Prob is nearly twice its predicted uncertainty. We propose MonoCal, which corrects both the scale and the shape with-out ground-truth depth. Scale calibration multiplies the un-certainty by a single constant measured from stereo pairs, requiring no retraining, and this constant closely matches the one fitted with ground truth. Since a constant cannot change the shape, we further retrain the uncertainty to pre-

Systems that fuse depth measurements rely on the magni-tude of the predicted uncertainty. Dense mapping systems weight or reject each depth measurement according to an uncertainty that is specified by hand as a function of dis-tance or of the sensor [3, 31–33], learned by a supervised network [3, 24], or derived from bundle adjustment [39]. A learned uncertainty can replace these models only if it has the correct magnitude. However, self-supervised monocular depth networks that predict an uncertainty σ, interpreted as the standard deviation of the predicted depth, are typically evaluated using sparsification metrics such as the area un-der the sparsification error (AUSE) [18, 37]. These metrics assess whether σ ranks the pixels according to their error. Since they are invariant to any rescaling of σ, a σ that is half or twice the size it should be obtains the same score [13, 29]. In this paper, we measure this magnitude using a single ratio, the absolute depth error divided by the predicted un-certainty, which equals 1 for a calibrated σ and 2 for a σ that is half the size it should be. We examine two properties of this ratio (Tab. 1). The scale describes whether the ratio is 1 over all pixels, and the shape describes whether the ratio is the same for pixels of small and large σ and for near and far pixels [35]. For MonoProb [29], which predicts the uncer-tainty as a standard deviation in meters, the error is nearly twice the predicted uncertainty over all pixels (Fig. 1a) and more than three times in the nearest depth range, indicat-ing that the network is overconfident. Moreover, the scale is miscalibrated for every uncertainty estimate we evaluate on KITTI, including published self-supervised networks, three other backbones and systems trained with depth labels, by factors between 0.6 and 6 (Fig. 4). For self-supervised net-works, the training objective does not constrain this mag-nitude, since the network is trained to reconstruct images rather than to regress depth [37] and no loss term compares 

σ with a depth error. Existing systems correct the magnitude using a constant fitted against a reference depth, on a network trained with la-bels (Sec. 2). However, this constant is not universal. When measured by the same rule, it varies across networks and datasets (Fig. 4), which has also been observed in supervised regression [27]. The constant therefore has to be measured where σ is used, and for self-supervised depth this is a set-ting without labels. To address this, we propose MonoCal, which corrects the scale and the shape without labels using two components. The first component, scale calibration , measures the con-stant. We obtain a depth from a stereo pair by semi-global matching [14] and compare it with the depth of the network. The median ratio of this residual to σ then determines a sin-gle constant. This procedure requires neither ground-truth depth nor retraining of the network. The stereo pair is re-quired only to measure the constant, while inference still uses a single image. On KITTI, the constant is within ×1.08 

of the ground-truth constant for 16 of 17 uncertainty esti-mates (Fig. 4, Fig. 1a). However, a constant can only correct the scale. On the reliability diagram in Fig. 1(a), a constant shifts the curve horizontally without changing its slope. Similarly, it shifts the entire profile along depth, so that MonoProb still un-derestimates the uncertainty in the nearest depth range even with the ground-truth constant (Fig. 3). The second com-ponent, the disagreement target , is designed to correct this shape. We retrain σ to predict where the depth of the net-work disagrees with that of a second, independently trained self-supervised network of a different architecture, and then apply scale calibration (Fig. 2). On KITTI, the ratio in the nearest depth range becomes 0.96, and the calibration er-rors across uncertainty levels and across depth are lower than those of the ground-truth constant (Tab. 2). The consistency gain generalizes to two of the three other backbones (Tab. 3).

## 2. Related Work 

Uncertainty in self-supervised depth. Self-supervised monocular depth is trained by view synthesis, from stereo pairs [10] or video [11]. Per-pixel uncertainty was intro-duced in this setting as the width of a Laplacian over the pho-tometric residual [21, 49], by applying the heteroscedastic loss of Kendall and Gal [20] to the reprojection error. In this formulation, the magnitude of the uncertainty is determined by the photometric error rather than by a depth error, and training a variance with a Gaussian likelihood has known failure modes [40, 41]. Poggi et al . [37] compared empir-ical, predictive and Bayesian estimators using the ranking metrics AUSE and AURG, and found self-teaching, in which the depth of a second network serves as the target, to be among the most useful. MonoProb [29] samples the pho-tometric loss over a predicted distribution and offers a self-distilled variant in which a student learns the depth distri-bution of a teacher. However, sparsification curves are invariant to a monotone rescaling of σ, and ARU and RMSU, which are pointwise distances between σ and the error, are minimized by a σ below cali-bration (Sec. 5.2). A second depth has previously been used as a target, namely SfM depth in Klodt and Vedaldi [21] and a teacher of the same architecture in self-teaching and in the self-distillation of MonoProb. In all three cases, this target also supervises the depth. In contrast, we use the disagree-ment between two networks of different architectures as the target of σ alone. 

Calibration of regression uncertainty. Pernot [35] defines three calibration targets for a predicted σ, namely average calibration (our scale ), consistency (calibration as a func-tion of σ itself) and adaptivity (calibration as a function of the input), and shows that consistency does not imply adap-tivity. We refer to the last two as the shape . The expected normalized calibration error of Levi et al . [28] measures consistency as the mean over σ bins of |RMSE /RMV − 1|.Rescaling σ by a constant fitted on labeled data is a stan-dard recalibration step. For example, σ scaling [27, 28] fits a single scalar by the likelihood on a validation set, sim-ilar to temperature scaling for classification [12], and iso-tonic recalibration [23] fits a monotone map. Calibration without target labels has been studied for classification un-der domain shift [16]. Our work adds a way to measure the constant without labels and a training target that corrects what the constant cannot. In depth estimation, f-Cal [2] re-ports the expected calibration error of a label-trained net-work. McCraith et al . [30] calibrate the metric scale of self-supervised depth rather than the magnitude of its un-certainty. Gustafsson et al . [13] note that a perfect AUSE is compatible with a consistently under-estimated σ. Hor-nauer et al . [15] compare error and uncertainty per bin on self-supervised Monodepth2 and MonoViT, but first apply min–max normalization to both, which again removes the magnitude of σ. Similarly, Landgraf et al . [26] combine five uncertainty estimators with Depth Anything V2 [48] and evaluate how well the uncertainty ranks the errors, not its magnitude. To our knowledge, consistency and adaptivity have not been measured for the σ of self-supervised depth. Concurrently, Deng et al . [7] audit the confidence of seven feed-forward 3D reconstruction models using the ratio of er-ror to predicted uncertainty per confidence bin, and repair it post hoc using a two-constant power law fitted with ground truth per backbone and dataset. In contrast, we evaluate self-supervised monocular σ, measure the constant without la-bels and also examine calibration along depth. 

How systems calibrate uncertainty in practice. Jung et al . [19] fit a constant so that the mean Mahalanobis dis-tance on a validation set is one. MP-SfM [34] fits a con-stant to an SfM point cloud of another dataset and adds a depth-proportional floor. Mono-SF [4] trains a recalibra-tion network on held-out ground truth, and Tao et al . [44] train σ with ground truth and choose the rejection thresh-old by mapping performance. All four build on networks trained with labels, and three of them recalibrate σ before use. OKVIS2-X [3] states that heuristic uncertainty models are not sufficient for learned depth. 

## 3. Measuring the Magnitude of Uncertainty 

For a pixel with predicted depth μ, predicted uncertainty σ

and ground-truth depth dgt , we define the error as the abso-lute depth error e = |μ − dgt |. Since a calibrated σ is as large as the errors it accompanies, we compute the ratio r

of error to predicted σ over a set of pixels using two statis-tics. The first, rmed = median (e/σ) / 0 .6745 , takes the per-pixel ratio first (the median absolute deviation of a Gaus-sian is 0.6745 σ). The second, rrms = RMSE /RMV, di-vides the root-mean-square error by the root-mean-variance. For both statistics, r = 1 corresponds to a calibrated σ and 3r = 2 to a σ that is half the size it should be. The median statistic remains exact for a calibrated Gaussian σ that varies from pixel to pixel within the set. In contrast, the RMS statistic corresponds to what a downstream system assum-ing Gaussian errors observes, but it is sensitive to the tails and can therefore be unreliable under heavy-tailed distribu-tions [36]. We therefore report both statistics and compare results within the same statistic. The scale of σ, not to be confused with the scale of the depth, is defined as r over all pixels, which corresponds to the average calibration of Pernot [35]. The shape describes how r varies across pixels along two axes (Tab. 1), which we call consistency and adaptivity following Pernot [35]. For

## 4. Method 

MonoCal consists of two components (Fig. 2): scale cali-bration , a constant that can be applied to any pretrained net-work (Sec. 4.1), and the disagreement target , a training tar-get for what the constant cannot change (Sec. 4.2 and 4.3). 

4.1. Scale Calibration 

With ground truth, the scale could be corrected by fitting the constant that yields rmed = 1 on labeled images. We measure the same constant using a stereo pair instead of the label. For each validation image, we obtain a depth dS from the stereo pair by semi-global matching [14], which has been used as a proxy label for monocular depth [45]. We align 

dS to the depth μ of the network by the per-image median ratio and measure the constant at the pixels where σ is eval-uated, i.e., the LiDAR-covered pixels of the standard evalu-ation crop. Only the positions of these pixels are used, not their depth values. Without the positions, the constant of MonoCal changes from ×2.26 to ×2.22 , i.e., by less than 2 %. 4.2. Limitation of a Single Constant 

Scale calibration multiplies every σ by the same number. On the reliability diagram of predicted σ against error (Fig. 1a), which is drawn in log coordinates, this multiplication cor-responds to a translation along x. The curve is therefore shifted onto the calibration line, while its slope and its bends are preserved. A calibrated σ yields a slope of 1, since the error grows in proportion to σ. In contrast, MonoProb has a slope of 0.74, both before and after applying any con-stant. When the σ of MonoProb increases tenfold, the er-ror increases only 5.5-fold, which means that σ overstates its own differences and that the uncertainty of small-σ pix-els is underestimated more than that of large-σ pixels. The same holds along depth. A constant scales the entire depth profile of r by a single factor, so correcting the middle ranges leaves both ends underestimated, whereas correct-ing the nearest range makes the middle ranges overestimated (Fig. 3). 4.3. Disagreement Target

Motivation. Disagreement between trained models has long been used to estimate generalization error [22] and pre-dictive uncertainty [25]. Two networks of different architec-tures disagree where at least one of them is inaccurate, which allows their disagreement to serve as a substitute for the er-ror. However, the disagreement does not necessarily have the magnitude of the error. Two self-supervised networks agree with each other more than either agrees with LiDAR, and before applying the constant, the retrained σ is further from calibration than that of MonoProb (Fig. 4). The target therefore determines where σ is large, while scale calibra-tion determines how large it is. Where both networks make the same error, the target provides no signal, and the con-stant can only restore the magnitude.

## 5. Experiments 

5.1. Experimental Setup 

Data and protocol. We conduct our experiments on KITTI [9]. We use the Eigen split [8] with improved ground truth [46], and split its 652 test images by drive into a val-idation set and a test set (326 images each). The constants are estimated and the configuration is selected on the val-idation set, and all methods are evaluated on the test set. Similarly, on Argoverse 1 and DrivingStereo, we estimate the constants on validation logs and evaluate on test logs, whereas nuScenes is evaluated on its validation set. All methods are evaluated on the same pixels, i.e., the official evaluation mask with 10 −3 < d gt < 80 m, in which the nearest LiDAR depth is about 3.7 m. Following the standard protocol for self-supervised monocular depth, μ is scaled per image by its median ratio to the ground truth, and σ is scaled by the same factor. The depth bins are fixed once and shared by all methods. 

Reporting. Each retrained model is trained four times, and we report the mean over the runs. Unless stated otherwise, all numbers are based on the median statistic. 

Reference with labels. The ground-truth constant is the global constant fitted with LiDAR ground truth on the vali-dation set using the same rule as s (×2.15 for MonoProb). It serves as a reference rather than a method, since a label-free result that reaches it achieves what labels provide under a global constant. 

Uncertainty estimates evaluated. In addition to Mono-Prob [29] and its self-distilled checkpoint, we evaluate the self-supervised estimators of Poggi et al . [37], the self-distilled σ that we trained using the public recipes of Mon-odepth2 [11], MonoViT [51] and Lite-Mono [50], and three systems trained with depth labels, namely MaGNet [1], the depth network of OKVIS2-X [3] and Metric3D v2 [17]. We further use Argoverse 1 [6], DrivingStereo [47] and nuScenes [5] as additional datasets. 

5.2. Main Results 

Scale. As shown in Tab. 2, scale calibration reduces the rmed 

of MonoProb from 1.94 to 0.96 without labels or retraining, compared to 0.90 for the ground-truth constant. Since both constants are measured on the validation set, neither yields exactly 1 on the test set. 

Shape. However, a constant alone is insufficient to correct the shape. It reduces only the part of the consistency er-ror caused by the scale, while SI-ENCE remains unchanged. In contrast, MonoCal reduces both the consistency error and SI-ENCE med by a factor of about four compared to any constant, and both are significantly lower than those of the ground-truth constant (Tab. 2). On the reliability diagram, the slope increases to 0.95 (Fig. 1a). The profile along depth also becomes flatter (Fig. 3). Specifically, the ratio in the nearest range decreases from about three to about one, whereas a constant alone leaves the uncertainty underesti-mated in the near range and overestimated in the middle ranges. The same conclusions hold for the RMS statistic on consistency and adaptivity. 

Standard metrics. MonoCal also preserves depth accu-racy, with an abs_rel of 0.091 against 0.092 for MonoProb (Tab. 2). All seven standard depth metrics are in Tab. S3. AUSE, which is invariant to any constant, is 26 % lower than that of MonoProb, although 12 % higher than that of the self-distilled MonoProb checkpoint. In contrast, ARU and RMSU [29] increase, which is expected. These metrics are pointwise distances between σ and |e|, and since |e| is right-skewed, their optima under a global constant are below a calibrated σ. Seitzer et al . [40] observed the same effect when using such a distance as a training loss, which leads to an underestimated variance. Consequently, a σ that is too small by a factor of two is close to its ARU optimum, and every calibrated σ obtains a higher value, by 29–33 % for the ground-truth constant and by 8–21 % for MonoCal (Tab. 2). 

5.3. Generalization 

Scale calibration across networks. On KITTI, the label-free constant corrects the scale of 17 uncertainty estimates (Fig. 4 shows 11 of them, Fig. S1 all). It is within ×1.08 of the ground-truth constant for all of them except the indoor checkpoint of OKVIS2-X. By construction, the scale with respect to the stereo residual is one, and Fig. 4 shows that it is also one with respect to the LiDAR error. Scale calibration works in both directions. For example, the uncertainty of the other OKVIS2-X checkpoint is too large on KITTI (0.66), and scale calibration corrects it to 0.99. 

Other datasets. On Argoverse 1 and DrivingStereo, where the constant is re-measured, the agreement is weaker than on KITTI, and for MonoProb neither constant yields a scale of 1 (Fig. 4). This is likely due to the stereo depth itself, since semi-global matching produces three times as many errors on DrivingStereo as on KITTI [47]. Since nuScenes provides no stereo pairs, the constant cannot be measured on this dataset, and reusing the constant measured on KITTI results in a scale of 1.4 for MonoProb (Fig. 4). 

The disagreement target on other backbones and datasets. The disagreement target reduces the shape errors of backbones that exhibit them (Tab. 3, Fig. 3). We apply it to Monodepth2, MonoViT and Lite-Mono, each trained us-ing its public recipe. On Monodepth2 and Lite-Mono, both shape errors decrease. 5.5. Qualitative Results 

Fig. 5 shows that scale calibration changes how bright the uncertainty is, whereas MonoCal changes where it is bright. In the top rows, the error and the uncertainty share the same color scale in meters, so that a calibrated uncertainty is as bright as its error, and the uncertainty of MonoProb is visi-bly darker. Af-ter scale calibration, red and blue are balanced, but some regions remain red, such as the upper left of (b) and the roadsides in (c), and MonoCal makes these regions closer to white. The uncertainty of MonoCal is also smoother. It no longer follows image texture such as the road markings in (a), which the error does not follow either, while preserv-ing the structures where the error is large, such as the vehi-cles in (b). 

## 6. Conclusion and Limitations 

In this paper, we measured the magnitude of the uncertainty of self-supervised monocular depth as the ratio of error to predicted uncertainty, separating the scale from the shape. We showed that the scale is miscalibrated for every uncer-tainty estimate we evaluated, by a factor that varies across networks and datasets. To correct both the scale and the shape without labels, we proposed MonoCal. Scale calibra-tion measures this factor from stereo pairs and requires no retraining, while the disagreement target corrects the shape, which is unaffected by any constant, by training the uncer-tainty on the disagreement with a second self-supervised network. 

Limitations. Although scale calibration is label-free, it still requires stereo pairs to measure the constant. On nuScenes, which provides no stereo pairs, reusing the constant mea-sured on KITTI results in a scale of 1.4, and on Argoverse 1 and DrivingStereo the constant is less close to the ground-truth constant than on KITTI. In addition, the uncertainty is retrained only on KITTI, and on the two other datasets we evaluate the same σ after re-measuring the constant. We leave measuring the constant without a second sensor for fu-ture work.
