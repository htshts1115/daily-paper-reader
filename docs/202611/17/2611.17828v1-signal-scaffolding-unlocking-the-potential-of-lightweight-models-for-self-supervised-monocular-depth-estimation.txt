# Signal Scaffolding: Unlocking the Potential of Lightweight Models for Self-Supervised Monocular Depth Estimation 

## Zhijie Wang, Li Zhang *, Yujie Chen, Shibiao Deng, Xin Gao, Houzhi Zhou, Jiangshuo Zhang Department of Mathematics, Hefei University of Technology Hefei, China

## Abstract 

With the rising demand for edge devices, lightweight self-supervised monocular depth estimation methods have continued to evolve; however, existing methods generally suffer from poor accuracy. We find that the root cause may lies in the failure of conventional training regimes to fully exploit the inherent potential of lightweight models. To address this, we propose a novel three-phase training strategy for lightweight self-supervised monocular depth estimation, termed Signal Scaffolding (SS). Without intro-ducing any additional dataset or parameters, we apply SS to ReForgeDepth, a lightweight network of our own de-sign. Experiments on the KITTI and Cityscapes datasets demonstrate that our method achieves state-of-the-art per-formance in the accuracy-efficiency trade-off and running in real time on edge devices. These results suggest that lightweight models can be improved further within their ex-isting capacity, and that the central question may be less about how parameters are allocated than about how to un-lock this latent potential through training. 

## 1. Introduction 

Monocular depth estimation is a core perception capabil-ity for autonomous driving and robotics. Supervised meth-ods rely on costly LiDAR or manual annotation, whereas self-supervised methods recover depth from monocular video via photometric consistency alone [17, 63]. The pur-suit of benchmark accuracy has driven a steady increase in model scale: transformer-based [42, 61] and diffusion-based self-supervised methods [18, 25, 44] routinely exceed tens of millions of parameters while pushing the state of the art. However, as perception tasks increasingly shift toward resource-constrained edge devices, deploying networks at such scale poses considerable challenges. Consequently, compact architectures have emerged as an active research

Existing research on lightweight monocular depth esti-mation focuses primarily on network architecture design [12, 32, 57, 66]. For instance, Lyu et al. [37] introduced high-resolution skip connections to preserve spatial details; Zhang et al. [59] proposed a lightweight CNN-Transformer hybrid; and more recently, Chen et al. [4] introduced fea-ture purification to improve estimation efficiency. How-ever, despite continual architectural innovation, lightweight monocular depth estimation has long faced an unexplained accuracy ceiling: the most competitive compact models cluster within a narrow accuracy band, and pushing be-yond it typically demands more parameters or more data [12, 32, 57, 59, 66]. In our experiments, we observe that pretraining does not always substantially improve the performance of lightweight networks—a phenomenon consistent with that reported by Muralidharan et al. [39] in supervised tasks. The prevailing view holds that this issue can only be re-solved by further scaling up the network [66] or by introduc-ing additional semantic segmentation datasets [48]; yet both arXiv:2611.17828v1 [cs.CV] 13 Nov 2026 remedies run counter to the original intent of lightweight de-sign and severely constrain the development of lightweight monocular depth estimation. We argue that the root cause might lie in the training pipeline inherited from networks of conventional scale: pretrain first, then fine-tune directly [14, 15]. Networks with rich redundancy naturally absorb pretraining knowledge [20, 27, 55]; lightweight networks, with fewer parameters and lower redundancy, can likewise absorb this knowledge, yet ultimately transfer only a small fraction of it. To address this challenging issue, we propose Signal Scaffolding (SS), a three-phase training strategy that ex-tends the conventional pretrain-then-fine-tune pipeline. Its central insight is that, once network parameters and train-ing data are fixed, the performance bottleneck is determined not solely by model capacity but also by the training strat-egy. We therefore opt to restructure the training pipeline so as to recover much of the representational capacity lost un-der the standard procedure. The strategy operates on how training is organized rather than on the parameters them-selves. To instantiate and validate this strategy, we further design ReForgeDepth, a lightweight network that incorpo-rates a Gated Multi-Dilation Block (GMD) and a Detail-Compensated Global Interaction (DCGI), together with an RGB Boundary Residual in the decoder, reducing the pa-rameter count while leaving detail and receptive field in-tact. Applied to ReForgeDepth, SS leaves the architec-ture, parameters, and dataset untouched; the model still at-tains state-of-the-art performance in the accuracy-efficiency trade-off on KITTI and Cityscapes, and it also generalizes well to Make3D and runs in real time on edge devices. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose Signal Scaffolding (SS), a novel three-phase training strategy designed to recover much of the representational capacity lost under the standard training procedure; it substantially improves the accuracy of lightweight models without modifying the network architecture or introducing any additional parameters or dataset. • We design ReForgeDepth, a lightweight network whose core comprises a Gated Multi-Dilation Block (GMD) and a Detail-Compensated Global Interaction (DCGI), together with an RGB Boundary Residual in the decoder, reducing the parameter while leaving detail and receptive field intact. • Experiments on the KITTI and Cityscapes datasets demonstrate that ReForgeDepth, when trained with SS, achieves state-of-the-art performance in the accuracy-efficiency trade-off, and runs in real time on edge devices. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Monocular Depth Estimation. 

Depth estimation is an ill-posed problem from a sin-gle image, so learning-based methods mainly differ in their source of supervision. Early models learned from dense an-notations: Eigen et al. [8] established the CNN formulation, followed by up-projection [28], ordinal discretization [11], and transformer backbones [42]. This accuracy depends on costly, range-limited, and noisy LiDAR or depth cam-eras, restricting data scale and label quality. Self-supervised methods instead use photometric consistency, judging depth by how well it reconstructs a nearby view. Godard et al. 

[16] derived this objective from calibrated stereo with left– right consistency, while Zhou et al. [63] showed that a moving monocular camera can jointly estimate depth and ego-motion. Because occlusions and static pixels corrupt photometric error, Monodepth2 [17] introduced minimum reprojection and auto-masking. In parallel, PackNet-SfM [19] proposed 3D packing for resolution-robust generaliza-tion, and MonoViT [61] combined convolutional and trans-former blocks for long-range context. 

2.2. Lightweight Depth Estimation 

Accurate depth networks are costly for edge devices, motivating self-supervised research. Early systems enabled embedded inference through compact encoder–decoder de-signs and pruning [9, 52], followed by lightweight back-bones [40, 41] and hybrid CNN–Transformer architectures [36, 45]. Lite-Mono [59] showed that compact, sub-3M en-coders can remain competitive. However, reduced capac-ity weakens fine structures and sharp depth discontinuities. This penalty has two sources: what a compact architecture cannot represent, and what it can represent but fails to reach under a direct self-supervised objective. The former is a genuine capacity limit; the latter is recoverable through the training signal alone, at no architectural cost—the source this paper targets. 

2.3. Training-Signal Design for Lightweight Depth Estimation 

Lightweight depth estimation is often approached through architectural design, but the training signal also determines how effectively a compact model adapts to the target task. Prior work has explored teacher–student dis-tillation and self-distillation for monocular depth estima-tion [24, 33, 54, 56], demonstrating the value of informa-tive training signals. Conventional distillation, however, has the student mimic the teacher’s outputs [22], with a ceil-ing set by the teacher; self-distillation [33] instead refines the model’s own predictions. Both act as replacement sig-nals rather than ones aligned with the student’s representa-tions. Signal Scaffolding (SS) departs from this paradigm: Figure 2. (a) Overall Architecture of the ReForgeDepth. The proposed architecture comprises three innovative modules: GMD, and DCGI, with detailed implementations illustrated in Figure 3. instead of allocating model capacity, it optimizes the train-ing strategy of a fixed capacity—first adapting the student to the target domain through photometric self-supervision, then gradually introducing domain-matched depth guid-ance. This three-phase strategy aligns the guidance with the student’s task-adapted representations, helping it better exploit its existing capacity. 

## 3. Methods 

We propose ReForgeDepth, a lightweight architec-ture whose Gated Multi-Dilation Block and Detail-Compensated Global Interaction substantially reduce pa-rameters, enabling effective deployment on mobile devices (Figure 2). The DepthNet adopts an encoder (Sec. 3.1) and decoder (Sec. 3.2) design and is jointly optimized with a PoseNet (Sec. 3.3) in a self-supervised manner (Sec. 3.4). A Signal Scaffolding (SS) training strategy (Sec. 3.5) further yields sharper structural details and object contours without inflating the parameter budget or requiring additional data. 

3.1. Encoder Design 

Motivation. A compact monocular-depth encoder must balance broad context for ambiguous regions with high-frequency cues for boundaries and thin structures. Stacked dilated blocks increase context at repeated feed-forward network (FFN) and projection cost, while fixed fusion cannot adapt to scene structure. Gated Multi-Dilation Block (GMD) therefore performs parallel consecutive di-lated convolutions in one block with input-conditioned gates and road-oriented anisotropic kernels. At each stage end, Detail-Compensated Global Interaction (DCGI) cou-ples local features with stage inputs, preserves signed high-frequency residuals, and adds global channel interaction. Together, GMD extracts adaptive local context, while DCGI restores detail and stage-level context efficiently. 

Gated Multi-Dilation Block (GMD). 3.5. Decoupling Depth from Capacity 

The performance plateau of lightweight depth models might stems from a capacity–training mismatch: direct fine-tuning may fail to reach the useful portion of a compact stu-dent’s hypothesis space. Signal Scaffolding (SS) progres-sively organizes training signals, unlocking the student’s re-coverable capacity without architectural or data changes. A domain-matched teacher (Flex-Large [66]) stimulates this latent capability. Our ablation experiments show that this potential does not come entirely from the teacher, but mainly from the inherent potential of the student model. ## 4. Experiments 

This section presents ReForgeDepth and its Signal Scaf-folding framework, and evaluates their effectiveness. For conciseness, the main text reports condensed experimental results, while the complete results are provided in the sup-plementary material. Figure 5. Qualitative results on the KITTI Eigen test split. Zoom-in views of the regions marked by colored boxes. Monodepth2 [17] and Lite-Mono [59] blur object boundaries and lose fine details, whereas R-MSFM-GC [65] and PuriLight [4] oversmooth fine structures. ReForgeDepth, in contrast, preserves sharp boundaries and fine details. 

4.1. Implementation 

We evaluate ReForgeDepth in PyTorch on standard and cross-dataset benchmarks (Intel Core Ultra 7 270K CPU, NVIDIA RTX 5080, 48 GB RAM). Training uses AdamW [35] ( 10 −2 decay) and drop-path in GMD/DCGI: 60 epochs at 5 × 10 −4 (cosine learning rate schedule [34]) from scratch, or 30 at 1 × 10 −4 with ImageNet pretraining [6], then 30 at 5 × 10 −5 with a frozen Flex-Large teacher. 

4.2. Datasets 

KITTI. We train and evaluate on KITTI [13] with the standard Eigen split [7]: 39,180 monocular triplets 

{It−1, I t, I t+1 } for training, 4,424 validation images, and 697 test images. 

Cityscapes. Cityscapes [5] features crowded urban streets with abundant moving objects and serves as our benchmark for dynamic scenes. We use the 58,335 training and 1,525 test images of [10] at a resolution of 416 ×128 .

Make3D. To assess cross-dataset generalization, we apply the KITTI-trained model directly to Make3D [43], which provides 134 outdoor test images, without any fine-tuning.

## 5. Conclusion 

This paper reveals the mismatch between the training strategy and model capacity in lightweight self-supervised depth estimation. We propose “Signal Scaffolding”—a three-phase strategy that gradually adjusts the training sig-nals to approach the target task, thereby unlocking the latent representational capacity of the lightweight model without the need for additional dataset or parameters. In addition, we design ReForgeDepth, which reduces the number of pa-rameters while retaining detailed information and the re-ceptive field. On the KITTI and Cityscapes datasets, Re-ForgeDepth achieves the best balance of accuracy and ef-ficiency among the compared methods, and outperforms other models of the same scale by a large margin. Two limitations remain: photometric consistency de-grades depth around moving objects, and the lack of ground-truth depth caps accuracy. Future work will inte-grate motion-aware masking into Signal Scaffolding and explore stronger geometric priors in even more compact ar-chitectures, narrowing the supervised gap while preserving on-device efficiency. Generality of Signal Scaffolding Table 8 applies SS to four students under two teachers. SS improves every student–teacher pair, and the gain is inversely related to stu-dent size: Lite-Mono-tiny [59] improves by 0.004–0.005, Lite-Mono [59] by 0.002, and the 8.7M Lite-Mono-8M [59] by only 0.001. This phenomenon can be quantified through a transitivity analysis. Let A = Lite − M ono − tiny , B =

Lite −M ono −8M , and C = F lex −Large . If the teacher prior were the sole determinant, the gain would depend only on the teacher: since C improves B by 1%, it should like-wise improve A by roughly 1%; in practice, A improves by 4.6%—4.6 times the predicted value. Conversely, replacing student A’s teacher with the roughly 6% stronger C raises the gain only from 3.6% to 4.6% (+1.0%), whereas swap-ping the student from B to A under the same teacher C

raises the gain from 1% to 4.6% (+3.6%). Teacher capa-bility is thus a secondary factor; what dominates, instead, is likely the student’s recoverable capacity and its ability to exploit the training signal. Two independent observa-tions are consistent with this reading. First, even after SS, 

B still trails C by 5%, indicating that the teacher’s knowl-edge is not transferred wholesale— B absorbs only about one-sixth of the teacher-student gap, so the bottleneck lies in student capacity rather than the teacher prior. Second, un-der the same 1.5M parameter budget and the same teacher, ReForgeDepth improves far more than Flex-Nano [66], in-dicating that the recoverable capacity also depends on the architecture. These results confirm that SS, by reducing the capacity–training mismatch, lets small models better ex-ploit their existing expressive power.
