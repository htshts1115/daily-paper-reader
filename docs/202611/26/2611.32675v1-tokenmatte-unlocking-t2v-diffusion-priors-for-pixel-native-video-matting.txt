# Abstract 

High-resolution video matting requires accurate recovery of continuous alpha values, including fine hair, soft bound-aries, and motion blur, yet dense alpha supervision re-mains scarce. Pretrained text-to-video diffusion transform-ers (DiTs) offer rich spatio-temporal priors, whereas exist-ing generative video matting approaches typically route al-pha predictions through RGB-trained VAE latents, which can attenuate fine details and introduce additional decod-ing overhead. We present TokenMatte, which adapts a pre-trained video DiT into a single-pass, auxiliary-free RGB-to-alpha predictor with a pixel-native output interface. At its core is a single shared linear readout that maps each spatio-temporal token directly to its corresponding (t × p × p)

block of alpha pixels, predicting spatial details and tem-poral variations within the same local video block. Tiling these blocks produces the full-resolution alpha sequence without a convolutional decoder or learned upsampling. The predicted alpha is supervised directly in pixel space, while the ground-truth alpha never enters the VAE, elimi-nating target-side alpha encoding and output-side VAE de-coding. Leveraging the pretrained video prior, TokenMatte is trained using only existing public matting datasets, with-out additional data curation or auxiliary inputs at inference. Compared with a matched latent-output baseline, our pixel-native interface improves matting accuracy while achieving a 1.51 × inference speedup. TokenMatte establishes a new auxiliary-free state of the art on VideoMatte, YouTubeMatte, and V-HIM60. 

# 1. Introduction 

Video matting is a core component of modern video edit-ing, compositing, background replacement, and visual ef-fects. In the most practical setting, the user provides only an RGB video and expects a temporally coherent alpha se-quence at the original resolution, without trimaps, masks, point prompts, or first-frame annotations [1, 5, 23, 29]. This auxiliary-free setting minimizes user interaction and sim-plifies deployment, but it is also one of the most challeng-ing operating points for matting: the model must recover fine boundary transitions, hair structures, motion blur, trans-parent regions, and foreground semantics directly from the video without any additional cues [14, 23]. A central bottleneck is supervision. Unlike segmenta-tion masks, alpha mattes are continuous pixel-space quan-tities that encode fractional opacity. A mask can iden-tify foreground regions, but it does not directly supervise hair-level structures, semi-transparent boundaries, motion-blurred transitions, or soft foreground-background mix-tures [3]. Accurate alpha labels are therefore expensive to annotate at scale, especially for real high-resolution videos with long temporal variation [35, 37]. As a result, much of the publicly available video matting supervision is green-screen captured, composited, synthetic, pseudo-labeled, short, or limited in scale [12, 22, 32]. This data bottleneck is particularly severe for auxiliary-free matting, where no external mask or reference annotation is available at inference time. Recent video matting research has actively sought to overcome this bottleneck [8, 20, 36, 37]. One line of work improves matting by scaling or curating task-specific data, including large real-video or pseudo-labeled video mat-ting datasets, learned quality evaluators, and mask-to-matte pipelines [12, 20, 36, 37]. These methods have substan-tially advanced the field, but they also shift progress to-ward increasingly large self-curated data resources and ad-ditional guidance signals. Another line of work exploits generative video priors, showing that pretrained video gen-eration models can help bridge the gap between limited al-pha supervision and real-world appearance variation [8, 33]. However, supervising in this lossy latent space limits mat-ting performance because the VAE round-trip destroys fine-grained details (e.g., hair) and propagates reconstruction errors during training; even an oracle round-trip of the ground-truth matte through the VAE already loses fine de-tail, and this ceiling is largest on the most detail-heavy benchmarks (Fig. 1(a),(c)). In this work, we present TokenMatte, which takes a dif-ferent path to bypass the data bottleneck. Instead of scal-ing task-specific data or relying on auxiliary prompts, we directly adapt a well-pretrained video DiT into an RGB-to-alpha predictor. Publicly available alpha data, without any self-curated resources, is fully sufficient to teach this translation. To exploit this prior, existing methods typically follow standard diffusion models by applying supervision in the latent space [8, 20], specifically, compressing the ground-truth alpha through a video VAE and supervising the model in latent space. However, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a) and (c), even a simple VAE “round-trip” (compressing the ground-truth matte into the latent space and decoding it back) in-curs significant reconstruction error and information loss, revealing a strong empirical reconstruction ceiling for alpha mattes. Supervising the model within this lossy latent space inevitably propagates these VAE-induced errors back to the video backbone during training (Fig. 1 (a)), compounding the optimization difficulty and preventing the network from capturing true pixel-level boundaries. We show that the tar-get interfaces of existing generative methods are unneces-sarily indirect, and circumvent this limitation by directly supervising the model in pixel space (Fig. 1(b)). Specifi-cally, we leverage the DiT’s fixed patch-aligned token grid, where each spatio-temporal token corresponds to a fixed lo-cal region, and employ a lightweight readout module to map tokens directly to alpha pixels. This native pixel-space training enables pristine super-vision, preserves delicate boundaries, and drastically im-proves efficiency. By completely bypassing the expensive VAE-decoding stage during inference, we eliminate a ma-jor computational bottleneck, reducing per-clip latency by 21.51 × (Fig. 1(d)). Ultimately, this design clarifies the roles of the prior and the data: pretraining provides the spatio-temporal foundation, while public alpha data merely cali-brates the token-to-alpha translation, unlocking the DiT as an efficient, RGB-only, alpha-native predictor. In summary, our main contributions are as follows: • We present TokenMatte, which adapts a pretrained video DiT into an auxiliary-free matting model, while retaining a lightweight token-to-pixel output interface. • We show this translation needs only a single per-token lin-ear readout that decodes each spatio-temporal token into its (t × p × p) block of alpha pixels—no alpha encoding, latent denoising, or VAE decoding. • Our model sets a new auxiliary-free state of the art on VideoMatte, YouTubeMatte, and V-HIM60, and runs 1.51× faster than the latent interface baseline. 

# 2. Related Work 

## 2.1. Auxiliary-free video matting 

Video matting predicts, for every frame of an RGB video, an alpha matte that is accurate within each frame and stable over time. It is closely related to segmentation but harder: rather than a discrete label per pixel, matting estimates a continuous opacity that captures the soft transitions of hair, motion blur, and semi-transparent boundaries [10, 35], and unlike its image counterpart [19, 24, 25], it must also stay temporally coherent, since individually plausible frames can still flicker as a sequence [16, 17, 42]. To reduce this ill-posedness, most methods take an auxiliary input: a per-frame trimap, costly to annotate, or a single first-frame mask [35]. MaGGIe mattes human instances from binary mask tracks, MatAnyone propagates a target-assigned al-pha through memory, MatAnyone-2 adds a learned quality evaluator and a large self-curated training set, and Video-MaMa conditions a generative prior on first-frame masks from SAM2; all reintroduce annotation or tracking at infer-ence [12, 15, 20, 36, 41]. We instead focus on auxiliary-free matting, where the model sees only the RGB input and predicts alpha with no trimap, mask, prompt, or annotation. This is the most con-venient and deployable setting, and a long line of image and video methods pursue it directly [8, 14, 16, 17, 23]. Its central difficulty is data: dense alpha labels are far more costly than segmentation masks, and public matting supervision is largely composited, synthetic, or limited in scale [12, 22, 37], which is precisely the gap a pretrained generative prior can help fill. 

## 2.2. Generative priors for video matting 

Large pretrained generative models have emerged as pow-erful visual priors, capturing appearance, structure, and, for video, motion and temporal continuity from large-scale syn-thesis [2, 39, 43]. Early efforts built on latent-diffusion U-Nets [28], which reach high resolution only by repeatedly downsampling into convolutional feature maps; modern dif-fusion transformers instead scale a video into a flat lattice of spatio-temporal tokens, each bound to a fixed space-time patch [2, 31]. Riding this prior, dense prediction has been recast as generation: latent-diffusion priors have been adapted to depth, normals, image matting, and segmenta-tion masks [7, 9, 13, 34, 38], and for video matting GVM [8] casts matting as flow-matching in latent space while Video-MaMa [20] synthesizes alpha-matte latents from a video diffusion backbone. Across this line, however, the alpha target is reached only through the backbone’s VAE: it is encoded into an RGB-trained latent and supervised there, so an opacity field scored in pixel space must survive an 8x-compressed space optimized for natural images, eroding the sub-pixel hair and semi-transparent boundaries matting must recover while adding a decode pass at high resolu-tion [8, 18, 20, 40]. Building on the ViT patch paradigm, pixel-space generators like JiT [18] share DiT’s [26] per-patch token view but drop the VAE to read raw pixels di-rectly, an insight our readout extends to auxiliary-free, high-resolution video matting by mapping each video DiT token to its corresponding block of alpha pixels. 

# 3. Method 

Recent generative video matting reaches the alpha target through the backbone’s VAE: alpha is encoded into a la-tent, supervised there, and decoded back to pixels [8, 20]. This inherits two costs from RGB synthesis. The VAE is optimized to reconstruct natural RGB, not the continuous transmittance of an alpha matte, so encoding alpha through it compresses exactly the sub-pixel hair and soft boundaries matting must recover, capping accuracy before learning be-gins; and decoding back to pixels adds a pass whose cost is most severe at the high resolutions matting demands. We avoid both by never sending alpha through the VAE: we unlock the RGB prior of a pretrained video DiT for video matting and read the alpha field directly off its tokens, in one forward pass. Following Fig. 2 from left to right, we utilize the prior (Sec. 3.1), read alpha off the token grid (Sec. 3.2), and supervise it in pixel space (Sec. 3.3). We seek the alpha sequence α ∈ RT ×1×H×W from an RGB video x ∈ RT ×3×H×W , with no trimap, mask, prompt, or first-frame annotation.

## 3.2. Pixel Readout 

A correspondence the encoder already fixed. A DiT or-ganizes its representations on a regular spatio-temporal to-ken grid, where each token zijk corresponds to a fixed out-put region Ωijk through patch-aligned indexing. A token is thus a spatio-temporal carrier; its channels, pretrained to hold an RGB latent, can instead hold the opacity of that block. Reading alpha is then not a reconstruction but an inversion of this existing correspondence, with the target swapped from RGB to alpha. 

A single linear readout. After adaptation, each token is mapped to its block of alpha pixels by one shared linear projection: 

ˆAijk = reshape( W z ijk + b) , ˆAijk ∈ R t×p×p×1,

(2) where t×p×p is exactly the pixel block zijk encodes. Tiling all blocks over {Ωijk } yields the full-resolution alpha se-quence ˆα. The projection W is shared across all positions and contains no convolution, no learned upsampling, and no inter-token mixing. 

A minimal token-local readout. We intentionally choose the simplest token-local output interface to test whether an adapted video prior can expose alpha directly in pixel space without a task-specific decoder. Empirically, this minimal readout already achieves strong matting accuracy and sub-stantially outperforms the matched latent-output interface (Sec. 4.4). Our claim is therefore not that richer local read-outs can never help, but that a high-capacity generative de-coder is not required for accurate pixel-space alpha readout. 

Sequence-length-independent readout. The shared readout acts independently on each spatio-temporal token and introduces no recurrence, memory bank, or dependence on sequence length. Sequence-length handling is left to the video backbone and evaluated empirically in Sec. 4.4. 

## 3.3. Pixel-Space Supervision 

The readout in Eq. (2) produces alpha directly in pixel space, so the alpha target never enters the VAE. We super-vise it with a pixel-space objective only: 

L = ˆα − α 1, (3) There is no alpha VAE encoding, no latent flow-matching or denoising objective, and no VAE decoding pass. This is a structural departure from the latent interface of recent generative matting, where the ground-truth alpha is encoded by the video VAE and supervised on the al-pha latent, a decoded pixel loss serving only to refine the output. That interface is constrained in two task-specific ways. (i) Target compression. The VAE is optimized for RGB reconstruction; encoding the alpha target through it compresses the sub-pixel hair, semi-transparent boundaries, and motion-blurred edges matting must recover, capping at-tainable detail before learning begins. Our readout removes the bound at its source by never encoding alpha. Both costs vanish when alpha stays in pixel space. On the accuracy side, an oracle round-trip of ground-truth alpha through the frozen RGB-trained VAE already incurs MAD 3.50/1.22/0.54 on VideoMatte/V-HIM60/YouTubeMatte, losing fine hair structure before learning (Fig. 1). Thus, not encoding alpha preserves detail, while not decod-ing it reduces latency. 

# 4. Experiments 

## 4.1. Experimental Setup 

Backbone and adaptation. We build our model on a pre-trained video DiT (Wan2.1 1.3B [31]). The VAE encoder 

E remains frozen as the pretrained RGB input interface, while the full DiT backbone is fine-tuned for RGB-to-alpha prediction. The backbone runs in a single deterministic forward pass with the time embedding fixed to τ0 and no noise injected. The output interface remains deliberately lightweight: the only newly introduced spatial module is the shared linear token-to-pixel readout W .

Training objective and schedule. We supervise ˆα purely in pixel space with an ℓ1 loss. The model is trained on 21-frame clips at 720 × 1280 resolution for 30K steps using AdamW with bfloat16 precision, taking approximately 12 hours on a TPU v6e-16. We use the same training config-uration across all experiments and introduce no auxiliary trimap, mask, or segmentation supervision. 

Inference. At test time, we run full-sequence inference in a single forward pass, processing clips of up to 100 frames at 1080 p at once, with no windowing or inter-segment prop-agation. The effect of inference length beyond the 21-frame training context is evaluated in Sec. 4.4. 

Evaluation metrics. Following RVM [23] and GVM [8], we report MAD, MSE, Grad [27], Conn (connectivity), and dtSSD [6]; all metrics are lower-is-better. 

Comparison protocol. We focus on the auxiliary-free setting, where the model receives only RGB video with no trimap, mask, point prompt, or first-frame annotation at in-ference. We compare primarily against auxiliary-free meth-ods and report mask-guided methods for context. Video-MaMa [20] is included under its first-frame mask-guided protocol, where SAM2 propagates the initial mask across the video; it is therefore not directly comparable to our RGB-only setting. We follow the unified VideoMatte and YouTubeMatte evaluation protocol of MatAnyone2 [37]. 

## 4.2. Datasets 

Training data. Public alpha supervision remains limited in scale and diversity: existing video matting datasets are largely composited or green-screen captured and dominated by human portraits. We therefore adapt a pretrained video DiT using existing public alpha supervision rather than con-structing an additional task-specific corpus. Concretely, we use VideoMatte240K [22] as the primary video matte source, P3M-10K [16] for large-scale portrait fine-alpha supervision, and V-HIM2K5 [12] for multi-instance video matting; their statistics are summarized in Table 1. This dif-fers from recent data-scaling lines of work that construct ad-ditional task-specific resources—e.g. GVM [8] renders the synthetic SynHairMan set and pseudo-labels real video seg-mentation, VideoMaMa [20] builds the 50K-video pseudo-labeled MA-V dataset via a generative mask-to-matte la-beler, and MatAnyone 2 [37] curates the real-world VM-Real dataset ( 28 K clips, 2.4M frames) with a learned quality evaluator. TokenMatte instead uses only the public datasets listed above while leveraging pretrained video representa-tions for task adaptation.

## 4.3. Main Results 

Comparison with prior work. Tables 2 and 3 report re-sults on the three benchmarks. Across all of them our method sets a new auxiliary-free state of the art, and the margin widens precisely on the cases that stress real-world appearance and multi-instance structure. On the compos-ited VideoMatte benchmark we lead every auxiliary-free baseline on all four metrics—e.g. MAD 5.11 vs. 6.33 and Grad 4.34 vs. 8.04 for GVM, nearly halving the gradi-ent error—indicating substantially sharper boundaries. On YouTubeMatte the advantage is far larger: at MAD 1.21, MSE 0.25, and Grad 3.46 we not only top the auxiliary-free group but also outperform the mask-guided VideoMaMa (1.36/0.29/3.57) on all three commonly reported metrics, despite using no auxiliary input. These results are ob-tained using only the public training datasets described in Sec. 4.2, without constructing an additional task-specific matting corpus. The trend is most pronounced on V-HIM60 

(Table 3), where multiple interacting human instances are the main difficulty. Among auxiliary-free methods, Token-Matte achieves the best MAD and Conn on all three splits and the best Grad and dtSSD on Easy and Medium, with especially large gains in MAD on the hardest split (6.09 vs. 11.04 for GVM). On Hard, GVM obtains lower Grad (13.52 vs. 15.87) and dtSSD (4.11 vs. 4.40), while Token-Matte retains lower MAD (6.09 vs. 11.04) and Conn (11.42 vs. 13.16). Mask-guided methods are reported only for con-text under their stronger input conditions.

# 5. Conclusion 

We presented TokenMatte, an auxiliary-free video mat-ting framework that adapts a pretrained video DiT with a lightweight token-to-pixel output interface. A shared linear readout maps each adapted spatio-temporal token directly to its corresponding block of alpha pixels, keeping alpha supervision entirely in pixel space. This design avoids the information loss introduced by encoding continuous alpha through an RGB-trained VAE and eliminates the VAE de-coding stage at inference. Under the same backbone, train-ing data, and optimization setting, the pixel-native interface achieves better matting accuracy and 1.51 × faster inference
