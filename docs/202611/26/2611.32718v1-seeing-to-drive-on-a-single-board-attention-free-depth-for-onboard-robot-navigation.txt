# Seeing to Drive on a Single Board: Attention-Free Depth for Onboard Robot Navigation 

Itai Mizlish and Boaz Ben-Moshe 

Department of Computer Science, Ariel University, Ariel, Israel itai.mizlish@msmail.ariel.ac.il, benmo@g.ariel.ac.il 

Abstract —A small ground robot can carry one camera and a few watts of compute, but the depth foundation models that could turn that camera into a range sensor are vision transformers sized for GPUs. We bring one to a Raspberry Pi 5 with the Hailo-8L, an accelerator that runs every layer in integer arithmetic, attention included. Quantised for it, Depth Anything V2 loses large parts of the scene, and we measure why: at 449 tokens, 91 % of softmax weights fall below one int8 step while carrying 17–33 % of the attention mass. Our answer is to distil, not quantise. An attention-free ResNet-34 U-Net student of Depth Anything V2-Large (24.5 M parameters) runs at 27.1 FPS on the chip, its int8 output matches its float twin (Pearson 0.99993), and on held-out scenes of its simulated yard it is the most accurate depth network on the chip (AbsRel 0.269 against 0.354–0.530). On 1,000 stereo-verified points of a real outdoor drive it is not resolvably different from the vendor’s Depth Anything V2-Small build, while running faster (27.1 vs 21.6 FPS). Ranging obstacles by where they meet the floor needs only the camera height, so mapping, hybrid-A ∗

planning and SLAM run on the Pi’s own CPU. In real-time hardware-in-the-loop tests in a Gaussian-splat reconstruction of a real yard, the stack reaches the goal without contact in 7.9 ±1.1 of 10 scenes, against 9.2 ±0.4 with exact obstacles, and in 7.7 ±0.7 on its own pose with simulated wheel odometry (ten seeds each; 8.2 ±0.4 with the real Pi and Hailo-8L in the loop, five seeds). The source code, pre-trained models and data are available at https://github.com/itaim18/DANIEL. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

A rover that drives through a yard of chairs, cones, crates and people must know where things are. Range sensors answer that at a cost in money, power, mass and blind spots: a planar LiDAR misses a table top and a low curb, and a stereo camera adds a baseline, power and cost (ours carries one only as a reference, Figure 2). A single RGB camera sees all of these, costs little and emits nothing, but gives no distance directly. Monocular depth has changed character in the last three years. Networks trained on tens of millions of images, from MiDaS [1] to Depth Anything [2], Depth Anything V2 [3], Depth Pro [4] and Depth Anything 3 [5], now predict relative depth sharp and stable enough to drive with. They are vision transformers sized for data-centre GPUs: Depth Anything V2-Large has 335 M parameters and takes 175 ms per frame even on the RTX 5090 laptop GPU used in this work. A small robot has a few watts, and none of these models targets an integer-only accelerator. We ask how much of that capability survives on the cheapest serious edge platform available today, a Raspberry Pi 5 with the 13-TOPS Hailo-8L of the Raspberry Pi AI Kit [6], and what a rover can do with it. The answer is not a compilation exercise. The accelerator executes every layer in integer arith-metic, attention included, and both our int8 build of Depth Anything V2-Small and the vendor’s lose large parts of the scene. Even a faithful depth map is only relative, so it cannot by itself tell a planner that a chair is 1.2 m away. And a car needs a map, a planner, a controller and an estimate of its own position within the same board and control period.

Depth on small computers. FastDepth [11] runs a pruned MobileNet encoder-decoder on a Jetson; Lite-Mono [12] and SC-DepthV3 [13] learn from video without labels. FastDepth and SC-DepthV3 are the two depth models Hailo ships for the Hailo-8L, and both appear in our benchmark. AsyncMDE [14] amortises a foundation model over time on a Jetson AGX Orin. Navigating with predicted depth. MonoNav [18] fuses transformer depth into a reconstruction to fly a Crazyflie, with depth computed off-board. Learned policies [21], [22] and imperative planners [23], [24] map images to paths. We keep planning classical (hybrid A ∗ [25], pure pursuit [26]) so that every failure can be attributed to perception, planning or localisation, and run all of it on a board an order of magnitude cheaper than an AGX Orin. We distil Depth Anything V2-Large into a network with no attention, no LayerNorm and no softmax: a ResNet-34 encoder [27] with ImageNet initialisation and a U-Net de-coder [28] of convolution, batch normalisation, ReLU, bilinear 

2× upsampling and skip concatenation. It has 24.5 M param-eters, almost exactly Depth Anything V2-Small’s 24.8 M, so the comparison isolates architecture from capacity. It maps 288 ×512 RGB, the robot camera’s aspect, to relative disparity at full resolution. 

Training. The loss is the scale- and shift-invariant L1 of MiDaS plus four-scale gradient matching, so only the shape of depth is supervised. v1 is distilled from teacher labels on 7,230 simulated point-of-view frames and 720 real frames, with three scenes (crates, people, barrels) and the real robot video held out. v3 fine-tunes v1 for eight epochs on rendered ground-truth disparity for simulated frames (13,902 simulated + 2,024 real), which reduces the teacher-inherited range bias on simulated props from about −30 % to −7 %. v5, the deployed student, weights the loss four times higher on rows below the horizon, where obstacles meet the floor; it cuts spurious near-floor hits from 13.7 % to 10.2 %.

F ROM RELATIVE DEPTH TO A METRIC SCAN 

The student’s disparity is d = a/Z + b with unknown, drifting a and b. Instead of learning metric depth we take metre scale from the camera height h. For a level camera with focal length fy and principal row cy , a floor point at image row 

v > c y lies at 

Zfloor (v) = fy hv − cy

. (2) An obstacle meeting the floor at row vc is at Zfloor (vc),whatever the network’s scale. The network only has to say where in each column the floor ends, a question about the shape of the depth map. The image columns become a 69 ◦,4-metre “laser scan”. 

The scan. Per row, the 25th percentile of disparity over the central 80 % of columns models the floor f (v), made monotone from the horizon down. For a pixel, (d−b)/(f (v)−

b) = Zfloor /Z is scale-free; a pixel at least Hmin = 5 cm above the floor at its range exceeds 1/(1 − Hmin /h ), and the first run of five such rows in a column is the obstacle’s contact. A second, profile-free detector flags vertical faces, where the normalised disparity slope ∂v d (v − cy )/(d − b)

drops from ≈ 1 (floor) to ≈ 0 (face). Following the contact upward gives an obstacle’s height; only hits at least 6 cm tall are mapped, which cut phantom hits from 2,376 to 75 on six recorded episodes. Two fixes came from failure analysis: anchoring the floor profile at the horizon (a chair seat above the camera had become “floor”, making a tube at 0.68 m read 1.16 m), and a detector for floating overhangs such as seats and table tops (Figure 9). The same profile is a ruler for every pixel, Z = Zfloor (v)( f (v) − b)/(d − b), which gives a metric point cloud from one frame without metric training (Figure 8); pixels at depth discontinuities are dropped. Per pixel it is less robust than the contact scan (median error −2 to +8 % on obstacle pixels, but a long tail at object edges), so we use it for overhangs and visualisation, not for ranging.

A hybrid A ∗ [25] over rear-axle poses (0.1 m cells, 72 headings, ten 0.25 m primitives forward and backward) checks a three-circle footprint against the sensed map and a prior drivability map of the yard. Control and safety. Pure pursuit tracks one direction segment at a time. The car re-plans when the path is blocked, when it leaves the path, or when it is wedged (throttle without progress for 1.5 s, which also places a “bump” obstacle at the bumper). Every reverse passes the Traxxas ESC’s brake-then-neutral gate, which the simulator models. 

SLAM. About 400 floor features within 1.5 m are tracked with forward–backward Lucas–Kanade [29], lifted to metric floor points with the same camera-height geometry, and fit by a RANSAC 2D rigid transform. Wheel odometry (encoder and gyro) enters directly. A slip guard declares the car still when the rotation-compensated floor flow is below 2 px while the encoder claims at least 2 cm per frame. Offline on 50 recorded episodes (Table II) an IMU alone barely helps; the wheel encoder is what makes own-pose navigation work. In the closed loop, with encoder and gyro, the onboard estimate stays within 0.13 m of the truth at the median and 0.99 m at the 90th percentile over ten seeds (Figure 10). 

Real-time budget. With the deployed code on the real board the NPU takes 35.0 ms per frame and the CPU stages (scan, map, SLAM, navigation) 46.7 ms at the median (64.1 ms at p90), overlapped (Figure 11). On real 1280 ×720 video the standalone runner reaches 16.5 Hz with SLAM and 27 Hz without it, limited by the NPU. Over 1000 episodes and 28 h the NPU time did not drift (p99 35.9 ms) and the firmware’s throttle register stayed clear. X. D ISCUSSION 

Distillation, not better quantisation. The common as-sumption is that a foundation model reaches a small NPU through better quantisation. On the Hailo-8L it does not: int8 attention is structurally lossy at a few hundred tokens, for our build and the vendor’s. Distilling into a purely convolutional student turns a quantisation problem into a training problem, and quantisation cost then disappears from the error budget (int8 = float at r = 0.99993). The plain recipe (ResNet-34, U-Net decoder, scale-invariant loss, rendered-truth fine-tune) transfers to any integer-only accelerator. 

Metric navigation without metric depth. Ground-contact ranging asks the network only where each column’s floor ends; the camera height does the rest. It is immune to relative depth’s frame-to-frame scale drift, and its failure modes are geometric and nameable (overhangs, low bases, row-filling surfaces). 

Applications. One passive camera and a few-watt accelerator suit small unmanned ground vehicles where cost, power and size are limited, notably in the defence industry, where a camera also has the advantage of emitting nothing, unlike LiDAR or active depth sensors, that could reveal the vehicle. 

An evaluation that explains itself. Oracle, exact-obstacle, simulator-pose and own-pose arms decompose the gap to a perfect score into execution (0.6), perception (1.3) and localisation (0.2 clean, within seed noise; 0.9 reached), so Table IV’s interventions were not tuned blindly. XI. L IMITATIONS 

The suite is one reconstructed yard with static obstacles; the wheel encoder and gyro the own-pose results rely on are simulated, and on the real drive the onboard SLAM, tuned in simulation, beats dead reckoning from the commands ( −0.11 

vs −0.31 ) but not the vision SLAM baselines; the student’s metric range on real ground is not calibrated and the camera pitch is measured (3.8 ◦) but not applied onboard; and the Pi’s camera path, power draw and an autonomous drive of the real car remain to be measured (Section IX is a manual drive). The real results come from one drive, one route and one lighting condition, without ground truth. Obstacle bases below 5 cm are invisible to the scan, and the planner commits to routes through space the camera has not seen. Absolute HITL scores are specific to the simulator build (a restored build scores the released code 1.4 goals per seed lower); all comparisons were within one build. XII. C ONCLUSION 

A foundation-quality monocular depth network can run on a Raspberry Pi 5 with a Hailo-8L, but not as a transformer. Distilled into an attention-free student it runs at 27 FPS, its int8 output equals its float output, and on held-out scenes of its simulated yard it is the most accurate depth network on the chip; on stereo-verified real outdoor points it is not resolvably different from the vendor’s Depth Anything V2-Small. Ranged by ground-contact geometry, its relative depth becomes a metric scan without metric training, and the Pi itself maps, plans, follows and localises on it. In a real-time hardware-in-the-loop run through a reconstructed yard, the system reaches the goal without contact in 7.9 ±1.1 of 10 scenes with simulator pose, 7.7 ±0.7 on its own pose and 8.2 ±0.4 with the real board in the loop (five seeds), where the same stack with perfect obstacles reaches 9.2 ±0.4. The measured gap points at two concrete next steps: perception of low structure and exploration that looks before it commits. ACKNOWLEDGMENT
