Abstract
Existing depth-from-focus methods can struggle with weak
focus cues, new cameras, or fewer focal slices. We present
FocusAnchor, a recurrent model that uses focus-volume
lookups to refine metric depth and repeatedly aligns a rel-
ative depth prior from a frozen foundation model to the
current depth estimate.
The aligned prior guides subse-
quent refinement in a feedback loop, carrying metric scale
recovered from textured regions into regions where focus
cues are weak while preserving scene structure. To sup-
port transfer across cameras, we synthesize training stacks
with randomized camera parameters and focus distances.
However, stack synthesis blurs existing image noise differ-
ently across slices, creating artificial depth cues even on
textureless surfaces. We add independent per-slice noise to
suppress these cues. We also introduce variable-stack train-
ing to improve robustness with fewer focal slices and sup-
port more practical mobile capture. Compared with FOSSA
ViT-B, FocusAnchor reduces AbsRel by 36% on NYU Depth
V2 and by 9% on DDFF-12 after fine-tuning.
In zero-
shot evaluation, FocusAnchor and its variable-stack vari-
ant, FocusAnchor-flex, achieve the two lowest AbsRel val-
ues on all five benchmarks. On ZEDD test, FocusAnchor
achieves 9% lower AbsRel than the best competing method
FOSSA ViT-B, while FocusAnchor-flex achieves lower Ab-
sRel with 40% fewer focal slices on ZEDD validation.

1. Introduction
Metric depth estimation predicts the distance from a camera
to visible surfaces in meters. The metric depths are useful
because they enable applications such as augmented reality
to correctly scale and position virtual objects [7], and help
robots judge distances for obstacle avoidance and manipu-
lation [22]. Camera focus can provide a metric cue because
surfaces appear sharpest when focused at their distance.
Capturing the same scene at several known focus distances
produces a focal stack, with each image called a focal slice.
Depth-from-focus (DFF) models [8, 17, 27, 29, 30] estimate
metric depth from sharpness changes across slices and their
1

known focus distances. However, changes in sharpness are
not equally informative across the image. Textured regions
often provide informative focus cues, while smooth walls
and other textureless surfaces can look similar across slices,
making their depth difficult to determine.
Relative-depth foundation models such as Depth Any-
thing V2 [32] can provide complementary scene structure
from a single image, including where focus cues are weak,
but their predictions lack metric scale. HybridDepth [8]
aligns a monocular depth estimate with a focus-based esti-
mate and refines the result. FOSSA [34] adapts a pretrained
depth model to focal stacks, demonstrating zero-shot trans-
fer to new cameras and scenes.
Despite these advances, three limitations remain. First,
metric depth can remain inaccurate in regions with weak fo-
cus cues, particularly textureless surfaces. Second, achiev-
ing strong generalization with limited task-specific training
data remains challenging. FOSSA, for example, is trained
on stacks rendered from 373k synthetic images. Third, ex-
isting methods often lose accuracy with fewer focal slices,
limiting their use when capture time or scene motion re-
stricts stack size. On an iPhone 18 Pro, we measure about
0.58 s per slice and 2.9 s for five slices (Sec. 4.4).
We introduce FocusAnchor, a recurrent DFF model built
around a feedback loop between depth refinement and prior
alignment (Fig. 1).
Given a focal stack, its focus dis-
tances, and an all-in-focus image, FocusAnchor maps a
frozen foundation model’s relative depth prediction onto
the focus range. A recurrent network refines this estimate
through lookups in a focus volume, which stores learned
scores across focal slices at each pixel. After initial scale
adjustment, each refinement step fits the frozen prediction
to the current estimate using a confidence-weighted local
scale and shift. The aligned prior and focus evidence guide
the next update, carrying metric scale recovered from tex-
tured regions into regions where focus cues are weak.
To support cross-camera transfer, we train the refinement
network under varied acquisition conditions using NYU
Depth V2 images. Following FOSSA, we synthesize fo-
cal stacks with randomized camera parameters and focus
distances. However, during the synthesis process, blurring
a captured RGB image also smooths the sensor noise al-
ready present. At each pixel, this noise is smoothed least
in the slice focused at the surface’s depth, creating an ar-
tificial depth cue even on textureless surfaces.
A model
can therefore learn to locate the in-focus slice from residual
noise rather than scene detail. This shortcut does not reflect
real capture, where sensor noise is introduced separately in
each exposure rather than blurred with the scene. We there-
fore add independent random noise to each rendered slice
to suppress this shortcut. To support different capture bud-
gets, we also train a variant with two to five focal slices,
FocusAnchor-flex.
FocusAnchor achieves strong accuracy on held-out
NYU Depth V2 images and on DDFF-12 after fine-
tuning.
Without target-dataset fine-tuning, FocusAnchor
and FocusAnchor-flex achieve the two lowest AbsRel val-
ues across five benchmarks, including real captured fo-
cal stacks in ZEDD, where FocusAnchor reduces AbsRel
by about 9% relative to FOSSA ViT-B on the public test
set. With variable-stack training, FocusAnchor-flex main-
tains strong performance across stack sizes.
On ZEDD
validation, FocusAnchor-flex achieves 73% lower AbsRel
than FOSSA ViT-B when both use two slices and achieves
lower AbsRel with three slices than FOSSA ViT-B with
five. These results suggest potential for mobile capture with
fewer exposures.
Our main contributions are:
• A recurrent DFF architecture that updates depth in units
of focus planes and repeatedly aligns a frozen depth prior
to the current estimate to guide refinement.
• A training recipe combining randomized camera param-
eters and independent per-slice noise to suppress artifi-
cial depth cues from synthetic stack rendering and sup-
port transfer to real captured stacks.
• Robustness across focal-stack sizes through variable-
stack training that enables FocusAnchor-flex to support
two to five slices and outperforms competing methods
with two or three slices on ZEDD.
• State-of-the-art zero-shot AbsRel on ZEDD, Infinigen
Defocus, iBims, HAMMER, and ARKitScenes.
2. Related Work
Depth from focus. Early DFF methods estimate depth
using handcrafted sharpness measures across focal set-
tings [25]. DDFF [9] introduced end-to-end DFF and the
DDFF-12 light-field benchmark, while DefocusNet [17]
narrowed the synthetic-to-real gap with a defocus-aware ar-
chitecture and the FoD500 dataset. DFV [30] uses a dif-
ferential focus volume from neighboring-slice feature dif-
ferences and estimates a distribution over focus distances,
while DualFocus [29] adds spatial and focal variational con-
straints to distinguish depth boundaries from texture arti-
facts and to encourage physically consistent training. We
adapt DFV’s focus volume for recurrent depth refinement.
Single-image depth estimation. Relative depth models
predict depth only up to an unknown scale and shift. Mi-
DaS [21] and Depth Anything [31] generalize across do-
mains through diverse training data, and Depth Anything
V2 [32] adds robustness and fine detail. Marigold [13] re-
purposes a pretrained diffusion model for zero-shot depth.
Metric depth models predict depth in meters from learned
scene priors, often with known or estimated intrinsics.
ZoeDepth [4] adds metric heads to relative-depth training.
Metric3D, Metric3D v2 and DA3 Metric [10, 15, 33] pre-
2


dict depth in a canonical camera space and rescale predic-
tions using the input focal length. UniDepth and UniDepth
V2 [18, 19] predict camera geometry alongside depth, and
Depth Pro [5] estimates the focal length, enabling metric
prediction without supplied intrinsics. However, the single-
image models we evaluated still incur large metric errors on
ZEDD (Tab. 3). We instead anchor a relative depth prior
using focus observations at known distances.
Fusing focus with single-image priors. HybridDepth [8]
aligns a relative single-image depth map to a DFF estimate
with a global scale and shift, then applies learned pixel-
wise scale corrections. FOSSA [34] integrates focus cues
into a pretrained metric depth transformer through attention
across slices and an embedding of each focus distance. In
stereo, MonSter [6] aligns a monocular prior inside a re-
current loop through learned per-pixel shift updates. We
instead align the prior in closed form with a local scale and
shift before every step, and combine it with focus-volume
lookups in units of focus planes.
Recurrent refinement with depth priors. RAFT [26] and
RAFT-Stereo [16] iteratively refine optical flow and dispar-
ity, respectively, using ConvGRUs guided by features re-
trieved from correlation volumes. DEFOM-Stereo [11] ini-
tializes disparity from Depth Anything V2 and applies scale
updates before additive refinement.Stereo Anywhere [2]
combines correlation volumes derived from stereo image
features and monocular depth predictions.
We extend
DEFOM-Stereo’s recurrent backbone with focus-volume
lookups and depth updates relative to known focus planes.

introduce focus-plane lookups and updates (Sec. 3.3), re-
peated prior alignment (Sec. 3.4), and per-slice noise and
variable-stack training (Sec. 3.6).

We take the focus volume from DFV [30] (Sec. 3.2). The
focus distances are known in meters, so the volume carries
metric depth. Textureless surfaces can have flat scores.
Depth prior. Depth Anything V2 [32] covers these sur-
faces. From one image, it predicts a relative inverse depth z
that captures the structure of the scene but matches the true
inverse depth only up to an unknown scale and shift.
RAFT-based update loop. We combine the volume with
the prior through the update loop of RAFT-Stereo [16, 26],
which refines an estimate at 1/4 resolution over several iter-
ations. In each iteration, a lookup reads the volume around
the current estimate. A ConvGRU takes the lookup, the es-
timate and fixed context features, and predicts a correction.
GRU weights are shared within each stage, and a learned
convex upsampling gives the full-resolution output. From
DEFOM-Stereo [11] we keep two ideas: the loop starts
from the single-image prior, and its first iterations rescale
the estimate before the rest add corrections.

3.3. Recurrent Refinement
FocusAnchor uses focus-volume lookups and a three-level
ConvGRU at 1/4, 1/8 and 1/16 resolution (Fig. 2, bot-
tom).
The scale stage corrects large initial scale errors
through multiplicative updates, while the step stage then re-
fines each pixel with bounded updates in focus-plane coor-
dinates. Each stage has its own GRU weights, shared by its
iterations, and the hidden state carries over.
Plane index u. The focus planes sit at ρi = d1/di, from
ρ1 = 1 down to ρN. We estimate the normalized inverse
depth x = d1/D, so the nearest focus plane is at x = 1
and the metric depth is D = d1/x. We also express x as
a plane index u = gρ(x), which counts focus planes from
the nearest one. A pixel on the second plane has u = 2,
and a pixel halfway between the second and third planes in
inverse depth has u = 2.5. The map gρ is piecewise linear
with gρ(ρi) = i and extends linearly beyond the end planes.
Stereo measures steps in pixels of disparity. We measure
them in planes, so a step of 1 moves the estimate to the next
focus plane for every camera and focus schedule.
Initialization x0. The first estimate takes its shape from the
prior and its scale from the focus planes. We min-max nor-
malize z to ˆz ∈[0, 1] and initialize x0 = ρN + (1 −ρN) ˆz.
The nearest predicted surface thus lands on the nearest fo-
cus plane, and the farthest on the farthest plane. Our focus
distances are in meters, so x0 starts at a metric scale. This
scale is only approximate, because a scene rarely spans ex-
actly the focus range.

3.4. Prior Alignment
In the loop described so far, the prior enters only through
x0 and the context. Where the volume is flat, as on a blank
wall, the GRU can thus drift away from the prior’s structure.
We therefore align ˆz to the estimate before every step-stage
iteration, and weight each pixel by how much the estimate
can be trusted (Fig. 2, bottom). The scale stage needs no
alignment, since x0 is itself an affine map of ˆz.

3.6. Training
We train FocusAnchor on NYU Depth V2 [24], rendering
focal stacks with FOSSA’s render [34]. Camera parameters
and focus distances vary per image to support cross-camera
transfer (Suppl. Sec. 7). FocusAnchor-flex is the same ar-
chitecture trained with variable stacks (Sec. 4.4). We ad-
dress the following two sources of artificial depth cues.
Focus planes. Prior focal-stack methods [8, 29] place the
nearest and farthest planes at each image’s minimum and
maximum ground-truth depth, so the network only places
pixels between two known depths (Suppl. Fig. 4). Follow-
ing FOSSA, 80% of our stacks instead use random focus
distances, with the nearest plane at 0.6 to 1 m and the far-
thest 7 to 15 times farther.
Per-slice noise. The renderer blurs one sharp photo once per
focus distance, so the photo’s sensor noise stays sharpest in
the slice that is in focus. On a textureless surface, such as a
white wall, this noise is the only detail, so its sharpest slice
hints at the depth. A real camera exposes every slice sepa-
rately, with new noise each time, so we add fresh Gaussian
noise to every rendered slice. If textureless pixels carried
no depth hint, their sharpest slice would be the one focused
nearest the true depth 20% of the time, one in five. In our
renders this happens 62% of the time, and the added noise
lowers it to 27% (Suppl. Sec. 7).
4. Experiments
We evaluate three aspects of FocusAnchor: (i) generaliza-
tion across scene and camera domains, (ii) sensitivity to
focal-stack size, and (iii) the contributions of prior align-
ment and iterative refinement. Trained only on NYU Depth
V2, FocusAnchor and FocusAnchor-flex achieve the two
lowest AbsRel values on all five zero-shot benchmarks
(Tab. 3). FocusAnchor-flex remains accurate with as few
as two slices, which suggests practical utility when a long
focal stack cannot be captured.
4.1. Experimental Setup
Datasets. Our evaluation covers captured,
light-field-
refocused, and synthetic focal stacks.
• NYU Depth V2 [24] contains indoor RGB-D images,
with 24,231 training and 654 test images in our split. We
evaluate on the test images following prior work [8, 29],
with focus planes spanning each image’s ground-truth
depth range.
• DDFF-12 [9] provides focal stacks refocused from light-
field images of 12 indoor scenes. We use five-slice inputs
and follow prior protocols [9, 30] for comparison.
• ZEDD [34] provides focal stacks captured with a full-
frame camera and a 50 mm lens, with dense LiDAR
ground-truth. We evaluate its validation and test splits,
each containing 50 scenes. We follow FOSSA evaluation
and test protocols. Inputs are resized and center-cropped
to 480 × 640, with valid ground-truth depths restricted to
[0.01, 1000] m.
• Infinigen Defocus [34] is a synthetic benchmark con-
taining 200 indoor scenes generated with Infinigen In-
doors [20] and rendered with defocus in Blender Cy-
cles. Following FOSSA, we use five images per scene
at F/1.4, with focus distances of 0.8, 1.7, 3.0, 4.7, and
8.0 m, retaining ground-truth depths in [0.01, 1000] m.
For FocusAnchor, RGB images and depth maps are re-
sized to 480 × 640 using Lanczos and nearest-neighbor
interpolation, respectively.
• iBims, HAMMER, and ARKitScenes provide RGB-D
images without focal stacks. For zero-shot evaluation,
we synthesize focal stacks from their RGB images and
ground-truth depth maps using the rendering procedure
from FOSSA’s evaluation [34]. For ARKitScenes, we use
5,594 validation images downsampled to 480 × 640.
Metrics. Following prior work [8, 29, 34], we report the
AbsRel, SqRel, RMSE, logRMSE and threshold accuracy
δt, the fraction of pixels with max( ˆdp/dp, dp/ ˆdp) < t. δ1,
δ2 and δ3 denote t = 1.25, 1.252 and 1.253. For DDFF-12,
the metrics are computed on disparity, and we also report
MSE. For the ZEDD test split, we use the official evaluation
server.
Implementation details. We train FocusAnchor on the data
of Sec. 3.6 for 60k steps on 384×512 crops with five slices,
using AdamW with a learning rate of 2 × 10−4, a one-
cycle schedule, and a batch of 8 on two RTX 4090 GPUs.
FocusAnchor-flex is trained likewise with 2–5 slices per
batch, then for 20k more steps at a learning rate of 5×10−5.
Following FOSSA [34], we use the ZEDD validation split
during development: FocusAnchor uses its final 60k-step
checkpoint, while FocusAnchor-flex uses a checkpoint se-
lected on this split. Each model is evaluated using a sin-
gle fixed checkpoint across all zero-shot benchmarks. For
comparison with HybridDepth and DualFocus on NYU, we
separately train FocusAnchor using their rendering proto-
col: the NYU camera, focus planes spanning each image’s
ground-truth depth range (Suppl. Fig. 4, top), and no per-
slice noise augmentation. For DDFF-12, we fine-tune on its
training split. FocusAnchor has 24.6M trainable parameters
plus the frozen Depth Anything V2 Large model. Five-slice
480 × 640 inference takes 92 ms on an RTX 4090.

5. Conclusion
FocusAnchor uses an aligned foundation-model depth prior
to carry metric scale recovered from textured regions into
regions where focus cues are weak.
FocusAnchor and
FocusAnchor-flex achieve competitive zero-shot perfor-
mance, and ablations demonstrate the benefits of repeat-
8


edly aligning the prior with focus-derived depth estimates
throughout refinement. Our variable-stack training supports
shorter focal stacks, but does not explicitly account for cam-
era or scene motion during acquisition. Future work could
incorporate these effects into model design and training to
improve robustness in handheld capture.

Noise test. A pixel is textureless when the Sobel gradient
magnitude of the gray all-in-focus image, averaged over a
7 × 7 window, is below 0.0265 for intensities in [0, 1]. This
covers 21% of the valid pixels of the training render. The
sharpest slice of a pixel is the one with the largest absolute
Laplacian, averaged over 3 × 3. The test passes when this
slice is the one focused nearest to the ground truth in in-
verse depth. We average the pass rate per image over 300
NYU training images. Without per-slice noise, textureless
pixels pass 62% of the time, against 20% by chance with
five slices. With the noise, they pass 27%. This likely stays
above chance because some of these pixels hold faint real
texture, which is a true focus cue. Textureless pixels in real
ZEDD stacks pass the same test 41% of the time.

16 iterations account for 41% of the 92 ms forward pass.
The frozen DAv2 ViT-L backbone, the slice encoder, and
the focus volume account for the remaining 54 ms. This
explains why halving the iteration count saves about one
fifth of the total time.

9. Focal-Stack Capture Time on a Phone
Table 7 breaks down the time to capture a focal stack on a
phone. Our iOS app moves the lens to five evenly spaced
positions across its focus range. At each position, it waits
until the lens has settled and then requests a photo. Photo
capture takes 85% of each slice, and the lens move takes the
remaining 15%. Within capture, the shutter fires about 0.1 s
after the request, and the photo is delivered about 0.39 s
later. Because every slice costs about the same, stack time
grows linearly with the number of slices. Each five-slice
stack equals the sum of its slices to within 2 ms. We did not
time two-slice stacks directly. Summing the nearest and far-
thest slices of each run gives an estimate of 1.16 ± 0.02 s,
60% less than a five-slice stack.

11. Slice Choice in the Stack-Size Study
ZEDD provides nine focus settings per scene. Table 4 uses
settings {1, 9}, {1, 5, 9}, {1, 4, 6, 9} and {1, 3, 5, 7, 9} for
two to five slices, so every stack spans 0.82 to 8.10 m. The
all-in-focus image is fused from the selected slices only.
FOSSA [34] reports a stack-size study on the same split
without stating which settings it uses. Its δ1.25 with five and
four slices (0.919 and 0.865, read from its Fig. 5c) agrees
with our runs of its released ViT-B model (0.919 and 0.857).
Its values with two and three slices (0.702 and 0.888) are
higher than ours (0.421 and 0.680), so the difference likely
lies in the slice choice. FocusAnchor-flex (0.855, 0.899,
0.876 and 0.920 for two to five slices) is above FOSSA’s
reported values at every size. Four slices are the only case
that cannot be evenly spaced among the nine settings, and
both FocusAnchor-flex and FOSSA’s own study dip there.
