## Abstract 

Promptable segmenters answer what a thing is. Dense prediction tasks also ask class-agnostic questions, such as what stands out or hides, how light is blocked, transmit-ted or reflected, and where structure lies. Robots, surveil-lance systems and image editors build on their answers. We study promptable concept maps (PCM), which extend SAM 3’s promptable concept segmentation from noun in-stances to maps of concepts named by short noun phrases such as “transparent object”, with “none” as an answer. PCM-Bench groups the standard test sets of seven such tasks by the question they ask. On it, we find that SAM 3 sees more than it detects. Its semantic path responds to many of these concepts, yet a low image-level presence score can block every detection of its instance path. We present UNVEIL, which trains a copy of SAM 3’s semantic path for these maps, with LoRA on copies of its last blocks. A separate presence decoder learns from verified negatives whether a concept occurs. With one text phrase per query, UNVEIL maps region concepts such as salient and camou-flaged objects, shadows, transparent objects and mirrors. The same weights map thin structures such as edges and

On each of the seven tasks, UNVEIL reaches the highest score among the compared promptable models, including SAM 3’s offi-cial fine-tuning and pipelines built on multimodal LLMs. Code is available at https://github.com/yuhuan-wu/UNVEIL .

## 1. Introduction 

Promptable segmenters let a vision system name what it wants in a short phrase and get its mask back. Vision agents already call them as tools [13, 127], and the phrases they answer name things, such as objects, parts and stuff. Many dense prediction tasks ask other questions of every pixel. Which object stands out [92], and which one hides in its sur-roundings [16]? Where does a shadow fall [88], and where are glass [102] and mirrors [112]? Where do the edges [2] and the skeleton [83] of a shape run? These questions are class-agnostic, since a salient or camouflaged object can belong to any category and a shadow or an edge belongs to none. Each question has its own benchmarks and spe-cialist models, and each answer is a map. For an absent concept, the right answer is “none”. SAM 3 [6] introduced promptable concept segmenta-tion, which returns every instance of a noun phrase. Uni-fied dense models answer several of the questions above with one network. Each specifies its task by a learned task prompt [59, 67], an image–mask example [128] or a task head [56]. Other systems pass harder queries through a mul-timodal language model before SAM 3 [130]. The dense questions call for SAM 3’s interface at the level of maps, with one noun phrase in, such as “shadow” or “transparent object”, and one map or “none” out. We refer to this set-ting as promptable concept maps (PCM). For analysis, we group concepts by the question they ask of a region into four kinds, Identity, Light, Context and Structure. PCM-Bench is a unified evaluation protocol on the official test splits of seven tasks and on LVIS categories left out of training [21]. Since SAM 3 already takes a phrase as its prompt, we first run it on PCM-Bench. On many prompts beyond cat-egories, it returns nothing (Fig. 1a). We find that SAM 3 carries more of these concepts than its detections show. Its instance path multiplies every detection score by an image-level presence score, and its semantic path predicts a map before this score is applied. SAM 3 accepts Identity and Light prompts on most images and Context and Structure prompts on almost none (Fig. 2). There the presence score is suppressive, yet the semantic path still holds spatial ev-idence for the prompt. Setting the presence score to one, and changing nothing else, restores much of this evidence in the instance path on every Context and Structure set. We call this suppression the presence veto. It also depends on wording, since SAM 3 accepts “glass” on most Trans10K test images and “see-through object” on hardly any of them. We build UNVEIL, short for Uncovering Non-detected Visual Evidence In Latents, on this finding (Fig. 3). Where a concept lies and whether it occurs are separate questions, and UNVEIL answers them with separate parts. It reads the maps from SAM 3’s semantic path and trains this path in a parallel branch of LoRA-tuned copies of SAM 3’s last blocks. A presence decoder learns from verified negatives whether a concept occurs, behind a stop-gradient that keeps its loss away from the maps. Task concepts and category names share one text interface, and the model still segments LVIS categories left out of training. Run next to SAM 3, UNVEIL leaves SAM 3’s weights and outputs unchanged. Salient and camouflaged objects follow two labeling con-ventions, one concept at a time or a partition of the image. For this pair, a learned convention vector per prompt selects the partition convention of USC12K [135], and a joint query 

splits a scene between the two concepts. On PCM-Bench, UNVEIL scores highest on every task among the compared promptable models (Tab. 3), which range from frozen SAM 3 and its official fine-tuning to SAM 3 Agent and ConceptSeg-R1 [130]. Its seven-task mean is 90.5, against 82.6 for the next best of them. SAM 3’s official fine-tuning trains its instance path, and it stays below UNVEIL on almost every test set, with all its parameters or with our budget. The choice of path matters more than the number of trained parameters. In summary, our main contributions include: • The presence veto. Across the Context and Structure sets, SAM 3’s suppressive presence scores coexist with usable spatial predictions of its semantic path. • UNVEIL. Reading the maps from SAM 3’s semantic path in a parallel branch, and learning presence apart from them, turns SAM 3 into one promptable model for re-gions, thin structures and categories. It scores highest among the compared promptable models on each of the seven tasks, and convention vectors extend it to the salient and camouflaged pair. • PCM-Bench. A unified evaluation protocol for seven class-agnostic tasks on their standard test sets, with a re-jection split, a scorecard and a released toolkit. 

## 2. Related Work 

Specialist and unified dense models. Adapting SAM. Adapters and U-Net-style decoders tune SAM [40], SAM 2 [74] and SAM 3 for camouflage, shad-ows and related tasks [9, 10, 105, 107], and SAM without adaptation struggles in concealed scenes [35]. Both SAM 3 adaptations [10, 105] keep its image encoder and replace the text prompt with a decoder for each task. LoRA [31] learns low-rank updates that merge into weights, and ladder side-tuning [85] trains a side network next to a frozen backbone. Fine-tuning can distort pretrained features [41], and weight ensembling [98], rehearsal [78] and distillation [51] limit this. We add LoRA to copies of the last encoder blocks, compare training paths in SAM 3 and replay LVIS masks. 

Text-promptable segmentation. CLIPSeg [66] predicts a dense map from a text or image prompt. Ground-ing DINO [58] detects open-set phrases, and Grounded SAM [76] turns its boxes into masks. GRES [55] and GSVA [101] let a referring expression match several tar-gets or none. UniLSeg [63] segments at many granular-ities from language instructions, salient objects included. SAM 3 [6] returns every instance of a noun phrase and adds a presence token that separates recognition from lo-calization. On counterfactually edited images, CAFE [52] finds that SAM 3 and other models often return accurate masks for misleading prompts and fail to reject invalid concepts. The presence veto concerns the opposite error, where SAM 3’s presence score rejects present concepts that its semantic path localizes, and setting it to one recovers them. ConceptSeg-R1 [130] places a multimodal language model in front of frozen SAM 3. DCR-SAM [53] and Ac-tiveSAM [87] adapt SAM 3 to many-class semantic seg-mentation. SegEarth-OV3 [45] fuses SAM 3’s semantic and instance heads without training and drops absent classes of remote-sensing images by the presence score. UNVEIL trains the semantic path for concepts beyond categories and thin structures, and learns presence from verified negatives. 

## 3. Concept Maps and the Presence Veto 

3.1. Promptable Concept Maps 

A query pairs an image with a short noun phrase t, such as “mirror” or “edges”, in the form of SAM 3’s prompts. One model answers the queries of every task. The answer is the map of the concept that t names, or “none” if the image does not show it. The map marks a region, as for a shadow, or a thin structure, as for edges. Promptable concept seg-mentation [6] returns the instances of a noun phrase, and referring segmentation [55, 101] the instances that an ex-pression describes. Open-vocabulary semantic segmenta-tion [45, 87] labels pixels with categories, and unified dense models [67, 128] specify each task by a task token or a visual prompt. Categories are one kind of concept among several in PCM. PCM-Bench queries each test set with the phrase of its training split, tests rewordings for robustness and scores “none” where negatives are verified. Each test set keeps its standard metric. Shadows use the balanced er-ror rate (BER), glass and mirrors IoU, and salient and cam-ouflaged objects the S-measure Sα [15]. Edges and skele-tons use ODS, the F-measure at the best threshold over a test set. 3Concepts differ in what they ask of a region. Identity 

asks what a thing is, as for objects, parts and stuff. Light 

asks how light meets a region, as for shadows, glass and mirrors. Context asks how a region relates to its scene or viewer, as for salient and camouflaged objects. Struc-ture asks where the geometry of a shape lies, as for edges and skeletons. PCM-Bench covers Identity with LVIS cate-gories and the other kinds with seven tasks. 

3.2. Two output paths in SAM 3 

SAM 3 [6] encodes a 1008 × 1008 image with a 32-block Perception Encoder ViT [4] and a noun-phrase prompt with a text encoder. A fusion encoder lets image tokens attend to prompt tokens and yields a prompt-conditioned memory M

on a 72 × 72 grid, which two heads decode. The instance path is a DETR-style decoder [5] with 200 object queries and one presence token. Query i predicts a box, a mask and a detection logit di. The presence to-ken predicts one logit p for the whole image. With σ the sigmoid, a detection scores si = σ(di) σ(p), and SAM 3 keeps the detections with si ≥ 0.5, so a low σ(p) sup-presses every detection. To score this path as a map, we take per pixel the highest si times the mask over the detec-tions with si ≥ 0.05 , the instance map . The semantic path 

attends from M to the prompt once more. A small pixel decoder and a 1 × 1 convolution then give a logit map S of 

288 × 288 pixels. SAM 3 pairs S with the same presence score for semantic segmentation [6], and we read S before this score is applied. 

3.3. Where the veto falls 

We run frozen SAM 3 on every image of the 15 test sets of PCM-Bench and on Identity concepts from LVIS [21], ADE20K [132] and SA-Co/Gold [6]. The acceptance rate is the share of images with a detection si ≥ 0.5. Its median is 0.99 for Identity, 0.91 for Light, 0.02 for Context and 0.00 for Structure (Fig. 2), so the veto depends on the kind of concept. SAM 3.1 has medians of 0.99, 0.92, 0.02 and 0.00, and across the 15 test sets its acceptance rates follow those of SAM 3 with a Spearman correlation of 0.99. We examine the kinds and wordings where acceptance collapses. On COD10K, SAM 3 accepts “camouflaged object” on 0.02 of the images, so its presence score suppresses al-most every detection. Its instance map reaches an IoU of 0.010. The semantic path holds spatial evidence for the same prompt and reaches 0.473. We score these maps by IoU after binarizing them at 0.5, since an empty map still re-ceives an S-measure near 0.5. A rejected image then has an empty instance map. SAM 3 itself sets the presence score to one when the target is known to be present, as for vi-sual prompts on a single frame or for counting [6]. We set σ(p) = 1 and change nothing else, which lifts the in-stance path to 0.469. Structure shows the same pattern on

## 4. UNVEIL 

UNVEIL trains copies of SAM 3’s fusion encoder, semantic path, presence decoder and last blocks for PCM (Fig. 3), and each task is a text prompt such as “shadow” or “edges”. LoRA [31] and the semantic head are existing parts, and we choose where to read the maps and how to learn presence. 44.1. Training the semantic path 

UNVEIL trains the semantic path together with the fusion encoder, the presence decoder and a LoRA branch, and keeps the ViT and the text encoder frozen. The LoRA branch and the trained fusion encoder give an adapted mem-ory M ′. Each task supervises σ(S) with its standard loss. Region tasks use BCE plus an IoU loss, with class-balanced BCE for shadows. Edges and skeletons use the class-balanced BCE of RCF [62] and HED [103]. The dense loss averages the per-task means. 

Shared LoRA branch. We copy SAM 3’s last ViT blocks and add LoRA [31] to their attention and MLP layers. The same branch serves all tasks without task-specific weights, and its adapters merge into the copies after training. 

Two deployments. When only our maps are needed, the copies replace SAM 3’s last blocks, and one semantic map replaces the per-detection masks that SAM 3 upsamples and merges. Otherwise they run in parallel with SAM 3’s own last blocks, which still feed its native heads, so SAM 3 keeps its weights and outputs. 

Concept replay. Training on seven tasks erodes the open vocabulary of the semantic path, so we replay Identity con-cepts. A replay sample pairs an LVIS image [21] with a category name, and the union of that category’s instance masks is its target under the region loss. Replay leaves out the categories and images used to measure retention, and categories related to glass and mirrors. 

4.2. Presence apart from the maps 

The presence decoder, a copy of SAM 3’s instance decoder whose presence token gives the logit, decides whether a concept occurs. It is trained with class-balanced BCE on this logit. Its positives are the training images of all tasks, and its negatives are images verified to lack the concept. They include COD10K background and non-camouflaged images [16] for “camouflaged object”, USC12K scenes with neither concept [135] for both Context prompts, and absent categories for replayed LVIS concepts. The decoder reads the adapted memory M ′ and the prompt tokens through a stop-gradient, so its loss never reaches the fusion encoder or the LoRA branch. The dense loss alone shapes the shared features, and without the stop-gradient 8 of the 15 test sets score lower (Sec. 5.5). A single query with prompt t returns σ(S) at image size if σ(p) ≥ τt,else an empty map. The threshold τt, fixed before testing, maximizes balanced accuracy on validation pairs from the training splits. Each prompt takes one forward pass, without a language model or a reference image.

4.3. An extension for two conventions 

Salient and camouflaged objects come with two labeling conventions. Both concepts depend on the scene, so the same kind of object can be either, as a butterfly is salient in ECSSD and camouflaged in COD10K. Single-concept benchmarks label each concept alone, and USC12K [135] labels the two as a partition of the image, so the same plain prompt receives different targets in training. For this pair, a learned convention vector per prompt is added to the text to-kens of USC12K’s training pairs, and single queries never use it. A joint query {salient object, camouflaged object }

adds the vectors to both members. A joint presence module behind a stop-gradient then keeps each member that passes a joint threshold τJ , also the value with the highest balanced accuracy on validation pairs from the training splits. We show this convention-conditioned extension for the salient and camouflaged pair only.

Every text-promptable model gets our training prompts, and external models with released weights keep their own thresholds. Each model is trained once and tested at its last checkpoint. Two models differ on a test set when the 95% interval of a paired bootstrap over its images excludes zero (Sec. A). 

Compared models. The scorecard holds promptable models that answer every prompt with one set of weights. Frozen SAM 3 and its fine-tuned versions are scored by their instance maps (Sec. 3.2). SAM 3’s official full fine-tuning trains every param-eter through the instance path, with our data, batches and iterations. Its LoRA fine-tuning puts our LoRA branch on SAM 3’s own blocks and trains the same path. ConceptSeg-R1 [130] runs with its released weights, and unified models enter with the printed values of their papers or with their released weights under our scorer. 

Implementation details. The LoRA branch copies ViT blocks 24 to 31, the last eight of 32, with LoRA of rank 16 and scaling factor α = 32 . The trained modules are the fu-sion encoder with the semantic path (10.9M), the presence decoder (11.4M) and the LoRA branch (2.26M). Together they hold 24.6M parameters, or 2.9% of SAM 3. The con-vention vectors add 512 parameters and the joint presence module 11.4M. UNVEIL trains for 11,000 iterations on 8 RTX 4080 SUPER GPUs, which takes 17.2 hours, with the optimizer settings of Sec. A. Each step draws 16 images for every task, for the USC12K pairs, for each pool of presence negatives and for replay. The base model of the ablations omits the joint presence module.

5.4. Saying “none” and two conventions 

Saying “none”. A single query for “camouflaged object” is accepted on 2.6% of the COD10K background images, which contain no camouflaged object. Presence separates these images and USC12K scenes with neither Context con-cept from present ones with an AUROC of 0.994. For LVIS categories verified absent from an image, it declines 98.0% of the queries. Without this decision, the maps of UNVEIL return a region for most absent concepts (Tab. 6). On the rejection split, the released multimodal models and Spider answer “none” on at most 14.2% of the queries on average (Tab. 6). On COD10K backgrounds and absent LVIS cat-egories, the single query of UNVEIL declines about as of-ten as SAM 3’s fine-tuned versions. On USC12K, whether a lone animal is salient depends on the labeling conven-tion [135], and the joint query, which follows USC12K’s convention, declines 95.6% of these queries. 

Two conventions. Because single-concept benchmarks label each concept alone, a single query accepts “camou-flaged object” on 0.79 of DUTS-TE images. USC12K gives each region to one concept, and trained on both conventions with plain prompts, “salient object” learns to skip animals. The joint query follows USC12K’s convention, and on its scenes with both concepts the two outputs overlap by 0.009 against 0.365 for two single queries. In these scenes, the camouflaged output of the joint query reaches an IoU of 0.691 with its own mask, against 0.553 for the single query.

## 6. Conclusion 

SAM 3 sees more than it detects. On concepts of context and structure, such as camouflaged objects and edges, its semantic path locates the concept while its presence score suppresses the detections. Promptable concept maps (PCM) 8ask for this evidence directly, with one phrase in and one map or “none” out. PCM-Bench scores them on concepts of four kinds. UNVEIL reads the maps from the semantic path and learns presence apart from them. With one set of weights, it maps regions, thin structures and categories and has the highest score on each task of the PCM-Bench score-card. Where a concept lies and whether it is present are thus two questions that a promptable segmenter can answer apart. Our concepts beyond categories come from seven tasks, and “none” is verified for the Context prompts and LVIS categories. Broader concept vocabularies with veri-fied negatives are the next step.

14 A. Training details 

We supervise σ(S), resized to the label, with one loss per task. Region tasks use binary cross-entropy (BCE) plus an IoU loss, and shadows use class-balanced BCE plus IoU. Edges use the class-balanced BCE of RCF [62], which ig-nores pixels that some but fewer than half of the annotators mark. Skeletons use class-balanced BCE [103] on targets dilated by one pixel. The dense loss averages the per-task means, and every task contributes the same number of im-ages per step. AdamW updates the fusion encoder, the semantic path and the presence decoder with a learning rate of 8 × 10 −5

and weight decay 0.1. The LoRA branch uses a learning rate of 2.5×10 −4 and the convention vectors 8×10 −4. Gra-dients are clipped at 0.1, and the schedule has 300 warm-up steps and a cosine decay. A step holds 192 images across all streams. The joint presence module has its own optimizer, clipping and data stream. The other modules thus train as in the base model, up to GPU nondeterminism. 

Statistics. Each model is trained once with one seed. Comparisons between our runs pair the images of a test set and resample them with a bootstrap. A count of sets on which one run scores higher or lower, as in Secs. 5.3 and 5.5, includes a set only if its paired 95% interval ex-cludes zero. A set whose interval contains zero counts as level. Comparisons with published results use the printed values.
