BiRefNet-V2: Rethinking Bilateral Reference
for High-Resolution Dichotomous Image
Segmentation
Peng Zheng, Deng-Ping Fan, Binglu Wang, Nicu Sebe
zhengpeng0108@gmail.com
Abstract—Dichotomous image segmentation requires pixel-accurate foreground–background separation while preserving fine struc-
tures such as thin filaments, sharp edges, and intricate textures. BiRefNet addressed this challenge through bilateral references,
combining image patches and gradient information, but its inward reference loses local spatial structure and lacks semantic guidance,
while its outward reference relies on single-channel gradient attention. We recast this paradigm as controlled information recovery—
locality determines what spatial information survives, semantic selectivity what information enters, and focused boundary supervision
where refinement occurs—and propose BiRefNet-V2 to implement it: (1) locality-preserving and semantically guided detail injection
through space-to-depth transformation, overlapping patches, and a Semantically Guided Gate; (2) a learned boundary stream with
focused supervision that generates full-channel residual attention without requiring boundary annotations; and (3) a dual-branch
decoder integrating depth-wise boundary convolutions with large-kernel attention. A modernized framework—LayerNorm, GELU, the
Lion optimizer, and a DINOv3 backbone—cuts training from 600 to 50 epochs. Extensive experiments demonstrate state-of-the-art
performance on DIS5K, camouflaged object detection, and high-resolution salient object detection across all 13 evaluation sets. Code
is available at https://github.com/ZhengPeng7/BiRefNet-V2.
Index Terms—Dichotomous image segmentation, high-resolution segmentation, bilateral reference, camouflaged object detection.

In this paper we re-instantiate the bilateral-reference

paradigm and the encoder–decoder skeleton from BiRefNet,
but its core components—both references and the decoder
block that links them—are redesigned (Table 1). The re-
design elevates those three requirements into a single
principle—bilateral reference as controlled information recov-
ery (Fig. 2(d)): detail recovery is a question of which infor-
mation to inject and where, controlled at both ends of the
decoder—realized by the three components summarized be-
low. Complementing these architectural changes, we switch
the backbone from a fully fine-tuned Swin-L [14] to a
self-supervised DINOv3 [15] encoder kept mostly frozen,
with only its last transformer stage fine-tuned at a low
learning rate (§3.5), shrinking both the trainable stack and
the training schedule while accuracy improves rather than
degrades. The payoff is generality: trained separately per
benchmark with no task-specific adaptation, the same ar-
chitecture surpasses models purpose-built for camouflaged
object detection and high-resolution salient object detection
as well as for DIS—suggesting that these detail-demanding
tasks pose the same information-recovery problem.

RELATED WORK
DIS. Qin et al. [1] formalized DIS and introduced the
DIS5K benchmark together with IS-Net, which mitigates
the loss of fine structures during encoding via intermediate
supervision. Later work attacked the same problem with
frequency priors [16], a unite-divide-unite decomposition
of trunk and boundary [9], image-pyramid processing at
multiple resolutions [11], and multi-view aggregation [10].
Our prior work, BiRefNet [13], introduced the bilateral-
reference paradigm. More recently, generative priors have
entered DIS. GenPercept [12] repurposes a pretrained dif-
fusion model for dense perception tasks, including DIS,
with single-step inference; DiffDIS [8] adapts pretrained
diffusion priors specifically to high-precision DIS, and is
the strongest fully automatic diffusion-based method in
our comparison; MaskFactory [17] instead uses diffusion
models offline to synthesize additional DIS training pairs. A
separate line adds user prompts on top of generative mod-
eling: LawDIS [18] formulates DIS as prompt-guided mask

3
generation inside a latent diffusion model, and FlowDIS [19]
as text-guided flow matching that transports the image
distribution to the mask distribution—a setting distinct
from the fully automatic methods we compare against.
SAMA [20] and FCLM [21] bring the same prompt-driven
setting to discriminative models. Outside the generative
family, PDFNet [7] pairs each image with a pseudo-depth
map from a monocular depth estimator as a structural
prior, and is the strongest of the published methods we
compare against on F x
β , the primary DIS metric. Unlike
the methods above, which strengthen the encoder, aggre-
gate multiple scales, or rely on heavy generative priors,
BiRefNet-V2 makes the original image itself re-enter every
decoder stage—now under explicit semantic control—while
keeping single-forward discriminative inference.
Detail Preservation in High-Resolution Segmentation.
Several families of methods compensate for the fine detail
discarded by encoder downsampling: skip connections [22],
[23] forward encoder features to the decoder, but those
features are already abstracted and lack pixel-level fidelity;
multi-scale input schemes [11], [24] process the image
at several resolutions in separate streams; and pyramid-
grafting methods [25] fuse features across lightweight and
heavy backbones operating at different scales. More di-
rectly, BiRefNet [13] injects original image patches into each
decoder stage (the inward reference), and MVANet [10]
decomposes the input into multi-view patches within a
single encoder–decoder stream. The injection of BiRefNet,
however, is indiscriminate and sacrifices spatial locality
by stacking patches along the channel dimension; our re-
designed inward reference addresses exactly these failures.
Auxiliary Supervision for Feature Refinement. Beyond
the primary segmentation loss, deep supervision [1], [9]
applies the loss at multiple decoder stages, contour su-
pervision [5], [26] adds explicit boundary-prediction heads,
and gradient-based supervision [27] predicts image-derived
edge maps to sharpen features near boundaries. The out-
ward reference of BiRefNet [13] follows the last route, but
its Laplacian target responds to background texture as much
as to object contours. BiRefNet-V2 instead gates the image-
edge target with the decoder’s evolving prediction and
upgrades to full-channel residual attention (§3.4).
3
BILATERAL REFERENCE NETWORK V2
In this section, we present the architecture of BiRefNet-V2.
After the framework overview (§3.1), the structure mirrors
the three requirements identified in §1: §3.2 restores locality
and adds selectivity on the input side, §3.3 structurally links
the two references, §3.4 brings focused supervision to the
output side, and §3.5 details training and inference.
3.1
Framework Overview
BiRefNet-V2 follows an encoder–decoder architecture with
bilateral reference. The pipeline is summarized below and
shown in Fig. 3.
Encoder. We adopt a DINOv3-Large (DINOv3-L) [15]
backbone encoder, a ViT-L/16 model with 24 transformer
blocks. Given an input image I ∈R3×H×W , the backbone
extracts four intermediate features {Fi}4

encoder into four consecutive block groups, which we call

them—blocks 19–24—which is fine-tuned as described in
§3.5. Since all four feature maps share the same channel
dimension Cbb = 1024 (a property of plain ViT architec-
tures), we employ a pyramid neck consisting of four 1 × 1
convolutions to project them into a hierarchical channel

Squeeze module. Following BiRefNet [13], we apply
a squeeze module to the deepest feature F4 prior to the
pyramid-neck projection. This module is a residual con-
volutional block with an Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling
(ASPP) [28] module between its two convolutions, which
captures multi-scale context to enrich global semantics.
Decoder. The decoder has four stages that progressively
refine predictions from the coarsest to the finest resolution.
At each stage s ∈{0, 1, 2, 3} (written s0–s3, from deepest to
shallowest, as in Fig. 3), the processing follows three steps:
1)
Inward reference (IR): The original image I is parti-
tioned into patches at a resolution matching the cur-
rent stage, processed by a lightweight convolutional
block, and selectively injected into decoder features
via a Semantically Guided Gate (SGG).
2)
Dual-branch decoder block (DB): The concatenated
features are transformed by a block that combines
the modeling of local boundaries with the aggrega-
tion of global context.
3)
Outward reference (OR) (stages s0–s2): The de-
coder output is refined through full-channel resid-
ual attention, where the model learns to predict
boundary maps supervised by prediction-gated
image-edge targets during training.
After stage s3, the decoder output is upsampled to
the input resolution and concatenated with a final inward
reference (the full-resolution injection site, denoted stage s4,
where each patch is a single pixel) into Dout to produce the
prediction ˆM = σ(Conv1×1(Dout)) ∈[0, 1]H×W , where σ
denotes the sigmoid function.
Multi-scale supervision. During training, intermediate
predictions are generated at stages s0–s2 via 1 × 1 convolu-
tions. Together with the final prediction, these form a set of
multi-scale supervision signals, each bilinearly upsampled
to the ground-truth resolution and supervised there. Table 1
summarizes the component-level differences from BiRefNet.

3.3
Dual-Branch Decoder Block
DIS requires simultaneously capturing fine boundary de-
tails and maintaining global structural coherence. A single
processing pathway struggles to satisfy both requirements:
small-kernel convolutions excel at local edge detection but
lack context, while softmax self-attention captures long-
range dependencies only at a quadratic cost that becomes
prohibitive at high-resolution decoder stages. We address
this with a dual-branch decoder block that processes both
aspects in parallel—a depth-wise convolutional boundary
branch and a large-kernel-attention context branch—and
fuses them adaptively.

Adaptive Fusion
The two branches are fused via a learned gate that adap-
tively weighs boundary and context features:

signal g), Proj is a 1 × 1 projection, and β = 0.1 is a fixed
scaling factor for the cross-branch residual. The fused fea-

restores the stage width, and are summed with a shortcut

convolution with LayerNorm; the sum is the block output

3.4
Outward Reference
While BiRefNet [13] computes a Laplacian edge map from
the input image and uses single-channel attention to weight
decoder features (Ys ←Ys ⊙a, a ∈R1×h×w), our redesign
(Fig. 4(c)) introduces three improvements: prediction-gated
image-edge supervision, lightweight boundary feature ex-
traction, and full-channel residual attention.
3.4.1
Prediction-Gated Image-Edge Supervision
BiRefNet already multiplies its Laplacian target by its in-
termediate prediction, but does so in logit space—a signed
Laplacian times an unbounded logit, squashed by a sigmoid
only at the loss (§1). We instead build the target in two steps,
bounding the edge map and the gate separately. First, an
edge map is extracted from the input image by a lightly
smoothed Laplacian: the grayscale input is filtered by a 5×5
bilateral filter, followed by a 5 × 5 Laplacian whose absolute
response is squashed by tanh:

3.4.3
Full-Channel Residual Attention
The key refinement mechanism is a channel-wise attention
map As ∈RCs+1×h×w (the shape of Ys: 768, 384, and 192
channels at s0–s2), produced from the boundary features by

modulation, selectively amplifying each feature dimension
by its own factor in (1, 2) instead of scaling all channels by
the same factor; (2) the residual form (1 + As) preserves the
original features when As ≈0, preventing information loss
and stabilizing gradients.

3.5.1
Training Procedure
Backbone. We keep the DINOv3-L backbone frozen except
for its last transformer stage, which is fine-tuned at 0.05×
the base learning rate, so that adapting the pretrained
representation refines rather than overwrites it. Together
with the decoder, squeeze module, pyramid neck, and the
inward- and outward-reference modules, this gives 84.9M
trainable parameters. §4.5 quantifies this partial adaptation

4
EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate BiRefNet-V2 on three high-precision segmen-
tation tasks: DIS, high-resolution salient object detection
(HRSOD), and camouflaged object detection (COD). All
three tasks require pixel-accurate segmentation masks with
fine structural details.
4.1
Datasets and Settings
DIS. We use the DIS5K benchmark [1], which contains high-
resolution images across 225 categories with pixel-accurate
annotations. The training set (DIS-TR) comprises 3,000 im-
ages. We report results on five evaluation sets: DIS-VD (470
images), DIS-TE1 (500), DIS-TE2 (500), DIS-TE3 (500), and
DIS-TE4 (500), the four DIS-TE splits being organized by
increasing structural complexity.
HRSOD. Following [11], [13], [25], we adopt different
combinations of the training splits of DUTS [32] (10,553
images), HRSOD [33] (1,610 images), and UHRSD [25] (4,932
images) as the training set. Evaluation is conducted on
three high-resolution benchmarks: DAVIS-S [33] (92 im-
ages), HRSOD-TE [33] (400 images), and UHRSD-TE [25]
(988 images). We additionally report the two standard low-
resolution salient object detection test sets, DUTS-TE [32]
(5,019 images) and DUT-OMRON [34] (5,168 images), eval-
uated with the same checkpoints without any re-training.
COD. Following the standard protocol [35], we train on
the combined training splits of COD10K [36] (3,040 images)
and CAMO [37] (1,000 images). Evaluation is conducted
on three test sets: CAMO-TE [37] (250 images), COD10K-
TE [36] (2,026 images), and NC4K [38] (4,121 images).
4.2
Evaluation Metrics
For DIS, following [1], [9], [13], we adopt six widely
used metrics: maximum F-measure (F x
β ) [39], weighted
F-measure (F ω
β ) [40], mean absolute error (M), structure
measure (Sm) [41], mean E-measure (Em
ϕ ) [42], and relaxed
human correction efforts (HCEγ) [1], the last of which
approximates the human effort (i.e., mouse clicks within
tolerance γ=5) required to correct faulty predictions. For
HRSOD, following [13], [25], we report Sm, F x
β , Em
ϕ , and
M. For COD, following [13], [35], we report Sm, F ω
β , mean
F-measure (F m
β ), Em
ϕ , max E-measure (Ex
ϕ), and M.
4.3
Implementation Details
We implement BiRefNet-V2 using PyTorch [43]. Training
uses two GPUs. Unless otherwise stated, all measurements
are performed on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090.
Backbone freezing and the DINOv3 last-stage tuning follow
§3.5; all decoder-side components are optimized at the base
learning rate. The base learning rate is 3 × 10−4, and the
total batch size is 8, split evenly across the training GPUs.
We apply gradient clipping at norm 10.0. Training uses bf16
mixed precision with a compiled computation graph. Unless
otherwise stated, input images are resized to 1024 × 1024
for both training and inference. We use random horizon-
tal flipping and foreground–background recomposition for
data augmentation.
The model is trained for 50 epochs on DIS5K, 30 epochs
on COD, and 20 epochs on HRSOD. The loss function
applies BCE (λ=30) and IoU (λ=2) losses to all decoder
predictions, applies SSIM (λ=400) to the final prediction,
and adds a BCE loss for the outward-reference boundary
predictions; the weights are set so that the three pixel-level
terms stay within the same order of magnitude. In the state-
of-the-art comparisons (Tables 2 to 4 and Fig. 6), every num-
ber reported for a training configuration comes from one
checkpoint, with no per-set selection: the checkpoint that
maximizes the task’s primary metric—F x
β for DIS, Sm for
HRSOD and COD—averaged with equal weight over its pri-
mary evaluation sets, namely DIS-VD and the four DIS-TE
splits for DIS; CAMO-TE, COD10K-TE, and NC4K for COD;
and DAVIS-S, HRSOD-TE, and UHRSD-TE for HRSOD. The
two low-resolution sets of Table 3 are reported without
taking part in the selection. The output masks are resized
back to the original resolution via bilinear interpolation
for evaluation. Detailed model size, GMACs, and inference
speed analyses are reported in §4.5.6. Pretrained models1
and an interactive online demo2 have been released.

4.5.1
From BiRefNet to the Modernized Framework
Table 6 isolates the modernized framework itself. The start-
ing point keeps every BiRefNet framework choice—a fully
fine-tuned Swin-L encoder, BatchNorm, ReLU, AdamW—
but carries no bilateral references; each subsequent row then
replaces one of those choices with its modern counterpart,
the encoder by a frozen DINOv3-L, BatchNorm by Layer-
Norm, ReLU by GELU, and AdamW by Lion. The four
replacements lift F x
β from 0.8722 to 0.8955, dominated by
the encoder swap (+1.48); LayerNorm adds a further +0.55,
GELU +0.23, and Lion +0.07. This configuration—a frozen
encoder paired with the plain single-branch decoder—
already exceeds the full BiRefNet model on DIS-VD (0.8955
vs 0.891, cf. Table 2) with ∼24× fewer trainable parameters
(8.8M) and a 12× shorter schedule. We fold the four replace-
ments, together with the last-stage tuning described below,
into a single modernization step in Table 5.
The top block of Table 6 closes with the DINOv3
last-stage tuning of §3.5. Applied directly on top of the Lion
row, it is the largest single step of the ladder, lifting F x
β from
0.8955 to 0.9155 (+2.00 points). Notably, it barely moves
HCEγ (1404→1374): the adaptation repairs the region-
level fit of the frozen self-supervised features—a strong
starting point, but slightly mismatched to the pixel-accurate
demands of DIS—while boundary quality is delivered by
the references built on top of it. Tuning depth has a single
interior optimum in F x
β , with all four of its points measured
against the same full-model anchor: freezing the encoder
entirely costs −0.0130 (0.9198±0.0028, HCEγ 807), tuning
the last two stages costs −0.0135 (0.9193±0.0050, HCEγ 723),
and unfreezing it altogether costs −0.0215 (0.9113±0.0030,
HCEγ 745) while also being the most expensive option. The
near-symmetry of the two inner losses in F x
β shows that
adapting exactly the last semantic stage is not a compromise
between cost and accuracy but the accuracy optimum itself,
and that deeper adaptation at this short schedule is both
costlier and less accurate. We therefore keep this adaptation
minimal and treat it as part of the modernized framework
rather than a separate module.

Inference efficiency. BiRefNet-V2 runs at 18.4 FPS with
bf16 mixed precision and batch size 1 at 1024 × 1024 input,
with a peak GPU memory footprint of ∼3.2 GB. Table 9
places this figure in context: every compared method listed
there is re-benchmarked under the single protocol of the
table caption, keeping each method’s official inference pro-
cedure intact (e.g., the test-time augmentation built into
the DiffDIS release). BiRefNet-V2 runs faster and leaner
than BiRefNet (18.4 vs 15.3 FPS; 3.2 vs 3.5 GB) despite
carrying a larger total parameter count; lightweight CNN-
based detectors such as IS-Net remain ∼4× faster, but at a
substantial accuracy deficit (Table 2). PDFNet, the strongest
competitor on the primary DIS metric, pays for its pseudo-
depth prior with a 433 M two-stage pipeline running at
1.9 FPS—an order of magnitude slower than BiRefNet-V2.
The contrast with the diffusion-based family is starkest:
GenPercept needs ∼950 M inference parameters for 3.9 FPS,
and DiffDIS—the strongest fully automatic diffusion-based
method in our comparison—runs its official inference at
0.31 FPS with a 16.7 GB peak, roughly 60× slower and 5×
more memory-hungry than BiRefNet-V2 while trailing it in
accuracy (Table 2). Single-forward discriminative inference
thus remains the only route that combines top accuracy
with a commodity-hardware footprint at full resolution. On
the COD side the pattern repeats: the strongest specialist,
ZoomNeXt, runs at 11.7 FPS and the Mamba-based RUN at
6.9 FPS, while BiRefNet-V2 serves COD with the identical
architecture and cost.

Training efficiency. The mostly-frozen-backbone design
also shrinks the training cost: BiRefNet-V2 converges in 50
epochs on DIS5K (versus 600 epochs for BiRefNet, a 12×
reduction), with a per-GPU footprint of 6.5 GB at batch size
1 and 11.4 GB at batch size 2 (1024 × 1024, bf16)—within
consumer-grade 32 GB cards. We attribute this efficiency to
two factors: (i) gradients flow only through the decoder and
the last backbone stage, removing most of the backbone’s
share of the activation memory; and (ii) the strong DINOv3
features remove the need for the long schedules that fully
fine-tuned encoders need to become foreground-aware.
Maximum native resolution. The same mostly-frozen-
backbone economy also raises the ceiling for native high-
resolution training: the 32 GB card used above sustains full
training steps at up to 2432 × 2432 (forward-only inference
up to 3968 × 3968), and a large-memory 268 GB GPU trains
at up to 7552 × 7552 (see the supplementary material).

LIMITATIONS
Fig. 12 shows two characteristic failure cases, drawn from
the worst predictions of BiRefNet-V2 on DIS5K as ranked by
per-image M. Both errors lie at the level of foreground defi-
nition—deciding what counts as the object—rather than seg-
mentation quality: the model truncates a target whose extent
is genuinely ambiguous (the bridge deck), and merges a
second, same-colored vehicle into the target (the cargo tricy-
cle). BiRefNet, PDFNet, and DiffDIS, shown alongside, fail
in the same way in both cases: the errors are neither intro-
duced by our redesign nor specific to the bilateral-reference
paradigm—they arise where the foreground decision itself
is the hard part. The pattern is consistent with the design:
the references chiefly sharpen how well the selected region
is delineated and only occasionally prune a spurious region
(the bucket in Ladder, Fig. 7), while the selection itself is
made by the semantic stream, whose verdict the Semanti-
cally Guided Gate can only execute, not overturn; when that
stream commits to the wrong target, detail injection refines
the wrong decision. The semantic axis, on which BiRefNet-
V2 also improves over prior methods (Fig. 9, top row), is
thus where its residual failures concentrate: the paradigm
moves the frontier from delineation to definition.
Beyond these failure modes, BiRefNet-V2 has two sys-
temic limitations. First, it inherits the inference cost of its
ViT-L backbone: although mostly frozen at training time,
the backbone accounts for 2068.3 of the 2208.8 GMACs per
forward pass, limiting full-resolution inference to 18.4 FPS;
latency-critical deployments must fall back to the synced
low-resolution regime of §4.5.6 or to distillation. Second,
training remains at 1024 × 1024, which under-exploits na-
tively 4K benchmarks such as UHRSD-TE (Table 3).
7
CONCLUSION
We have presented BiRefNet-V2, which recasts the bilateral-
reference paradigm for high-resolution DIS as controlled
information recovery: raw image detail re-enters the decoder
locally, selectively, and under supervision focused on the
object being segmented. Tracing the shortfalls of the original
BiRefNet to these three unmet requirements, we redesigned
both references and the decoder block that links them, and
paired them with a modernized, mostly-frozen framework.
The resulting model leads across all 13 evaluation sets of
three tasks and converges in an order of magnitude fewer
epochs than its predecessor.
The broader lesson is a separation of concerns: a heavy
semantic stream that can stay mostly frozen, and a light
detail stream that runs at full resolution under semantic
gating. This separation lets the framework absorb stronger
frozen encoders (§4.5.5) and set each stream’s resolution
independently (Fig. 8). We see the paradigm as a reusable
template for dense prediction tasks whose difficulty lies in
fine structure rather than semantics, with alpha matting and
medical segmentation as immediate candidates.
