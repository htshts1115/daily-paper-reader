SynLF: Zero-Shot Metric Depth from Light Field
Cameras via Physics-Grounded Synthesis
Zhexuan Cao, Yuduo Guo, Peisheng Ding, Zhan Shi, and Hui Qiao⋆
Department of Automation, Tsinghua University, Beijing, China
Abstract. Accurate monocular metric depth estimation remains inher-
ently ill-posed due to scale ambiguity. Light field cameras mitigate this
ambiguity by capturing micro-baseline disparities within a single lens
to recover metric depth, but current learning-based solutions are lim-
ited by scarce training data and often overfit Lambertian appearance
patterns, restricting real-world robustness. To address this, we propose
SynLF, which alleviates data scarcity via physics-grounded light field
(PG-LF) synthesis and performs robust real-world metric depth esti-
mation through VisDepth, a prior-initialized iterative estimator with ex-
plicit multi-view occlusion reasoning. During training, we synthesize light
field supervision on-the-fly from RGB-D via physics-grounded light field
synthesis, including geometry-consistent view synthesis, depth-dependent
defocus, and non-Lambertian perturbations. At inference, the model is
directly evaluated on real light field captures without fine-tuning. Ex-
tensive experiments demonstrate that SynLF generalizes zero-shot to
our custom real-world light field dataset, which features highly chal-
lenging conditions such as transparency, reflections, specular highlights,
and textureless regions. It achieves 30 mm absolute and 1.4% relative
errors across 0.5–2.5 m, demonstrating that physics-grounded LF syn-
thesis from large-scale RGB-D provides a scalable and storage-efficient
alternative to conventional light field acquisition for accurate single-lens
metric depth estimation.

tonomous driving and robotics [8, 9]. While active sensors (e.g., LiDAR) are
bulky and costly [22], monocular estimators offer a compact alternative by lever-
aging large-scale datasets and powerful foundation models [16, 32]. However,
due to the inherent scale ambiguity of conventional cameras, recovering reli-
able absolute metric scale from a single image remains fundamentally ill-posed.
In contrast, light field (LF) cameras provide a principled single-lens solution:

Despite this theoretical advantage, current learning-based LF depth estima-
tion is fundamentally bottlenecked by the scarcity of training data. Render-
ing multi-view LF supervision is computationally expensive, and available su-
pervised LF datasets remain far smaller than modern monocular RGB-D cor-
pora, ranging from tens of benchmark scenes to roughly thousand-sample ren-
dered/captured datasets [15,24]. This data scarcity often causes models to overfit
idealized Lambertian appearances and generalize poorly to real-world complex-
ities such as occlusion, non-Lambertian surfaces, and textureless regions [6,30].
Furthermore, existing methods typically aggregate multi-view evidence through
implicit weighting [28], lacking explicit visibility constraints. Such undercon-
strained geometric reasoning inevitably leads to brittle performance in challeng-
ing real-world scenes.
To address these limitations, we introduce SynLF, a framework that combines
physics-grounded light field synthesis (PG-LF synthesis) with a visibility-
aware depth estimation network (VisDepth), as summarized in Fig. 1. Dur-
ing training, SynLF synthesizes light field supervision on-the-fly from RGB-D
inputs via PG-LF synthesis, which selectively models the imaging effects that
directly corrupt cross-view disparity correspondence—the core cue for light field
depth estimation—including occlusion-aware view synthesis, depth-dependent
defocus, and non-Lambertian perturbations. Built upon this supervision, Vis-
Depth performs prior-initialized, visibility-aware iterative disparity estimation
with explicit multi-view occlusion reasoning. By coupling the synthesis pipeline
and the iterative estimator via the same depth-aware projection operator, our

framework inherently enforces strict geometric reasoning rather than overfitting
to appearance shortcuts, establishing a scalable and robust solution for light field

We evaluate SynLF on real-world data captured by a custom-built light field
camera paired with a high-precision structured-light sensor, covering challenging

Our contributions are three-fold:
– We propose PG-LF synthesis, a physics-grounded light field synthesis
pipeline that generates on-the-fly supervisory light fields from abundant
RGB-D, eliminating the need for pre-rendered light field datasets.
– We introduce VisDepth, a prior-initialized, visibility-aware iterative esti-
mator that explicitly reasons about multi-view occlusions for robust light
field metric depth estimation.
– The resulting framework, SynLF, demonstrates strong zero-shot general-
ization on challenging real-world light field captures and achieves accurate
metric depth estimation without fine-tuning.
2
Related Work
2.1
Monocular Metric Depth Estimation
Monocular metric depth estimation seeks to recover scene geometry from a single
RGB image, yet the problem is fundamentally ill-posed due to scale ambiguity:
multiple geometrically plausible 3D scenes can produce identical image obser-
vations with different absolute scales. Early approaches mitigated this issue by
adopting scale-invariant objectives that emphasize relative depth relationships
rather than metric accuracy [10].
Subsequent methods explored various strategies to reduce scale ambiguity,
including discretized depth classification [2, 11], large-scale synthetic supervi-
sion [21], and scale/shift-invariant objectives or affine-invariant geometry rep-
resentations to improve cross-domain robustness [20,29,32]. More recent works
explicitly aim at metric depth prediction by modeling camera geometry, esti-
mating camera parameters, or training for absolute metric scale over diverse
data [4,16,19]. Nevertheless, even state-of-the-art monocular estimators can ex-
hibit significant scale errors in simple scenes, underscoring the inherent limita-
tions of single-view geometry.
2.2
Light Field Depth Estimation
Light field cameras capture multiple sub-aperture views within a single exposure,
effectively forming a narrow-baseline camera array within a single-lens imaging

system [1,13]. Compared with conventional monocular imaging, this multi-view
structure provides additional geometric constraints that help resolve scale am-
biguity.
Early approaches leveraged optimization-based formulations or epipolar plane
image (EPI) analysis to estimate depth from geometric and photometric con-
straints [14, 26, 31, 33]. More recent learning-based methods construct multi-
disparity cost volumes and employ neural networks for disparity regression and
refinement [5,28,30]. These supervised approaches typically rely on fully rendered
light field datasets with ground-truth disparity annotations, such as HCI [15] and
the synthetic subset of UrbanLF [24].
However, due to the limited scale and diversity of available light field datasets,
learning-based models often struggle to generalize beyond controlled synthetic
settings. In addition, occlusion reasoning is commonly handled implicitly through
view aggregation mechanisms without explicit visibility constraints, limiting ge-
ometric consistency across views [28]. Together, these data and modeling limi-
tations substantially restrict the robustness and scalability of current light field
depth estimation methods.
2.3
Light Field Data Synthesis
Common light field depth benchmarks and synthetic subsets are primarily gen-
erated using graphics rendering engines [15,24], which construct full 3D scenes
and render images from micro-baseline camera arrays. Although this strategy
produces geometrically accurate supervision, it requires rendering many sub-
aperture views per scene (typically 5×5 to 9×9), making large-scale generation
much heavier than producing a single RGB-D pair. As a result, available datasets
remain limited in scale and diversity.
To improve efficiency, recent work has explored synthesizing light fields di-
rectly from a single image using neural networks [12, 23, 27]. However, these
methods learn to hallucinate plausible views via reconstruction losses without
explicit geometric consistency guarantees, and typically require real light field
captures for training. As a result, the synthesized views may appear visually
reasonable but lack the multi-view geometric fidelity required for reliable depth
supervision.

system. In this way, SynLF transfers supervision from large-scale RGB-D data
to real light field depth estimation and directly generalizes to real captures with-
out fine-tuning.
3.2
Physics-Grounded Light Field Synthesis
The design of PG-LF synthesis is guided by a task-driven principle: we selectively
model the imaging effects that directly corrupt the core cue exploited by light
field depth estimation—cross-view disparity correspondence—rather than pur-
suing generic photorealistic simulation. Light field depth estimation fundamen-
tally relies on establishing consistent correspondences across sub-aperture views.
Three factors systematically disrupt this process in real captures: (1) occlusion,

where foreground objects block background content across views; (2) depth-
dependent defocus, where out-of-focus blur attenuates the very textures used for
correspondence matching; and (3) non-Lambertian appearance, where specular or
transparent surfaces cause the apparent depth to diverge from the physical sur-
face depth. PG-LF synthesis addresses each of these through geometry-consistent
forward splatting, disparity-conditioned defocus, and targeted non-Lambertian
perturbation, respectively.

Depth-Dependent Defocus. Standard synthetic RGB-D datasets are typically
rendered under an all-in-focus pinhole model, whereas real light field cameras
exhibit pronounced depth-dependent defocus blur. We specifically model defocus
because its CoC magnitude is directly coupled to scene disparity (Eq. 1), mean-
ing it systematically attenuates the very correspondence cues that drive depth
estimation. Other optical effects such as motion blur or cat-eye bokeh, while
present in practice, are not primarily disparity-coupled and thus less critical for
bridging the sim-to-real gap. We model defocus using a disparity-conditioned
CoC formulation.
Under the thin-lens model, the CoC magnitude is determined by the mis-
match between the scene depth z and the focal-plane depth zf. Since light-field
disparity is affine to inverse depth (d = α/z −β), aligning the zero-disparity
plane with the focal plane yields a direct mapping:

In practice, we implement defocus via disparity slicing. We discretize the
disparity map into bins, assign each bin a corresponding Gaussian kernel, and
blur the image layer by layer. The blurred layers are then composited in back-
to-front order to preserve correct occlusion boundaries and prevent foreground
textures from bleeding into the background.
Non-Lambertian Perturbation. Real scenes often violate the Lambertian as-
sumption due to specular reflections and transparent media. Physically, on such
surfaces, the apparent depth perceived through multi-view matching diverges
from the true surface geometry: reflections appear behind the surface, while
transmitted light shifts toward background depth. Lambertian warping misses
this discrepancy by tying radiance strictly to the surface. To mimic such ef-
fects without corrupting the underlying geometry, we perturb the disparity used
for sub-aperture view synthesis, so that the projected source samples are in-
tentionally displaced from their geometrically correct locations. This produces
view-inconsistent reprojections that simulate the failure of strict photometric
matching under non-Lambertian conditions.
To avoid conflating this perturbation with true geometric discontinuities,
it is applied only to regions that are locally depth-flat yet visually textured,
while excluding depth boundaries and weak-texture areas. Importantly, only the
synthesized sub-aperture views are perturbed, whereas the original center view
remains unchanged. As a result, trivial appearance matching across views is
discouraged, and the model is forced to rely more on stable geometric cues from
the synthesized light field together with reliable center-view cues, improving
generalization to non-Lambertian scenes.

Visibility-Aware LF Disparity Estimation

a reasonably accurate initial disparity estimate. In practice, directly starting it-

in textureless and non-Lambertian regions where light field correspondence is
ambiguous. We therefore introduce a lightweight prior-guided disparity initial-
ization stage whose role is not to solve the full task, but to provide a reliable
starting point for subsequent visibility-aware iterative estimation.

Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup
We implement SynLF in PyTorch and train it on the Hypersim dataset, which
contains 77,400 photorealistic RGB-D images of diverse indoor scenes with ac-
curate per-pixel depth annotations. The disparity is computed from depth using
Eq. (1), with α = 16 and β = −9. Samples containing invalid depth values are
removed, leaving 74,013 images for training.
During training, light field supervision is synthesized on-the-fly from RGB-D
inputs using the proposed physics-grounded synthesis pipeline. Unless otherwise
specified, the input image is randomly cropped to 768 × 768. We train SynLF
with the AdamW optimizer using a total batch size of 16. Leveraging the rich
texture and geometric diversity of large-scale RGB-D data, convergence is fast
and 8K optimization steps suffice (approximately two passes over the Hypersim
training set; see the supplementary material for the training-loss trajectory). The
learning rate is initialized at 5×10−5, linearly warmed up to 5×10−4, and then
decayed polynomially to 1 × 10−5. The disparity range is set to dmin = −9 and
dmax = 20. During both training and inference, the ConvGRU module performs
24 iterative updates.
For quantitative evaluation, we report four standard depth metrics: mean
absolute error (MAE, in mm), root mean squared error (RMSE, in mm), absolute
relative error (AbsRel, in %), and threshold accuracy δ1.

4.2
Real-World Light Field Dataset
To evaluate zero-shot generalization under realistic imaging conditions, we cap-
ture a real-world light field dataset using a custom acquisition system consisting
of an industrial camera with a 9×9 micro-lens array, paired with a high-precision
structured-light camera for metric depth. All scenes are static during capture.
Using calibrated light-field intrinsics and the extrinsic transformation between
the two sensors, we reproject the structured-light depth into the light-field center
view to obtain per-pixel metric depth annotations.
For evaluation, we collected 173 annotated indoor captures spanning diffuse
objects and challenging non-Lambertian materials (e.g., specular metals, plas-
tics, glass, and reflective displays), as well as 30 outdoor light field captures
without annotations for qualitative evaluation. Since structured-light sensing
may fail under severe transparency or strong reflections, we restrict quantitative
evaluation to valid annotated pixels using the sensor validity mask; all methods
are evaluated on the same valid regions for fair comparison. For metric evalua-
tion, predicted center-view disparity is converted to depth using a camera-specific
disparity-to-depth mapping calibrated offline. The geometric calibration yields
sub-pixel mean reprojection error; details are provided in the supplementary
material.
Ethics and privacy. Our indoor dataset contains only static objects and
does not involve human participants. For outdoor qualitative examples, by-
standers may incidentally appear in some captures; we do not use any personal
identity information and we anonymize the figures by blurring/cropping identi-
fiable regions (e.g., faces, bodies, license plates). Any data released will undergo
privacy review and anonymization.

architectural gain of 23.06 mm.

4.4
Ablation Study
For the ablation study, we report MAE (mm) and AbsRel (%) for brevity. As
shown in Tab. 5, both non-Lambertian perturbation and depth-dependent de-
focus independently improve zero-shot transfer: the former discourages shortcut
learning from strict photometric matching and improves robustness to reflec-
tive regions (Fig. 3), while the latter bridges the mismatch between all-in-focus
RGB-D and the pronounced blur of real light field cameras, particularly benefit-
ing heavily defocused foregrounds (Fig. 3). Notably, the largest gain comes from
combining both components, suggesting that robust real-world transfer requires
exposure to both appearance inconsistency and blur-induced ambiguity during
training.
We further ablate two design choices in the supplementary material. Increas-
ing GRU iterations from 6 to 24 steadily reduces MAE from 36.39 to 30.05 mm,
confirming that iterative visibility-aware refinement progressively sharpens ge-
ometry in occluded or ambiguous regions. In contrast, scaling the frozen prior
from DAv2-Small to DAv2-Large does not improve results, indicating that SynLF’s

5
Discussion
Our results suggest that the main bottleneck of real-world light field depth es-
timation lies not only in model design, but in the mismatch between idealized
supervision and real imaging conditions. SynLF shows that large-scale RGB-D
data can be transformed into effective light field supervision when the synthesis
process preserves multi-view geometry and introduces physically motivated non-
ideal effects. More broadly, PG-LF synthesis shifts the paradigm from collecting
large light field datasets to designing task-driven synthesis that preserves the ge-
ometric structure required by the downstream task while enriching the training
distribution with realistic imaging variations.
At the same time, our synthesis model remains an approximation of real
light field formation. Although it captures key factors such as visibility, defocus,
and non-Lambertian perturbation, it does not explicitly model the full com-
plexity of real optical effects, or severe material-dependent appearance changes.
In addition, our quantitative evaluation focuses on static indoor scenes, and
ground-truth depth in highly reflective or transparent regions remains inher-
ently challenging even with active sensors.
These limitations point to several directions for future work, including richer
optical modeling for light field synthesis, extension to more diverse outdoor or
dynamic scenes, and broader applications of physics-grounded synthetic super-
vision to other light field perception tasks beyond depth estimation.
6
Conclusion
We presented SynLF, a framework for robust single-lens metric depth estima-
tion from light field cameras that combines physics-grounded light field synthe-
sis with visibility-aware iterative disparity estimation. By generating geometry-
consistent supervisory light fields on the fly from large-scale RGB-D data, SynLF
alleviates the scarcity of conventional light field supervision, while the proposed
VisDepth explicitly reasons about multi-view visibility to improve robustness
under occlusion, weak texture, reflections, and defocus. Extensive experiments
on challenging real-world captures demonstrate strong zero-shot generalization
and accurate metric depth estimation without fine-tuning. These results suggest
that physics-grounded light field synthesis from RGB-D provides a scalable and
effective path toward real-world light field depth estimation.
