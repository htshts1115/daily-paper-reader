# Abstract 

We study monocular metric depth estimation (MMDE) with-out camera intrinsics at training or inference. When focal length and scene depth vary together, depth changes are dif-ficult to perceive in the image, yet the edge-frequency statis-tics exhibit systematic, scale-correlated shifts. Building on this observation, we introduce a spectral quantile estima-tor (SQE) that analyzes the Fourier spectrum of a predicted edge map and outputs a single score used as a proxy for metric scale. Consequently, we propose MD2E, a method that models depth-to-edge cues by deriving edge targets from depth annotations, calibrating metric scale using the spectral score, and using edge predictions to regularize depth boundaries while producing metric depth. Across diverse cameras and datasets, MD2E achieves state-of-the-art performance in MMDE in both zero-shot and fine-tuning settings. The project page is available at https: //2j472no.github.io/MD2E/ .

# 1. Introduction 

Monocular depth estimation (MDE), which aims to predict a dense depth map from a single RGB image, serves as a fundamental prerequisite for numerous computer vision and robotics applications. It provides essential 3D scene under-standing for tasks such as autonomous driving [8, 49], aug-mented reality [55, 66], and visual SLAM [23, 59]. Despite the inherent ill-posedness of inferring 3D geometry from monocular cues, recent advances in deep learning have en-abled significant progress by leveraging large-scale datasets and powerful neural architectures. To bridge domain gaps in MDE, MiDaS [42] mixes het-erogeneous supervision with a scale- and shift-invariant objective and principled multi-objective training, allowing learning even when annotations are incompatible across modalities and ranges. This re-solves scale ambiguity and enables robust Monocular Met-ric Depth Estimation (MMDE). In this paper, we present an MMDE approach that pre-dicts metric depth from a single image without camera pa-rameters at training or inference. As illustrated in Figure 1, RGB appearance can remain nearly invariant under focal length and depth coupling for a fixed target, whereas the corresponding edge map shows systematic changes in the distribution of edge energy across spatial frequencies, in-cluding apparent edge thickness. We leverage this cue with a spectral quantile estimator (SQE) that computes a scalar score tpred from the edge map by analyzing its Fourier spec-trum: a smooth radial high-pass is applied, energy is ag-gregated into a differentiable radial histogram, and a soft quantile of the cumulative radial energy is extracted. This score serves as a scale proxy that calibrates the predicted depth, converting relative estimates to metric units and en-abling accurate MMDE from a single image. Motivated by the observation that the edge spectral-quantile score tpred provides scale cues not evident in RGB, we propose MD2E, which models depth-to-edge cues to train monocular metric depth without camera parameters. Specifically, MD2E converts dense depth annotations into edge supervision, predicts an edge map, and uses an SQE to compute tpred ; this score calibrates the metric scale, while the edge predictions regularize depth to preserve sharp boundaries. Experiments show that MD2E yields more ac-curate metric depth than methods that learn camera settings and achieves state-of-the-art results in both zero-shot and fine-tuning settings. In summary, our core contributions are as follows: • We propose MD2E, a monocular metric depth estimation paradigm that models depth-to-edge cues to train without camera parameters. Concretely, MD2E converts dense depth annotations into edge supervision, predicts an edge map, and uses a SQE to compute the edge score tpred for metric-scale calibration. The edge predictions regularize depth to preserve sharp boundaries. • We show that the spectral-quantile edge cue adapts across different camera settings and scene types, enabling the network to capture camera-induced edge variations. • Extensive experiments in both zero-shot and fine-tuning settings demonstrate that MD2E attains state-of-the-art performance in monocular metric depth estimation. 

# 2. Related work 

2.1. In-domain MDE 

Early MDE primarily followed an in-domain protocol: models were trained and evaluated on a single dataset, with objectives tailored to that dataset’s distribution and annotation scheme. Architecture-centric methods [36] strengthened encoder-decoder designs to preserve detail and suppress artifacts. Discretization-based parameteriza-tions cast depth into ordered bins to stabilize optimiza-tion and sharpen boundaries, including ordinal regression with non-uniform bins [15], image-adaptive binning [3], interval-estimation/bin refinement [46], and locally adap-tive bins [4]. Transformer-based models improved global context aggregation within the same in-domain setting, such as NewCRFs [63] coupling transformer features with CRF reasoning, DepthFormer [30] with multi-scale attention, and BinsFormer [31] with tokenized bin representations. Recently, in-domain fine-tuning on the target dataset has become an important evaluation protocol for state-of-the-art MDE methods, as adopted by Depth Anything V2 [61], UniDepthV2 [41], and Metric3D v2 [22]. 

2.2. Robust MDE 

Robust MDE has transitioned from single-dataset train-ing to heterogeneous supervision and cross-dataset gen-eralization. Early efforts used SfM/MVS reconstructions from Internet photo collections to obtain large but noisy supervision [29], and leveraged relative-depth annotations in unconstrained images to broaden scene coverage [10], yet dataset bias persisted. MiDaS [42] mixes heteroge-neous datasets with a scale- and shift-invariant objective under multi-objective training, achieving strong zero-shot transfer across indoor and outdoor domains; DPT [43] keeps this recipe with transformer backbones and improves the accuracy-efficiency trade-off. Building on this line, Depth Anything [60] scales data and training to foundation-style models, further strengthening zero-shot generalization while remaining competitive under fine-tuning. Construct-ing large-scale datasets through mixed training that com-bines multiple MDE datasets is an effective strategy for achieving robust MDE. 

2.3. Zero-shot MMDE 

Scale- and shift-invariant losses improve cross-dataset ro-bustness but remove absolute scale, so models general-ize only in relative depth. This gap motivates zero-shot MMDE, which predicts metric depth on unseen data with-out adaptation. One line of work recovers scale by canon-icalizing cameras under the pinhole model and restoring it with known intrinsics [62]. Larger variants keep this as-sumption while improving robustness [22]. To remove the need for camera parameter inputs, Depth Pro [6] integrates a 5773 focal-length prediction module into the model and learns it under supervision from camera annotations. Another line learns dense camera representations to infer metric scale from a single image [40, 41] and geometry-aware mappings under unknown cameras [20]. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to employ edge maps as a cue for zero-shot MMDE. 

2.4. Sharp MDE 

Sharp MDE requires precise predictions on thin structures and clean depth discontinuities. Wang et al. [54] intro-duced the normalized multiscale gradient loss, which en-forces agreement of disparity gradients and achieves shift as well as scale invariance in disparity space. MiDaS [42] augments a scale- and shift-invariant objective with a multi-scale gradient-matching term, and Depth Anything V2 [61] adopts a related gradient-matching loss, yielding crisper boundaries and finer detail. UniDepthV2 [41] introduces an edge-guided loss that enhances edge localization and sharpness in metric depth outputs. Diffusion-based meth-ods [24, 38] leverage generative priors to supply missing fine structure when supervision is sparse. Increasing spa-tial resolution [6, 35] effectively improves the fine-grained details of depth predictions and yields sharper results. In this paper, we supervise the model with dense depth anno-tations to learn a depth-aware edge predictor, and further leverage the predicted edges as supervision to obtain sharp depth predictions. 

2.5. Edge cues in MDE 

Edge cues are widely used to sharpen boundaries and con-strain discontinuities in MDE. Early self-supervised meth-ods introduced edge-aware regularizers that weight smooth-ness by image gradients to preserve object borders [18]. Subsequent works made the role of edges explicit: Zhu et al. [67] enforced consistency between segmentation bound-aries and depth discontinuities to regularize border regions; Xian et al. [56] proposed an edge-guided sampling strat-egy within a structure-guided ranking loss to emphasize fine structures; Talker et al. [50] learned a depth-edge predic-tor from dense synthetic supervision to refine edges under sparse LiDAR labels; Li et al. [28] detected RGB edges and fed the resulting edge maps to a fusion model that aggre-gates depths from multiple sources. In contrast to previous approaches, we convert dense depth annotations into edge maps and use these depth-derived edges for supervision, which avoids noise from high-frequency textures and miti-gates artifacts caused by missing or incomplete depth labels in edge regions while retaining the benefits of edge-aware guidance. 

# 3. Method 

This section presents MD2E for accurate and sharp MMDE. As shown in Figure 2, a depth-to-edge transformation con-verts depth annotations into edge annotations to supervise edge prediction. The model outputs an edge map and an initial depth map. The predicted edge map is fed to a spec-tral quantile estimator to obtain an edge score for calibrat-ing the predicted depth to metric scale. In addition, the predicted edge map is used to supervise depth prediction at boundaries, which yields sharper object contours in the depth map.

3.2. Spectral quantile estimator 

To capture the effects of camera focal-length changes from an edge map and account for scene variations in depth cal-ibration, we propose the spectral quantile estimator (SQE). Focal length alters the image formation scale: when focal length changes, the same scene structure projects to differ-ent pixel spans, shifting edge thickness and the distribution of edge energy across spatial frequencies [32, 65]. Vari-ations in scene layout, for example, indoor environments with dense man-made edges and repeated fine structures versus outdoor scenes dominated by large planar facades or open ground, also redistribute edge energy across radial spatial frequencies, which in turn affects monocular metric depth calibration. # 6. Conclusion 

We present MD2E for MMDE by modeling depth-to-edge cues. The method first converts dense depth annotations into edge maps and uses these maps to supervise edge pre-diction. From the predicted edge map, an SQE computes an edge score that calibrates metric depth. Because edge pat-terns vary with camera focal length and scene layout, this score captures both camera and scene shifts and enables ac-curate metric-depth calibration. Meanwhile, the edge map yields sharper depth boundaries. Across zero-shot and fine-tuning settings, MD2E attains state-of-the-art MMDE per-formance.
