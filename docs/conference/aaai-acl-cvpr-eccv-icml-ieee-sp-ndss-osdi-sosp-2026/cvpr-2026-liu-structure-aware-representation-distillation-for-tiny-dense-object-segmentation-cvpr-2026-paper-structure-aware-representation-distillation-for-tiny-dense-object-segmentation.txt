# Abstract 

Dense scenes containing numerous tiny objects pose a fun-damental challenge for segmentation models, where small localization errors can significantly degrade downstream measurements. We present Structure-Aware Representa-tion Distillation (SARD), a teacher-compatible framework that transfers structural knowledge from a large teacher to a compact student via feature-space alignment rather than mask imitation. SARD builds a structure-importance map by integrating boundary salience, geometric complex-ity, and local feature variation, and uses it to guide a unified representation loss that combines feature consis-tency with distribution alignment. This encourages the student to allocate capacity to geometrically informative regions while preserving global context. Experiments on Cityscapes, ADE20K, and a challenging rock fragmenta-tion benchmark (RockFrag) show that SARD consistently improves both mIoU and boundary IoU over strong dis-tillation baselines; on RockFrag, SARD improves a Swin-T student over CWD by +4.3 mIoU and +6.7 bIoU. A ResNet-50 student distilled from a Swin-L teacher achieves up to 7.7 × parameter reduction and 9 × higher through-put than the teacher, with no additional inference overhead beyond the student network, demonstrating that structure-aware representation distillation is effective and efficient for tiny-dense segmentation. Code is available at: https: //github.com/liuuuuuuxuesong/SARD .

# 1. Introduction 

Accurate segmentation of scenes containing many tiny and densely packed objects remains a fundamental challenge in computer vision. Applications ranging from medi-cal microscopy [20] and remote sensing [14] to industrial inspection depend on precise delineation of small struc-tures, where even a one-pixel deviation can significantly al-ter downstream measurements. Recent foundation models such as SAM [7] and Mask2Former [3] demonstrate strong generalization, yet remain computationally expensive. Ef-ficient variants including FastSAM [22], MobileSAM [19], and EfficientSAM [16] reduce computational cost but often underperform on domains dominated by tiny-scale patterns. Developing lightweight models that retain structural fidelity is therefore essential for practical deployment. Traditional knowledge distillation strategies [6] focus on mimicking the teacher’s output masks or intermediate log-its. Recent advances for dense prediction [9, 12] extend this through pixel-wise alignment, while others explore de-coupled logit distillation [21], masked generation [17], and multi-layer review [1]. While effective for coarse bound-aries, these approaches fail to capture subtle structural cues critical for dense regions. Consider rock fragmentation analysis, where thousands of irregular fragments must be individually segmented: uniform distillation devotes equal attention to fragment interiors and boundaries where touch-ing rocks separate, causing students to learn coarse seman-tic alignment while losing boundary fidelity. This failure mode is pervasive across tiny-dense scenarios. Existing methods assume uniform information density, treating all spatial locations equally despite fundamental differences in geometric complexity. We argue that successful transfer for tiny-dense objects requires structure-aware representation alignment rather than output imitation. While specialized architectures for boundaries [5, 10, 15] and multi-scale processing [8, 13] improve geometric precision, they require task-specific de-signs that limit generalizability. Instead of matching masks directly, the student should learn to reproduce the geome-try of the teacher’s feature space, where fine-scale edges, boundaries, and region density are encoded. This perspec-tive reframes distillation as a representation learning prob-lem: preserving spatial structure and local information flow, independent of specific teacher architectures. Crucially, not all locations contribute equally. Boundaries between touch-ing instances and crowded regions demand substantially more representational precision than homogeneous interi-ors. SARD constructs a structure-importance map combining boundary salience, structural complexity, and local feature variation, and uses it to weight representation-level distillation losses. Rather than model-ing edges or regions in separate stages, SARD embeds these cues within the representation space itself, allowing the stu-dent to allocate capacity to structurally informative regions. This yields improved precision on tiny objects and sharper boundary delineation without architecture constraints or ad-ditional inference modules. Our main contributions are as follows: • A structure-aware representation distillation framework that emphasizes boundaries and dense regions, address-ing the spatial non-uniformity inherent in tiny-dense seg-mentation. • A unified formulation combining feature consistency and distribution alignment under structure-aware weight-ing, applicable to diverse teacher–student pairs without architecture-specific modifications. • Comprehensive validation showing consistent improve-ments across challenging scenarios. On our RockFrag benchmark, for example, SARD improves a Swin-T stu-dent over CWD [12] by +4.3 mIoU and +6.7 bIoU, high-lighting its benefit on boundary-critical tiny-dense seg-mentation. Extensive experiments on urban scenes (Cityscapes [4]), scene parsing (ADE20K [23]), and rock fragmentation (RockFrag) demonstrate that structure-aware representation alignment consistently improves geometric precision across architectures and compression ratios. 

# 2. Related Work 

## 2.1. Knowledge Distillation for Dense Prediction 

Knowledge distillation [6] transfers knowledge from large teacher models to compact students by matching output distributions. While early methods focus on logit dis-tillation [6] or intermediate features [11, 18], recent ad-vances address the unique challenges of dense prediction tasks. Structured knowledge distillation [9] transfers pixel-wise and pair-wise relations for semantic segmentation. Channel-wise distillation (CWD) [12] normalizes activation maps into probability distributions and minimizes KL di-vergence to focus on salient regions. Other recent methods explore decoupling target and non-target knowledge [21], masked feature reconstruction [17], and multi-layer re-view [1]. However, these methods apply uniform spatial weighting, treating boundaries and interiors equally despite their vastly different importance for tiny and densely packed objects. 

## 2.2. Compact Models for Segmentation 

Deploying segmentation models on resource-constrained devices has motivated various compression strategies. Foundation models like SAM [7] achieve impressive zero-shot generalization but remain computationally expensive. Recent efforts including FastSAM [22], MobileSAM [19], and EfficientSAM [16] reduce parameters through architecture-specific modifications. Mask2Former [3] uses masked attention for universal segmentation, while boundary-focused methods [5, 10, 15] and multi-scale architectures [8, 13] improve geometric precision through specialized designs. These approaches achieve efficiency through either architecture engineering or task-specific components that limit their generalizability across different teacher-student configurations. 

Our approach. We observe that existing distillation methods overlook a fundamental characteristic: not all spa-tial locations are equally important for tiny-dense segmen-tation. We propose SARD that dynamically emphasizes boundaries and crowded regions where geometric preci-sion matters most, providing a general framework appli-cable to diverse teacher-student pairs without architecture constraints. 

# 3. Method 

Standard knowledge distillation transfers representations uniformly across spatial locations, learning feature spaces optimized for general objects. However, tiny-dense ob-ject segmentation requires features specialized for geomet-ric complexity: sharp boundaries where instances contact, multi-faceted surfaces, and tightly packed spatial distribu-tions. SARD shifts the learned feature space toward these characteristics by weighting representation alignment ac-cording to local structural importance, guiding the student to prioritize boundaries, junctions, and crowded regions where uniform distillation is insufficient. Figure 1 sum-marizes the overall architecture: a frozen teacher provides feature maps, a structure-importance map W (i) is derived from these signals, and structure-weighted representation losses are applied to the student, together with a standard segmentation loss when labels are available.

# 4. Experiments 

We evaluate SARD on tiny-dense object segmentation across standard benchmarks and an industrial rock fragmen-tation dataset. Our experiments focus on three aspects: (1) comparison with existing distillation methods in terms of mIoU and boundary IoU, (2) the effect of structure-aware weighting through controlled ablations, and (3) how SARD behaves with compact students and diverse teacher–student architectures, including promptable and non-promptable models.

## 4.2. Comparison with Distillation Baselines 

Table 1 reports the main comparisons with knowledge dis-tillation baselines on Cityscapes, ADE20K, and RockFrag using same-backbone Swin teacher–student pairs. Table 2 evaluates cross-architecture teacher–student combinations, and Fig. 2 provides qualitative examples. 

Consistent gains on standard benchmarks. On Cityscapes and ADE20K, SARD improves over all logit-and feature-level KD baselines for both the Swin-L →Swin-T and Swin-B →Swin-S settings. For the stronger compres-sion setting with Swin-L as teacher and Swin-T as student, SARD attains 80.3% mIoU on Cityscapes and 46.9% mIoU on ADE20K, surpassing CWD by +1.4 and +2.1 mIoU, respectively. With Swin-B as teacher and Swin-S as stu-dent, SARD still yields improvements (80.1% vs. 79.4% mIoU on Cityscapes, 47.1% vs. 46.0% on ADE20K), in-dicating that structure-aware representation transfer is ben-eficial even under milder compression. 

Boundary-localized improvements. Across all datasets, gains in boundary IoU (bIoU) exceed those in mIoU. On Cityscapes, SARD improves bIoU from 73.8% (CWD) to 76.1% (+2.3), compared to a +1.4 mIoU gain. A similar pattern appears on ADE20K (+2.2 bIoU vs. +2.1 mIoU), and for Swin-B →Swin-S (+1.0 / +1.4 bIoU vs. +0.7 / +1.1 mIoU on Cityscapes/ADE20K). This consistent behavior supports the design goal that structure-aware weighting pri-marily enhances geometric precision rather than only region overlap. 

Tiny-dense RockFrag segmentation. RockFrag is the most boundary-critical benchmark in our evaluation, con-taining thousands of touching fragments per image. With Swin-L as teacher and Swin-T as student, SARD improves the student from 55.9%/40.6% (mIoU/bIoU with CWD) to 60.2%/47.3%, i.e., +4.3 mIoU and +6.7 bIoU. With Swin-B as teacher and Swin-S as student, SARD reaches 59.6% mIoU and 46.5% bIoU versus 57.8%/42.8% for CWD (+1.8 / +3.7). These boundary gains are particularly important for rock fragmentation analysis, where accurate separa-tion of tiny fragments directly affects downstream size-distribution estimates. Qualitative examples in Fig. 2 show that SARD reduces merge/split errors and sharpens small-object boundaries on both Cityscapes and RockFrag. 

Architecture diversity. Table 2 further shows that SARD generalizes across heterogeneous teacher–student pairs, including ViT-L →ViT-T, SAM-H →EfficientSAM-Ti, and Mask2Former-L →M2F-R50. Without changing the loss, SARD delivers average improvements of +1.5–+1.8 mIoU and +2.3–+2.7 bIoU over CWD, with RockFrag bIoU gains of +5.5–+6.2. Together with the Swin experiments, these results suggest that structure-aware representation distilla-tion generalizes across architectures rather than acting as an architecture-specific heuristic. 

## 4.3. Effect of Structure-Aware Weighting 

Table 3 isolates the contribution of structure-aware weight-ing on RockFrag using Swin-L as teacher and ResNet-50 as student. 

Representation distillation as a baseline. Logit-level KD achieves 52.3% mIoU and 36.9% bIoU. Replacing logits with the representation objective in Eq. 10, while using uniform weighting W (i) = 1 , improves performance to 53.8% mIoU and 38.5% bIoU. This indicates that feature-level transfer already provides a strong baseline, and the uniform variant serves as a controlled reference for eval-uating structure-aware weighting. 

Benefits of structure-aware weights. Introducing the full structure-aware weighting yields 58.6% mIoU and 45.2% bIoU, corresponding to +4.8 / +6.7 gains over uniform SARD and +6.3 / +8.3 over vanilla KD. The larger abso-lute improvement in bIoU suggests that emphasizing struc-turally important locations primarily enhances geometric accuracy along boundaries. 

Complementary structure components. Ablating the components of the structure score S(i) = βeE(i) + 

βcC(i) + βdD(i) reveals complementary roles. Boundary-only weighting ( W ∝ E) attains 56.2% mIoU and 42.8% bIoU, density-only weighting ( W ∝ D) yields 55.4% / 41.6%, and curvature-only weighting ( W ∝ C) achieves 55.8% / 42.1%. Pairwise combinations further improve performance (e.g., E+C reaches 57.8% / 44.6%), and the full E+C+D setting performs best. These trends indicate that geometric complexity (edges and curvature) and spatial crowding (density) capture distinct aspects of structure that are most effective when optimized jointly.

# 5. Conclusion 

We introduced Structure-Aware Representation Distillation (SARD), which uses a structure-importance map combining boundary salience, structural complexity, and local feature variation to focus feature-level distillation on geometrically critical regions. Experiments on Cityscapes, ADE20K, and RockFrag show consistent improvements over strong distil-lation baselines, with especially large gains in boundary IoU on tiny-dense rock fragmentation.
