# Seeing Through Blur: Tackling Defocus in Spike-Based Imaging 

## Xiantao Ma 2,* Siwei Dong 3,* Lin Zhu 1,2,† Lizhi Wang 1 Hua Huang 11Beijing Normal University 2Beijing Institute of Technology 3Peking University 

## Abstract 

Spike cameras are a novel class of neuromorphic vision sensors that capture scene dynamics with ultra-high tem-poral resolution via spike planes. While recent methods have addressed motion blur and noise in spike-based re-construction, defocus blur caused by shallow depth of field or lens adjustment delays remains a critical yet underex-plored issue in real-world applications such as autonomous driving. In this work, we present DeSpike, the first end-to-end defocus removal framework specifically designed for spike cameras. Our method begins by explicitly model-ing the defocus formation process using a physics-inspired thin-lens approximation to simulate spike responses under optical blur. Guided by this formulation, DeSpike em-ploys multi-temporal-scale integrate-and-fire (IF) neurons to compensate for FPN and extract defocus-aware features from spike streams. These features are then processed by a physics-informed deblurring module constructed from learnable discrete PSF priors. To address spatially vari-ant blur, we introduce a Transformer-based fusion mecha-nism that adaptively weighs multi-scale deblurring results through attention across defocus levels. Finally, a coarse-to-fine iterative refinement stage combines spike features and PSF priors for progressive restoration. Extensive ex-periments on both synthetic and real-world defocused spike datasets demonstrate that our method achieves superior performance over state-of-the-art deblurring approaches in terms of structural fidelity, perceptual sharpness, and con-trast, setting a new benchmark for defocus-aware spike-based image reconstruction. 

## 1. Introduction 

Neuromorphic vision sensors have emerged as a promis-ing alternative to traditional frame-based cameras, inspired by the efficiency and adaptability of biological visual sys-tems. Among them, event cameras [12, 14, 18] and spike cameras [2, 3, 8] represent two complementary paradigms. Event cameras asynchronously record changes in logarith-

In contrast, spike cameras adopt an integrate-and-fire mechanism that directly encodes absolute intensity changes as temporal spike trains, enabling not only extreme temporal resolution (up to 40,000 Hz) but also the ability to capture fine-grained intensity details under chal-lenging lighting and motion conditions [37]. While both sensors share neuromorphic properties, spike cameras differ fundamentally by emitting spike planes that encode absolute scene intensity across time, provid-ing rich spatiotemporal cues for downstream vision tasks. These characteristics make spike cameras particularly at-tractive for high-speed, low-latency applications such as au-tonomous driving, robotics, and dynamic scene reconstruc-tion [13, 17]. However, effectively reconstructing images from spike streams remains a core challenge. Prior research has pri-marily focused on mitigating motion blur [35] and suppress-ing sensor noise [40]. Yet, a critical degradation, defocus blur, remains largely unaddressed. In real-world scenar-ios, defocus arises from lens refocusing delays or shallow depth-of-field effects under dynamic conditions [32]. Un-like motion blur, which is a temporal distortion often alle-viated by the high sampling rate of spike sensors, defocus blur originates from optical dispersion at the sensor level, fundamentally altering the spatial distribution of incoming photons and thus the resulting spiking behavior. This makes defocus particularly damaging and difficult to recover using conventional temporal-domain techniques. Although several autofocus methods [22, 26] have re-cently been developed for spike cameras, they typically rely on hand-crafted metrics or iterative optimization, which are significantly more complex and less generalizable com-pared to mature autofocus techniques in traditional frame-based cameras. Moreover, these methods cannot correct or recover information from already-defocused spike streams, limiting their applicability in practical downstream tasks such as reconstruction or perception. To fill this gap, we propose DeSpike, the first end-to-end defocus removal framework specifically designed for spike cameras (see Fig. 1). ## 2. Related Work 

Traditional Image Defocus Deblur. Existing research on defocus deblurring can be categorized into two technical pathways. Early approaches typically adopted a two-stage method [5, 9, 19, 24, 34] involving defocus map estimation and subsequent restoration [5, 9, 16, 34]. Recent advances demonstrate the superiority of deep learning over conven-tional methods. Abuolaim et al. [1] pioneers the end-to-end DPDNet for defocus deblurring. Son et al. [25] enhances spatial-variant kernel adaptability through kernel-sharing parallel atrous convolutions, while Lee et al. [10] propose iterative filter adaptation networks. Quan et al. introduce GKMNet [20], which physically models defocus blur us-ing predefined Gaussian kernels of discrete sizes and learns mixing coefficients through scale-recurrent attention mod-ules. Their subsequent work further improves efficiency by representing 2D symmetric kernels through 1D dictio-nary learning and replacing deconvolution with truncated Neumann series approximation [21]. Tang et al. [28] en-hance the fitting capability of the deblurring network by decomposing blur kernels into regular and irregular com-ponents. However, existing deep learning-based defocus removal methods exclusively target traditional frame-based cameras, while research on defocus removal for spike cam-eras remains unexplored. 

Spike-based Reconstruction. Image reconstruction has long been a focal research topic in spike camera-based vi-sion tasks. Early approaches leveraged the physical charac-teristics of spike streams to achieve image restoration. Zhu et al. [37] propose the TFP method, which estimates light intensity by counting spike occurrences within a fixed time window. To address its motion artifacts in dynamic sce-narios, they further developed the TFI method [37], which calculates the reciprocal of inter-spike intervals to repre-sent scene brightness, thereby adapting to dynamic environ-ments. Inspired by the structure of the human retina, they further proposed a foveated spike reconstruction architec-ture for image restoration [38]. To meet the reconstruction demands of complex dynamic scenes, Zhao et al. [35] intro-duce the first end-to-end neural network, Spk2Imgnet. Zhu et al. [39] propose NeuSpike-Net, which enhances recon-struction quality by fusing complementary DVS and Spike camera data. Zhang et al. [31] propose WGSE, which lever-ages wavelet transforms to extract robust representations from spike streams. Zhao’s BSF et al. [36] enhance recon-struction performance in high dynamic range (HDR) sce-narios by block-to-pixel alignment through multi-order dif-19677 ferencing and multi-granularity matching. Zhu et al. [40] propose RSIR through comprehensive modeling of multi-ple noise types in spike cameras, significantly mitigating the impact of noise on spike-based image reconstruction quality. However, while these methods effectively suppress factors such as motion blur and sensor noise, they fail to achieve high-quality reconstruction in defocus scenarios. 

Spike Camera Autofocus. Several methods [22, 26] have recently explored autofocus for spike cameras, typically by optimizing predefined sharpness metrics across spike planes or regions. For example, some approaches compute spike-based focus scores to guide lens adjustment in real time. While promising, these methods are often limited by com-putational complexity and lack the generality and speed of autofocus systems in conventional cameras. More impor-tantly, they are fundamentally preventive: they aim to avoid defocus during acquisition but cannot restore already defo-cus spike streams. Consequently, they are unsuitable for post-hoc reconstruction or downstream vision tasks when defocus has already occurred. 

## 3. Physics-based Spike Defocus Imaging Model 

3.1. Spike Sampling in Neuromorphic Cameras 

Spike cameras are neuromorphic vision sensors that mimic biological visual systems by continuously integrating in-coming light at each pixel and emitting discrete spikes once a threshold is reached. Unlike frame-based cameras that sample the entire scene at fixed intervals, or event cam-eras that capture changes in brightness, spike cameras pro-vide flexible intensity measurements with ultra-high tempo-ral resolution (e.g., up to 40,000 frames per second) and fine spatial granularity. Each pixel in a spike camera contains a photodiode and an integrate-and-fire circuit (as shown in Fig. 2) [27]. When exposed to light, the photodiode generates photocurrent proportional to the instantaneous light intensity. This cur-rent is integrated over time until the voltage on a capacitor exceeds a predefined firing threshold. When this happens, a spike is emitted and the pixel is reset. Mathematically, the accumulation process is represented as:

Due to its asynchronous, pixel-level sampling, spike camera is naturally robust to motion blur and illumination variation. However, the sampling is sparse in time and subject to en-vironmental distortions, including defocus. When the lens is not perfectly focused, photons from a scene point are no longer tightly concentrated at a single pixel but dispersed across a region. This results in a lower rate of intensity accumulation at any single pixel, delay-ing or even suppressing spike firing. The consequence is distorted spike streams that no longer faithfully reflect the original scene structure, especially in regions with shallow depth-of-field. Therefore, a physically meaningful model of how defocus affects spike sampling is critical for effective reconstruction.

However, when the scene is out of focus, the light from a point source is dispersed across neighboring pixels due to the formation of a circular point spread function (PSF). As described in Section 3.2, this spatial spreading reduces the photon density at each individual pixel. In the context of the IF model, this means that each pixel now accumu-lates light more slowly, resulting in delayed or even missing spike emissions. Formally, if the incoming light at a focused pixel is 

I(x, y, t ), then under defocus, the effective integrated in-put becomes (I ∗ G)( x, y, t ), where G denotes the Gaussian approximation of the PSF. As shown in Eq. 2, the spike out-put depends on the temporal integral of this blurred signal. When the energy in the spread-out signal does not reach the firing threshold θ, no spike is generated. Despite preserving global photon energy, defocus fun-damentally reshapes the spike emission behavior in both spatial and temporal domains. Through the lens of the integrate-and-fire model, we identify the following core mechanisms and implications: 

1) Local Integration Imbalance: Defocus blur acts as a spatial low-pass filter, redistributing localized intensity across neighborhoods. This introduces an imbalance in the per-pixel energy accumulation rate—pixels that were previ-ously dominant in brightness now accumulate more slowly due to diffusion, while neighboring darker pixels may ben-efit from energy inflow. However, this redistribution is gov-erned by the local gradient field and the kernel support of the PSF, leading to spatially non-uniform integration rates. 

2) Non-monotonic Temporal Perturbation: Unlike motion blur or temporal noise, defocus does not introduce a uniform delay. Instead, it causes non-monotonic perturba-tion in the spike timing field: some spikes are delayed due to local attenuation, others are advanced due to cross-pixel energy inflow. This breaks the temporal order consistency that is typically assumed in spiking pipelines, introducing temporal aliasing effects and uncertainty. 

3) Structure-Dependent Temporal Distortion: The severity and polarity of temporal shifts depend strongly on the local structural context. High-gradient edge regions are more prone to temporal advancement at boundaries and de-lay in flat interiors, leading to temporal shear within spa-tially coherent regions. This results in spike planes where object contours are temporally skewed and spatial features are misaligned across time. These effects expose fundamental limitations in naive temporal integration or motion aggregation strategies. Net-works trained under focused assumptions fail to general-ize to defocused spike streams due to the non-local and asymmetric nature of temporal shifts. Therefore, a defocus-aware modeling framework must not only correct spatial blur but also explicitly align or reconstruct temporally dis-torted activation patterns to restore perceptual consistency.

Multi-Temporal-Scale Neuronal Integration. Motivated by the spike-level statistical insights in Sec. 3, we intro-duce multi-temporal-scale neuronal integration to jointly model early and delayed spike responses caused by spa-tially varying defocus. Our module is implemented us-ing non-spiking neurons, which accumulate spike inputs through differentiable membrane integration rather than dis-crete firing events. This module enables unified training across multiple temporal scales, addressing local integra-tion imbalance, non-monotonic perturbation, and structure-dependent temporal shear through IF-based accumulation and normalization. This includes local integration imbal-ance, non-monotonic perturbation, and temporal shear ob-served in spike planes. These neurons accumulate spike in-put over discrete time windows {T1, T 2, . . . , T n}, allowing us to capture early and delayed spike responses induced by different degrees of blur. Formally, the integrated feature for each window is given by: 

Fi = SN (S(Ti)) , i = 1 , 2, . . . , n, (3) where S(Ti) is the input spike sequence within window Ti,and SN denotes a spike normalization or encoding layer. In our network, n is set to 5, with Ti values of 64, 96, 128, 160, and 192, respectively. This module produces a feature hierarchy that encodes both sharp and blurred structures at varying temporal scales. To further enhance feature fusion, we introduce a trans-former attention mechanism that weights different kernel outputs according to their contextual relevance. This en-ables adaptive combination of PSF-corrected features for diverse spatial regions.

## 6. Conclusion 

In this work, we propose the first end-to-end network for defocus spike reconstruction. Our framework leverages a differentially sampled spike plane design and a multi-spatiotemporal scale architecture, integrating physical prior modules with a transformer-based fusion mechanism to achieve hierarchical and progressive refocusing of defocus spikes. Both qualitative and quantitative results on synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate that our model delivers superior performance with strong robustness.
