## Abstract 

In-Context Learning (ICL) has shown great effectiveness in developing generalist image segmentation models. Its sig-nificant advantage over text-based descriptions is the ability to convey intricate visual appearance details through sim-ple reference images. However, finding a perfectly matching single example for real-world rare and complex concepts is difficult. Moreover, existing methods are largely confined to semantic or instance-level understanding of the refer-ence image, struggling to express more precise segmenta-tion needs through the input. To address this, we propose 

CDICS , a novel framework that leverages Compositional prompts and phased task Decoupling to achieve compo-sitional prompt-controlled In-Context Segmentation. Our method introduces compositional prompts derived from ref-erence prompts, combining semantic, part and color im-ages to dynamically define segmentation targets. To effec-tively fuse this control information, ensure synergy while suppressing interference, and mitigate feature coupling risks, our decoupled two-stage architecture firstly performs coarse-grained semantic localization, then refines the result using compositional appearance prompts to precisely match the specified attributes. This design extends traditional in-context segmentation, enabling it to support compositional prompts. Additionally, we reconstructed two datasets and their benchmarks to acquire data with part-color-specific attributes. Our method demonstrates superior performance on the compositional prompt-controlled in-context segmen-tation task. It also extends the capabilities of existing in-context segmentation, and makes an attempt toward real-world fine-grained segmentation.

(2) First-stage Decoding : Based on the semantic prompt, we generate and aggregate instance-level masks to derive a coarse semantic map that bootstraps the second-stage decoding. (3) Second-stage Decoding : We input the color-similarity-map-enhanced target image fea-tures and the part features into an Appearance Fusion Mod-ule. In our architecture, the first stage learns generic object 

concepts, while the second stage employs the Appearance Fusion Module to integrate part and color prompts. The de-coupled representations are then recomposed through itera-tive fusion, achieving the fine-grained segmentation. This coarse-to-fine strategy not only preserves the global local-ization ability provided by semantic guidance, but also con-siders part and color cues at a detailed level, enabling pre-cise prompt-based segmentation. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • Compositional Prompt Mechanism : We introduce, for the first time, a compositional prompt scheme that inte-grates object semantics, part morphology, and color at-tributes into a segmentation network, offering flexible and fine-grained target descriptions. We also design a unified Appearance Fusion Module to enhance sensitivity to fine-grained appearance attributes. • Two-stage Fusion Architecture : We propose a “coarse semantic → fine refinement” pipeline, first filtering target categories via semantic prompts, and then refining seg-mentation by combining user-specified part and color at-tributes to get the user-required segmentation results. • Leading Performance in Precision : Our model demon-strates leading performance on compositional segmen-tation tasks. Extensive experiments show that it sub-stantially reduces the false positive rate for fine-grained attribute queries, while maintaining highly competitive performance on conventional in-context segmentation benchmarks. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Image Segmentation 

Image segmentation is a fundamental problem in com-puter vision, aiming to assign a semantic label to ev-ery pixel in an input image. Early image segmenta-tion predominantly relied on convolutional neural net-works (CNNs), with milestones like FCN [21], U-Net [29], and Mask R-CNN [8] establishing foundational encoder-decoder and region-proposal architectures. Subsequently, Transformer-based architectures such as SegFormer [41] and Mask2Former [2] advanced the field by unifying de-tection and segmentation frameworks with superior perfor-mance. Recently, the paradigm has shifted from closed-set recognition towards open-world perception. Contemporary methods increasingly leverage large-scale foundation mod-els [4, 14, 42, 45] to extract highly generalizable features for complex, real-world segmentation tasks. 

2.2. Vision In-Context Learning 

In-Context Learning (ICL), originating from natural lan-guage processing [1, 5, 38], enables models to perform new tasks without updating weights, relying solely on inference-time example pairs (“contexts” or “prompts”). Vision ICL extends this paradigm to computer vision tasks, often using reference image-mask pairs as prompts to guide the model in segmenting a query image [23, 31, 32, 34, 35]. Early methods like Painter [34] and SegGPT [35] cast segmenta-tion as mask painting or coloring. Recent approaches lever-age foundation models: Matcher [19] computes semantic correspondences for SAM [28] using DINOv2 [24], while SINE [20] enhances contextual usage via prompt fusion and feature alignment. Despite progress in conventional seg-mentation, existing ICL methods underutilize the interac-tive potential of visual sample prompts and continue to face challenges in handling segmentation with more fine-grained appearance requirements. Our work introduces a novel compositional in-context segmentation framework that inte-grates semantic, part, and color cues to achieve fine-grained control over object appearance. 13180 2.3. Reference-Based Segmentation 

Reference Expression Segmentation (RES) aims to seg-ment objects in images based on natural language descrip-tions [17, 18, 36, 40]. Unlike vision ICL, RES methods rely on language models to parse free-form text for ob-ject segmentation. While early works include CMPC [10] and ReSTR [12], recent advancements have increasingly utilized Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) to push the boundaries of referring segmentation by leverag-ing cross-modal reasoning to interpret complex user instruc-tions [13, 27]. However, linguistic vocabulary for color is limited and discrete, leading to ambiguity in color-based scenarios such as e-commerce product matching or indus-trial quality inspection. Users often cannot precisely de-scribe colors verbally, and interpretations of the same color term may vary, lacking a unified standard for mapping color-language semantics. Compared to a pure text or an example-image prompt, our method uses a color image as a visual cue, offering a more user-friendly and consistent ap-proach to representing color semantics across different lin-guistic expressions. 

2.4. Compositional Paradigms in Visual Tasks 

The concept of decomposing images into a combination of elements has been actively explored in other visual do-mains. Compositional Zero-Shot Learning (CZSL) explic-itly disentangles visual primitives to recognize and general-ize to unseen compositions [11, 15]. Recent CZSL methods leverage cross-modal disentanglement and logic rules for precise semantic alignment [6, 39]. Additionally, Attribute-Based Retrieval and Composed Image Retrieval (CIR) use multimodal queries for fine-grained visual control, effec-tively bypassing unimodal constraints [16, 30]. We intro-duce this compositional paradigm into the domain of in-context segmentation. By fully exploiting visual samples as compositional inputs, our framework enhances fine-grained control over segmentation targets and effectively eliminates the reliance on “perfect reference samples”. 

## 3. Method 

3.1. Overview 

To enable precise responses to compositional prompts like “semantic-part-color” and thereby address segmentation tasks that demand fine-grained attribute control at the task-definition level, we propose a decoupled in-context seg-mentation framework (CDICS) . Our core design is to or-thogonally decompose such complex tasks into two inde-pendent sub-problems: semantic identification and appear-ance constraint matching, which are handled by two spe-cialized decoder stages, respectively (as shown in Figure 2). 

Stage 1: Rough Semantic Localization answers the high-level question, “What is the target ? ” by performing a rough spatial localization of all object instances belonging to the specified semantic category. 

Stage 2: Appearance Constraint Refinement operates on the objects identified in Stage 1, answers the fine-grained question, “What does this specific target look like ? ” by applying compositional appearance constraints to precisely match its color and part attributes. This design, by separating high-level semantic iden-tification from fine-grained appearance matching, effec-tively circumvents the challenging issue of feature coupling among different constraints. This allows the model to fo-cus more effectively on learning and applying fine-grained appearance features, thus achieving a precise understanding and segmentation of compositional prompts.

3.3. Decoder Stage 1 

The objective of the first-stage decoder is to perform rough semantic localization. At this stage, the model completely disregards fine-grained details such as part and color, lever-aging only the semantic prototype Fsem ∈ R1×C to locate all potential object instances within the target feature map 

Ftar ∈ Rn×m×C . We implement this process using a trans-former decoder, where a set of learnable instance queries (Qins ∈ RN ×C , with N being the number of instances) interact with the image features to progressively learn the position and shape of each instance. Finally, this stage out-puts a set of rough instance masks and their corresponding semantic labels, which collectively form a solid foundation for the fine-grained analysis in the second stage.

## 4. Experiment 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

4.1.1. Datasets 

To comprehensively evaluate our model’s capabilities, we utilize four datasets, which are uniformly formatted into prompt-target pairs. We specifically annotated and recon-structed two datasets for our compositional task, and also use the standard benchmark for general in-context segmen-tation capabilities. We reconstructed two datasets with fine-grained color annotations to directly supervise and evaluate our model’s ability to handle compositional prompts. 

ColorPACO : We reconstructed ColorPACO by per-forming fine-grained color annotations on the PACO dataset [26]. The original PACO dataset contains 75 ob-ject and 456 object-part categories. For each part instance, we annotated its precise RGB value based on its visual ap-pearance, rather than a discrete color label. 

ColorPartImageNet : To assess the model’s out-of-domain generalization ability, we applied similar color an-notations to the PartImageNet dataset [7]. PartImageNet consists of 158 classes from ImageNet, with several part annotations per class (e.g., Head, Body, Foot, Tail). Images in this dataset typically contain only a single primary object. A key feature of our annotated datasets is the partitioning into positive and negative samples. 1) Positive Samples :The object, part, and color described in the prompt are all present in the target image. 2) Negative Samples : The tar-get image contains an object of the prompted category, but its part or color attributes do not match. For negative sam-ples, the ground-truth mask is all-zero. Positive and nega-tive samples are not generated in pairs but are independently sampled from the dataset at an approximate 3:1 ratio to pro-vide a more holistic and unbiased evaluation of the model’s performance. To ensure fair comparison and evaluate general in-context segmentation abilities under previous settings, we also employ the following standard datasets. 

COCO-Ins : The COCO dataset, comprising 80 “thing” and 53 “stuff” categories, is widely used for various seg-mentation tasks. We utilize its instance segmentation anno-tations (COCO-Ins) to bolster the model’s capacity for fine-grained, instruction-based segmentation in a single stage.

Our training and evaluation pipeline is designed to as-sess both specialized compositional understanding and gen-eral segmentation robustness. For training , we use a mix-ture of ColorPACO and COCO-Ins . ColorPACO pro-vides direct supervision for our primary “semantic-part-color” compositional prompt-controlled in-context segmen-tation task, while the diverse instances in COCO-Ins en-hance the model’s fundamental instance recognition capa-bilities and prevent overfitting to our specific compositional structure. For evaluation , the model is tested on two held-out datasets: ColorPartImageNet and COCO-20 i. Col-orPartImageNet is used to measure out-of-domain general-ization on the compositional task, while COCO-20 i bench-marks the model’s performance on the standard FSS task against prior art. The detailed dataset setup can be found in the Appendix. 

4.1.2. Evaluation Metrics Segmentation Accuracy : For positive samples, we employ the standard Intersection-over-Union (IoU) to measure the overlap between the predicted and ground-truth masks. For instance segmentation, we also report the standard Average Precision (AP) and AP at an IoU threshold of 0.5 (AP 50 ). 

Instruction Discrimination Ability : For negative sam-ples, we use the False Positive Rate (FPR) for evaluation. FPR = FP FP + TN (9) where FP (False Positives) is the number of background pix-els incorrectly predicted as foreground, and TN (True Neg-atives) is the number of background pixels correctly pre-dicted as background. FPR represents the fraction of background pixels that are falsely activated by the model when it is expected to output an empty mask. A lower FPR signifies a superior ability to accurately interpret and reject invalid instructions, rather than segmenting indiscriminately. 

4.2. Main Results and Analysis 

4.2.1. Implementation Details 

Our model is constructed upon the SINE architecture and initialized with its pre-trained weights. The model is jointly trained on the ColorPACO and COCO-Ins datasets with a data sampling ratio of 1:1. We use the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10 −4. The model is trained on 8 NVIDIA A6000 GPUs with a batch size of 160 for 60 epochs.

4.2.3. Ablation Studies 

To validate the effectiveness of the key components in our method, we conducted a series of ablation studies on the ColorPACO dataset (results in Table 3). We first estab-lished a baseline model (Baseline), which only uses the basic encoder when fusing color, part, and semantic fea-tures (achieving 50.3% IoU and 12.0% FPR). Subsequently, we incrementally integrated our two core proposed modules atop this baseline: the Appearance Fusion module and the Two-Stage training strategy. The experimental results unequivocally demonstrate the contribution of each component. After introducing the Ap-pearance Fusion module to the baseline, the model’s per-formance showed a comprehensive improvement (IoU in-creased from 50.3% to 51.4%, and AP 50 rose from 27.7% to 30.8%), while the FPR was also significantly reduced by 2.7% (from 12.0% to 9.3%) Our designed Appearance Fusion module can effectively fuse features that align with the color and part prompts into the coarse-grained semantic features, thereby providing effective fused features for the subsequent precise segmentation. Moreover, adopting the Two-Stage training strategy fur-ther advanced the model’s performance. We attribute this gain to the decoupling of the task, which facilitates a more focused learning process for distinct objectives. This strat-egy enables the model to effectively utilize the features learned in the first stage, providing a strong foundation for the second stage to achieve more precise segmentation. 

## 5. Conclusion 

Addressing the limitations of current in-context segmenta-tion models in understanding fine-grained visual attributes, this paper proposes a novel framework named CDICS. By introducing innovative “Compositional Prompts”, CDICS is the first attempt to integrate object semantics, part morphol-ogy, and color attributes into a unified pipeline, thus achiev-ing a level of flexibility and precision in target description that surpasses previous paradigms. Experimental results demonstrate that our CDICS framework achieves state-of-the-art performance on compositional prompt-driven seg-mentation tasks, significantly outperforming existing meth-ods in both segmentation accuracy and fidelity to instruc-tions. This work extends the paradigm of traditional in-context segmentation and represents a new step toward real-izing more fine-grained, controllable, and interactive visual understanding for real-world applications. 13186 6.
