Abstract
Multi-Exposure Fusion (MEF) aims to generate a high-
quality image from multiple inputs captured at different
exposure levels. Although recent methods have achieved
promising results, most rely on statistical metrics that do
not adequately reflect human perceptual preferences. Elec-
troencephalography (EEG) provides a direct physiological
signal of human cognitive responses, but its use in low-level
vision is limited by scarce paired data and the absence of
bio-signals at inference time.
In this paper, we address
these limitations from both data and method perspectives.
We introduce Cog-Expo, the first dataset of human cognitive
responses to multi-exposure stimuli, which connects neuro-
science and computational photography. We then propose
a bi-level coupled learning framework that uses cognitive
information during training but requires only image inputs
during inference. Specifically, a Mental Integrated Trans-
former serves as the Teacher to learn visual representa-
tions guided by cognitive priors, while a lightweight Stu-
dent is trained to approximate such guidance from image
inputs. Through bi-level optimization, the Teacher learns
representations amenable to distillation, enabling the Stu-
dent to recover cognitive guidance efficiently. Extensive ex-

1. Introduction
Multi-Exposure Fusion (MEF) is a fundamental task in
computational photography that aims to synthesize a single
high-dynamic-range image from multiple inputs captured
at different exposure levels. The primary goal of MEF is
to produce fused images that are visually consistent with
human perception. Although both hand-crafted and deep
learning-based methods have achieved substantial progress,
most existing approaches still depend on statistical metrics
or pixel-level reconstruction losses [22, 23]. Such optimiza-
tion objectives are convenient for training, but they do not
adequately reflect human perceptual preferences. In partic-
ular, they often fail to capture subtle perceptual factors such
as visual comfort, artifact tolerance, and saliency, which
are closely related to the inherent properties of the Human
Visual System (HVS). This limitation highlights the need
to incorporate authentic human cognitive feedback into the
image fusion process.
Recent advances in high-throughput electroencephalog-
raphy (EEG) provide a potential way to introduce human

cognitive feedback into MEF. EEG offers direct and time-
resolved measurements of neural activity, making it pos-
sible to observe how the brain responds to visual stim-
uli with millisecond-level precision [8, 34]. Unlike sub-
jective ratings, EEG provides objective physiological evi-
dence of perception, which makes it particularly suitable
for modeling fine-grained perceptual preferences.
How-
ever, its application to low-level vision tasks such as MEF
still faces two major obstacles. First, existing EEG-vision
datasets [9, 10, 15] mainly target high-level recognition
tasks and do not include exposure-varying stimuli. Conse-
quently, there is still no dataset that establishes a connection
between EEG signals and multi-exposure image sequences.
Second, although EEG can provide valuable cognitive pri-
ors during training, the corresponding signals cannot be col-
lected during inference. Therefore, a central challenge is
how to leverage EEG-based cognitive guidance in training
while preserving an image-only inference pipeline.
To address these limitations, we make two main con-
tributions. First, we construct Cog-Expo, the first EEG-
based cognitive dataset designed for MEF. Built on the
SICE benchmark [5], this dataset covers diverse exposure
subcategories. EEG signals were collected from ten par-
ticipants while they viewed images with different exposure
levels, resulting in 10,800 high-quality samples. Each stim-
ulus was presented for 1000 milliseconds and recorded at
1000 Hz to capture the fine-grained temporal dynamics of
visual cognition. We applied minimal preprocessing to pre-
serve the original neural information and to provide a re-
liable foundation for data-driven cognitive guidance. Sec-
ond, we propose a Bi-level Optimization (BLO) framework
with a Teacher–Student architecture. The Teacher, termed
the Mental Integrated Transformer, learns fusion strategies
under the guidance of EEG signals and models perceptual
cues preferred by humans. The Student is a image-only net-
work that is jointly trained through BLO to approximate the
cognitive supervision provided by the Teacher. Rather than
using a separate two-stage distillation procedure, the cou-
pled optimization process encourages the Teacher to learn
representations that are naturally distillable. This design al-
lows the Student to emulate cognitive guidance effectively
while maintaining an EEG-free inference process. To sum-
marize, our main contributions are as follows:
• We construct Cog-Expo, the first EEG-based dataset
for investigating human cognitive responses in multi-
exposure image processing, bridging neuroscience and
low-level vision for cognition-aware MEF.
• A novel Bi-level Optimization framework is proposed to
transfer cognitive guidance from a privileged Teacher to
an EEG-free Student, allowing human-aligned learning
during training and image-only inference.
• The Mental Integrated Transformer is devised to explic-
itly guide visual feature extraction with cognitive priors
through a spatial-cognitive cross-attention mechanism.
• Extensive experiments show that the proposed method
achieves superior performance and produces results that
are more consistent with human visual perception.
2. Related Works
2.1. Multi-Exposure Fusion
Multi-exposure image fusion (MEF) aims to generate a vi-
sually enhanced image by integrating complementary in-
formation from inputs captured at different exposure lev-
els. Early methods mainly relied on hand-crafted priors and
multi-scale representations [1, 17, 19, 20, 38]. With the de-
velopment of deep learning, data-driven MEF methods have
achieved substantial progress. DeepFuse [33] introduced
an unsupervised CNN that learns luminance and chromi-
nance fusion from unpaired data, while MEF-GAN [44]
employed adversarial learning to produce sharper textures
and richer details. More recently, Transformer-based frame-
works have further improved global consistency by model-
ing long-range dependencies [26, 32, 35, 39]. Despite these
advances, existing methods remain vulnerable to motion
misalignment, extreme exposure variation, and content-
dependent degradation. More importantly, they rely exclu-
sively on visual priors [37, 46] and largely ignore cogni-
tive cues, such as perceptual attention and neural feedback,
which may provide additional guidance for exposure selec-
tion and structural preservation.
2.2. Cognition Information Utilization
In recent years, human cognitive information has received
increasing attention with the development of advanced
neuro-observation technologies. The human cognitive sys-
tem processes visual information efficiently and exhibits
strong robustness across diverse environments. These prop-
erties differ substantially from those of conventional com-
puter vision methods and may offer complementary advan-
tages for visual information processing. In particular, event-
related potential (ERP) components in EEG signals, such as
P300, are closely associated with attentional allocation and
decision-making. Prior studies have shown that such signals
are involved in high-level cognitive functions, including ob-
ject recognition [3, 21], visual attention regulation [27], and
scene understanding [45, 48]. Some studies have further at-
tempted to directly decode cognitive information into visual
content by reconstructing presented stimuli [10, 15, 18].
In addition, cognitive responses have been used as objec-
tive indicators of perceptual experience in subjective visual
quality assessment [12]. However, these studies generally
integrate cognitive signals through a brain–machine loop
and still depend on raw cognitive signals. As a result, they
do not enable a more general paradigm in which cognitive
capabilities can be transferred into vision models without

3. Cog-Expo Dataset
3.1. Participants
Ten healthy adult participants were recruited for this study
from the undergraduate student population, including 8
males and 2 females, with a mean age of 22.3 years (SD
= 1.68). All participants reported normal or corrected-to-
normal vision. Written informed consent was obtained from
all participants after a full explanation of the experimental
procedures, and monetary compensation was provided. The
study procedure was reviewed and approved by the Institu-
tional Ethics Committee.
3.2. Experimental paradigm
Preparation. To strengthen the cognitive characteristics of
the dataset, we collected participant information and pro-
vided pre-experiment practice. We collected basic personal
information, including handedness, recent sleep status, re-
cent emotional state. Our procedure required participants
to sensitively attend to different exposure regions and their
semantic content within them, engaging higher-level cog-
nitive processing. Participants were informed of their at-
tentional tasks and completed a 30-minute practice session
before the formal recording. The practice images were dis-
tinct from those used in the formal recording.
Stimuli. The multi-exposure image stimuli were obtained
from the SICE dataset [5], which contains 360 image groups
with multiple exposure levels and a normal-reference im-
age. For each group, the highest-, lowest-, and normal-
exposure images were selected to form a stimulus block,
enhancing cognitive responses to exposure variation and en-
abling assessment under extreme conditions. In total, 306
groups were used for training and 44 for validation.
Recording Process.
The experiment followed a design
similar to the rapid serial visual presentation (RSVP)
paradigm, but with longer presentation times for each im-
age to emphasize the visual-cognitive processing. As illus-
trated in Fig. 2, participants were seated approximately 57
cm from the screen, with a visual angle of about 10 de-
grees. A fixation cross was superimposed at the center of
each image to assist participants in maintaining their gaze,
enhancing attention and engagement. We used Psychtool-
box [4] to control stimulus presentation and recorded EEG
data throughout the experiment. At the beginning and end
of the recording, we collected five minutes of resting-state
data. Within a block, a blank screen with a fixation cross
preceded the stimuli, which were shown sequentially as un-
derexposed, normal, and overexposed images for 1 second
per image, separated by 0.5 s blank screens. After every
three blocks, participants answered whether the main object
remained recognizable under extreme exposure, encourag-
ing them to actively focus on cognitive-semantic informa-
tion. After 120 blocks, a session was completed, followed
by a triggered extended rest. The entire data collection re-
quired 3 sessions, and all 10 participants completed four
identical experimental rounds, resulting in 10 datasets.
3.3. Equipment and Preprocessing
EEG data were recorded using a Neuracle NSW364 EEG
acquisition system (Neuracle, China), equipped with a
standard 10–20 electrode cap containing 64 channels and
a high-resolution amplifier.
The electrodes were made
of powder-sintered material with antioxidant properties to
minimize signal drift during extended sessions. During data
acquisition, electrode impedance was maintained below 18
kΩ, and the sampling rate was set to 1 kHz. After recording,
offline preprocessing was conducted using pyedflib [11]. To
preserve the integrity of the original signals, only EMG and
EOG artifacts were removed, with a 50 Hz notch filter, and
channels with poor signal quality were excluded.
4. The Proposed Method
This section presents our framework for utilizing human
cognitive signals to guide multi-exposure fusion. We for-
mulate the problem as a bi-level optimization, coupling a
cognition-guided Teacher with an LDR-only Student. Un-
like previous two-stage approaches, this coupled formula-
tion ensures that the Teacher produces knowledge that is
inherently distillable, bridging the gap between training-
time privileged information and inference-time constraints.
We first introduce the preliminaries and the EEG-as-a-prior
mechanism. We then detail the bi-level formulation, the ar-
chitecture of the proposed Mental Integrated Transformer,
and the specific objective functions used to align perceptual
feedback with image reconstruction.

4.2. Bi-level Optimization Formulation
The main challenge in leveraging EEG is that it is avail-
able during training but unavailable during inference. To
address this issue, we adopt a teacher–student distillation
framework, where the Teacher is guided by EEG signals and
the Student relies only on visual inputs. However, standard
two-stage distillation is not ideal in this setting, because
a fixed Teacher may learn representations that are difficult
for the Student to imitate. We therefore propose a coupled
learning framework formulated as a Bi-level Optimization
problem. This joint optimization encourages the Teacher to
learn representations that are more distillable, allowing the
Student to absorb cognitive guidance more effectively.
The Teacher defines the lower-level problem, optimizing
its parameters θT utilizing both the LDR images I and the
projected EEG prior E. Crucially, we formulate the lower-

denoted as LLower(θT , θS). This formulation implies that
the Teacher’s optimization is conditioned on the Student’s
current state. The Student constitutes the upper-level prob-
lem, optimizing its parameters θS using only LDR images I

4.4. Mental Integrated Transformer
We design the Teacher model, termed the Mental Integrated
Transformer (T), as a multi-modal U-Net that integrates vi-
sual features with cognitive priors. The model takes a pair
of LDR images I = Ilow, Iover and leverages exposure-
specific cognitive states to guide feature extraction and re-
construction.
During encoding, the concatenated visual
inputs are processed by a hierarchical encoder, while the
corresponding EEG signals Eraw
low and Eraw
over are mapped
into latent embeddings through an EEG encoder and trans-
formed by MLP adapters into cognitive tokens vlow
cog and
vover
cog .
Each encoder block performs cross-attention, us-
ing intermediate visual features as Query and the cogni-
tive tokens as Key and Value. This mechanism enables the
network to dynamically modulate visual features based on
the subject’s cognitive responses, emphasizing perceptually
salient or visually demanding regions. During decoding, the
network reconstructs the fused image IT
F under the guid-
ance of a target cognitive state Eraw
GT , which is encoded into
a global token vGT
cog. At each upsampling stage, the decoder
features attend to this target token, aligning the reconstruc-
tion with human perceptual preferences represented by the
high-quality reference state.
For the Student network (S), which operates without
EEG signals, we preserve architectural consistency by re-
placing biological priors with pseudo-cognitive priors de-
rived directly from the input LDR images. These image-
based priors act as Key and Value in the cross-attention lay-
ers, enabling the Student to approximate cognition-aware
guidance using only visual cues available during inference.

5.3. Depth Estimation Analysis
We evaluate the depth estimation capability of our model
through the quality of the generated depth maps. Accurate
depth estimation is important for MEF because it provides
geometric cues for preserving structural coherence across
different exposure levels. As shown in Fig. 7, we compare
the results on the MEFB dataset with several state-of-the-
art methods. The qualitative comparison shows clear dif-
ferences in structural accuracy. Our method produces depth
maps with sharper edges, clearer boundaries, and smoother,
more geometrically consistent depth transitions, indicating
a better understanding of the underlying 3D scene structure.

6.2.3. Ablation on Mental Integrated Transformer
To assess the structural effectiveness of the proposed Men-
tal Integrated Transformer (T), we performed ablation stud-
ies on its key components. We compared the full model
with three variants: (1) removing cognitive guidance from
the encoder, (2) removing cognitive guidance from the de-
coder, and (3) replacing the cross-attention mechanism with
simple feature concatenation. As shown in Table 6, the full
model consistently outperforms all variants across metrics.
The largest decline occurs when replacing cross-attention
with concatenation, highlighting its essential role in inte-
grating high-dimensional cognitive priors into the visual
feature space.
7. Conclusion
This work presents a cognitively aware framework for
multi-exposure fusion. To address the lack of task-specific
cognitive data, we construct Cog-Expo, the first dataset that
pairs MEF sequences with human EEG responses and pro-
vides a dedicated benchmark for cognition-aware fusion re-
search. To overcome the unavailability of EEG signals dur-
ing inference, we further propose a novel bi-level distilla-
tion framework that transfers cognitive guidance from an
EEG-guided Teacher, namely the Mental Integrated Trans-
former, to an image-only Student. Extensive experiments
demonstrate that the proposed method achieves state-of-the-
art performance and produces fused images that are bet-
ter aligned with human perception in both quantitative and
qualitative evaluations across multiple benchmarks. These
results verify the effectiveness of bio-signals as a supervi-
sory prior and further suggest their broader potential for
cognitively aware low-level vision tasks.
