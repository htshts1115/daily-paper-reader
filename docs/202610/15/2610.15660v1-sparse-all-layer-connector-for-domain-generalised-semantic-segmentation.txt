# Abstract 

Domain generalised semantic segmentation (DGSS) has recently benefited from combining multiple foundation models, such as vision-language and vision founda-tion models, to leverage their diverse representations for generalisation. Existing multi-model approaches share a common design choice: cross-model fusion is restricted to depth-aligned layers, implicitly assuming that mutually beneficial information resides at the same depth across models. However, foundation models pretrained under different objectives may develop distinct representation hierar-chies, making the optimality of depth-aligned fusion questionable. Moreover, we observe that the foundation models exhibit different depth-wise domain sensitiv-ity. Motivated by these observations, we propose a Sparse All-Layer Connector (SALC) that enables each layer to access information from preceding layers of another model. As the number of accessible layers grows with depth, dense ag-gregation may hurt generalisation. SALC therefore learns to adaptively select a sparse subset of informative layers. We further introduce a candidate dropout regu-larisation that strengthens sparsity and encourages SALC to explore diverse layers, leading to more robust selection. Across four foundation model combinations and three DGSS evaluation settings, the proposed design demonstrates stronger generalisation than conventional depth-aligned fusion. The code is available at 

https://github.com/RuoyuGuo/SALC .

# 1 Introduction 

Domain generalised semantic segmentation (DGSS) seeks to learn a segmentation model that performs robustly on arbitrary unseen target domains using only source domain data. To extract robust features, prior work has explored a range of strategies [14, 47], while more recent work leverages foundation models for their strong generalisability [60]. Despite these advances, relying on a single foundation model limits the diversity of representations, as each model is bounded by its pretrained knowledge. This has motivated a growing body of work that integrates multiple foundation models to learn heterogeneous representations for DGSS, such as vision-language models (VLMs), depth estimation foundation models (DFMs), and vision foundation models (VFMs) [71, 11, 12, 45, 63]. In practice, existing multi-model approaches [71, 11, 12] adopt depth-aligned fusion as a natural default: a learnable connector (e.g., linear or cross-attention layers) is placed at the l-th layer of one foundation model to fuse features from the l-th layer of the other. Previous studies have found that this approach can fuse invariant features or visually grounded features into a stronger representation [11]. However, models pretrained under different objectives may process information along distinct hierarchies [50, 56] and prior findings have shown that foundation models exhibit

To examine this empirically, we study a representative combination [24, 71, 59], a VLM (CLIP [49]) and a VFM (DINOv2 [44]). We compare the depth-aligned baseline with a variant that randomly pairs layers across the two models. As shown in Figure 1 (a), random pairing yields mixed results relative to depth-aligned fusion, suggesting that the effectiveness is sensitive to layer matching. We further analyse layer-wise cross-domain behaviour using Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD). Figure 1 (b) shows that CLIP and DINOv2 exhibit markedly different depth-wise patterns: CLIP reaches its lowest at middle layers and increases toward the end, while DINOv2 decreases monotonically with depth. These observations motivate us to fuse features between models in a flexible cross-depth manner. In this paper, for each layer in one model, which we refer to as a query layer , we allow it to aggregate information from multiple layers of the other model, which we refer to as candidate layers .However, this flexible design poses two critical challenges. First, the number of candidate layers grows with depth, and the informativeness of each candidate layer varies across tokens, which complicates effective fusion. Second, dense aggregation across all candidate layers can introduce irrelevant or domain-specific features, which may hurt generalisation. Although some existing methods [6, 53] propose various multi-modal fusion strategies, they typically utilise cross-attention and TopK routing, which are insufficient here. This is because cross-attention uses softmax, which assigns non-zero weights to every candidate layer and may retain irrelevant features, while TopK routing enforces sparsity but with a fixed K that cannot adapt across depths and tokens. To this end, we propose the Sparse All-Layer Connector (SALC) for cross-model fusion in DGSS. Different from existing methods that restrict fusion to depth-aligned layers, SALC allows each query layer to access an adaptive subset of all preceding layers up to the same depth in the other model, so that only the most relevant features are fused. To learn adaptive selection, we adopt Sparsegen [31], which projects predicted layer scores into a sparse weight distribution over candidate layers. Unlike softmax or TopK routing, Sparsegen can control the active candidate layers through a sparsity factor rather than a manually defined number. Building on this, we predict a token-wise sparsity factor using the candidate layer at the same depth, thereby enabling adaptivity at both the token and layer levels. Moreover, we propose a candidate dropout regularisation that perturbs over-selected layers during training, guiding SALC toward more robust and discriminative selection. We explore two instan-tiations: uniform drop , where the same drop rate is applied for all layers and frequency-based drop , where a higher drop rate is applied for over-used layers. We evaluate SALC on five foun-dation models spanning VLMs (CLIP, SigLIP [70]), VFMs (DINOv2, SAM [29]), and a depth model (DepthAnythingV2 [64]) across three evaluation settings, showing competitive performance compared to depth-aligned fusion. Our main contributions are summarised as follows: • We propose SALC, a multi-model DGSS framework that aggregates cross-layer features from different foundation models through a sparse selection mechanism. The selection is adaptively controlled for each layer and token through a learnable sparsity factor. • To achieve more robust layer selection, we further introduce a candidate dropout regularisa-tion that strengthens sparsity and exploration of informative layers. 2• Through experiments on three types of foundation models across three DGSS evaluation settings, we demonstrate the effectiveness of SALC and provide an in-depth analysis of how cross-layer selection benefits domain generalisation. 

# 2 Related work 

Domain generalised semantic segmentation (DGSS). DGSS requires the segmentation model to remain robust when encountering unseen images [16, 18, 27, 33, 37]. Classic approaches mainly follow three directions, including data augmentation [68, 32, 28, 74, 19, 7, 25, 23, 2, 17, 73, 61, 41], learning invariant representation [1, 20, 62, 19, 14, 5] and feature normalisation [46, 47, 48, 9]. Despite their effectiveness, these methods rely on models trained with less data. Recent methods adapt foundation models to DGSS through parameter-efficient fine-tuning [60, 21] or selectively tuning domain-sensitive parameters and features [4, 69, 66, 72, 3]. To extract more diverse cues, several methods fuse features from multiple foundation models, such as language [45, 71, 42, 22], depth map [12, 11], and high-resolution images [57]. Unlike prior multi-model DGSS methods that primarily explore different model combinations, we improve generalisation through the fusion design itself. We propose flexible cross-depth layer fusion together with a frequency-based dropout regularisation that encourages SALC to discover more robust features. 

Multi-model feature fusion. Multi-model feature fusion aims to exploit complementary represen-tations from different encoders or modalities [35]. In multimodal large language models, one line of work enhances visual perception by combining visual tokens from multiple encoders, resolutions, or expert branches [36, 26, 58, 34, 53, 54, 35]. Another line aggregates multi-layer features before feeding into the language model with depth-aligned or dense connectors [38, 39, 6, 24, 8, 10, 65]. In DGSS, recent multi-model methods fuse complementary cues from VFMs, VLMs, and depth foundation models, but their cross-model interactions are typically established between depth-aligned layers [71, 11, 12]. These designs improve the diversity of visual cues but adopt depth-aligned fusion as a default choice. This assumption is questionable under domain shift, where different layers exhibit different transferability across models. Unlike these works, SALC tackles cross-model layer assignment as a learnable problem: for each query layer, it adaptively selects a sparse subset of candidate layers. This makes cross-model fusion more selective and better suited to DGSS, where irrelevant or domain-specific candidate features may hurt generalisation. 

# 3 Method 

3.1 Overview Problem formulation. We address the DGSS problem, where a model is trained on a labelled source domain Ds and evaluated on unseen target domains Dt whose samples are inaccessible during training. We use Ds → D t to denote training on Ds and evaluating on Dt.

Overall architecture. As illustrated in Figure 2, our framework consists of two frozen encoders 

EA and EB pretrained under different objectives and a segmentation decoder. The core component of our framework is a set of SALCs, which allow each query layer to access all preceding candidate layers while activating only a sparse subset for aggregation. To improve the robustness of selection, we further propose a frequency-based regularisation. Given an input RGB image, the two encoders extract features in parallel. The last-layer features from both encoders are then concatenated and projected by a linear layer into a unified representation, which is fed into a Mask2Former [13] decoder to produce the final segmentation map. 

3.2 Sparse all-layer connector Revisiting depth-aligned fusion. In prior multi-model DGSS works [71, 11, 12], given an input image x, it is fed into two encoders EA and EB with L transformer layers each. We denote the output of their l-th layer as f lA, f lB ∈ RT ×D , where T and D are the number of tokens and feature dimensions. A depth-aligned connector Cl is employed to fuse features from the same layer of each encoder and residually updates the query encoder: f lA ← f lA + ∆ f lA, where ∆f lA = Cl(f lA, f lB ) ∈ RT ×D. The effectiveness of SALC relies on its ability to make input-dependent selection of candidate layers. However, during training, we observe that at certain query layers, the selection weights spread too broadly across candidate layers, suggesting the module fails to make decisive choices at these layers. We thus propose a regularisation that drops accessible candidate layers, encouraging more decisive selection at every layer. We explore two instantiations: uniform dropout and frequency-based dropout .The regularisation is applied only during training and does not incur inference costs. At each training step, we sample a binary mask M ∈ { 0, 1}l, where M[i] = 0 indicates dropping the 

i-th candidate. We stack the per-token weights from Equation (2) into W ∈ RT ×l, multiply W by 

M to drop candidate layers, and renormalise each row so that the weights sum to 1 for every token. The renormalised ˜W then replaces W in Equation (4) for aggregation. For sampling M, we first explore uniform dropout , where each candidate layer is independently dropped with a fixed probability p. This approach treats all candidate layers equally. We further explore frequency-based dropout , where the drop probability p of each candidate layer is scaled by its historical selection frequency, giving dominant candidate layers a higher chance of being dropped. Following prior multi-model DGSS work [71], we train SALC using the standard segmentation losses from Mask2Former [13]: Ltotal = αbce Lbce + αdice Ldice + αcls Lcls , where Lbce , Ldice , and Lcls 

represent the binary cross-entropy loss, the dice loss for the predicted masks, and the cross-entropy loss for each queried proposal, respectively. 

# 4 Experiments 

4.1 Datasets & evaluation protocols Datasets. We evaluate SALC on five driving-scene semantic segmentation datasets that share 19 common classes. Specifically, GTAV (G) [51] and SYNTHIA (S) [52] are two synthetic datasets, containing 24,966 and 9,400 training images, respectively. Cityscapes (C) [15] consists of 2,975 training and 500 validation images. BDD100K (B) [67] and Mapillary (M) [40] include 1,000 and 2,000 validation images, respectively. These three real-world datasets capture diverse scenes from a variety of global cities. 

Evaluation. Following the evaluation protocols of existing DGSS methods [60, 4, 46], we evaluate SALC on three evaluation settings with the mIoU metric: (1) G →{C, B, M}, (2) S →{C, B, M}, and (3) C →{B, M}. 

Implementation details. Following existing multi-model DGSS methods [71, 12, 11], we evaluate SALC on two VFMs (SAM [29], DINOv2 [44]), two VLMs (CLIP [49], SigLIP [70]), and one DFM (DepthAnythingV2 [64]). This results in four combinations of EA and EB : DINOv2-CLIP, DINOv2-SigLIP, SAM-CLIP, and DINOv2-DepthAnythingV2. 4.2 Comparison with State-of-the-art DGSS methods 

We compare SALC with various DGSS methods: (1) classic methods, including SAN-SAW [48], AdvStyle [74], and CMFormer [5]; (2) Single foundation model methods, including tqdm [45], Rein [60], SET [66], FADA [4], CLOUDS [2] and SoMA [69]; and (3) Multi foundation model methods, including MFuser [71], DepthForge [12] and Vireo [11]. We report results on G→{C, B, M} , S→{C, B, M} , and C→{B, M } in Table 1. SALC achieves competitive performance across all three settings, outperforming both single-foundation-model and depth-aligned multi-model baselines on most backbone configurations. On G →{C, B, M}, SALC achieves an average of 69.40 with DINOv2-CLIP, surpassing the previous depth-aligned baseline MFuser by 1.58 mIoU and the strongest single-foundation-model method (SoMA) by 1.13 mIoU. The improvement is larger on the challenging S →{C, B, M}, where SALC reaches 52.99 with DINOv2-CLIP, surpassing MFuser by +2.65. Notably, while multi-model baselines like DepthForge and MFuser sometimes underperform single-encoder CLOUDS on this setting, indicating that naive depth-aligned fusion may hurt generalisation under large domain shifts. SALC’s effectiveness holds across multiple backbone combinations, reaching the largest single-domain gain of +4.16 mIoU over DepthForge on G →C. We observe that SALC’s improvements on SAM-CLIP are smaller and occasionally slightly negative. We provide further analysis in Appendix E. We also provide qualitative comparisons in Figure 7. 

4.3 Layer selection analysis 

In this section, we analyse the layer selection behaviour of SALC across domains and backbones. Our analysis reveals three key findings: the learned selection (1) leverages diverse candidate layers without collapsing onto a fixed subset, (2) exhibits backbone-specific preferences that align with the complementary roles of VFM and VLM, and (3) remains largely consistent across domains. Sparsity control. Figure 5 shows the depth-wise distribution of the learned sparsity factor λ across the four domains. We observe that λ exhibits substantially higher cross-domain variability at deeper layers while remaining stable at shallow ones. We believe this is because deeper query layers tend to encode richer high-level information, which may require finer per-token sparsity adjustment to handle semantics that vary across classes and spatial locations. This pattern is also consistent with the cross-domain shift observed in the preceding ratio at deeper layers, indicating that domain variation in SALC is more visible at deeper layers through both weight assignment and sparsity control. 

Dropout regularisation. Figure 6 compares the average active-layer count and cross-domain weight std before and after applying our dropout regularisation. After regularisation, the number of active candidates is clearly reduced and the selection becomes more concentrated. The cross-domain std of the selection is also reduced, as shown in the fourth column. Beyond compression, dropout exhibits distinct behaviour across query layers and models. At layers that are already moderately sparse, it does not further reduce the active-layer number but instead reorganises the dominant candidates, redirecting the selection toward different informative layers. Based on these observations, we suggest two complementary reasons why dropout improves generali-sation. First, by strengthening sparsity and lowering cross-domain variation, dropout guides SALC to commit to the most consistently informative candidates rather than spreading weight across many candidates that may carry domain-specific noise. Second, by perturbing dominant candidates during training, dropout encourages SALC to discover diverse informative layers, leading to a more robust selection that does not depend on any specific layer.

Adaptive sparsity control. We first study how to control the sparsity factor λ in SALC. Specifically, we compare a fixed λ, a dynamic layer-wise λ and our dynamic token-wise λ. The fixed λ is set to the averaged λ learned by SALC for a fair comparison. As shown in Table 2, the other two variants reduce average mIoU by 1.56. As shown in Table 2, uniform dropout can harm generalisability because it drops candidates indiscriminately, unlike frequency-based dropout, which targets over-used candidates to strengthen sparsity and explore alternative informative layers. Cross-layer fusion. To evaluate whether our sparse all-layer fusion is more effective than ex-isting designs, we compare five fusion strategies: (1) no fusion, (2) depth-aligned fusion (linear connector), (3) cross-depth fusion (attention), (4) cross-depth fusion (Top2 routing), and (5) cross-depth fusion (SALC). As shown in Table 4, cross-depth fusion outperforms depth-aligned fusion by leveraging multi-level features, but generalises less well than SALC on this bench-mark due to the lack of dynamic sparsity. 

Candidate scope and fusion direction. Beyond the core SALC mechanism, we further study the candidate scope and the cross-model fusion direction. For the candidate scope, we restrict SALC to use only the most recent 2 or 4 layers as candidate layers. For fusion direction, we disable fusion from CLIP or DINOv2 by removing SALC in their respective encoders. As reported in Table 3, reducing the candidate scope hurts performance, dropping the average mIoU by 0.37 and 0.12 for window sizes of 2 and 4, respectively. This suggests that broader access to multi-level features benefits generalisation. Moreover, removing fusion from either model degrades overall performance, indicating that bidirectional fusion is essential for SALC. 

# 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we propose a Sparse All-Layer Connector for multi-model fusion in domain generalised semantic segmentation. Unlike existing methods that fuse features only at the same depth, SALC enables each layer to leverage features from all preceding layers of the other model. To avoid redundancy that may harm generalisation, we adopt Sparsegen with a token-wise, content-dependent sparsity factor for adaptive selection across layers and tokens. In addition, we introduce a frequency-based dropout regularisation that strengthens sparsity, lowers cross-domain variation, and encourages exploration of diverse layers. We evaluate SALC on five foundation models spanning three model 9types, achieving competitive performance across three evaluation settings and demonstrating the effectiveness of cross-layer fusion. # C Stability of dropout 

Since dropout introduces stochasticity during training, we further evaluate the stability of uniform 

and frequency-based dropout by reporting the mean and standard deviation of three independent runs in Table 7. Across both DINOv2-CLIP and DINOv2-SigLIP, frequency-based dropout achieves the highest average mIoU with the smallest standard deviation ( ≤ 0.11), while uniform dropout exhibits substantially larger variance (0.89 on DINOv2-CLIP and 0.30 on DINOv2-SigLIP). The instability of uniform dropout aligns with its design: it drops candidates indiscriminately, providing no consistent regularisation target across runs. In contrast, frequency-based dropout focuses regularisation on over-used candidates, yielding both stronger and more stable improvements.

# E Limitations 

E.1 Limited improvement on SAM-CLIP 

We hypothesise that the smaller gain of SALC on SAM-CLIP stems from the weak layer-wise structure of SAM features when paired with CLIP. As shown in Figure 12, we compare the layer-wise Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) [30] between CLIP vs DINOv2 and CLIP vs SAM. CLIP vs DINOv2 exhibits a clear layer-dependent structure: shallow CLIP layers align strongly with shallow-to-mid DINOv2 layers, while deeper CLIP layers form a distinct, less similar region. This indicates that each DINOv2 layer carries distinguishable information, providing useful signals for SALC’s selection. In contrast, CLIP vs SAM shows uniformly low similarity across nearly all layer pairs, with no comparable layer-wise structure. Such weak and uniform alignment makes it difficult for SALC to identify informative SAM layers for cross-model fusion. We attribute this to SAM being trained with a different objective (segmentation supervision rather than self-distillation as in DINOv2), which leads to features less compatible with CLIP’s representation space. 

E.2 Limited gain on real-to-real benchmark 

Compared to the G →{C, B, M} and S →{C, B, M} settings, SALC’s improvement on C → {B, M} is smaller, with most of the gain concentrated on Mapillary. We attribute this to the smaller domain gap between real-world source and target domains, where low-level features from the source domain transfer effectively to the targets. SALC selects layers at the semantic level rather than preserving low-level features, which limits its advantage in this setting. 17 Figure 8: Layer selection statistics of SALC across domains (G, C, B, M) for DINOv2-SigLIP.

# F Additional ablations 

We provide additional ablations of SALC. Specifically, we study the choice of γ, the effect of EMA momentum, and the effect of applying RMSNorm before scoring. All experiments use the DINOv2–CLIP combination and are evaluated on the G →{C, B, M} setting. The EMA momentum is used to track the running statistics for our frequency-based dropout , mitigat-ing the noise introduced by the small batch size of 2. RMSNorm is applied to reduce the magnitude variations across depths in Ft before computing the importance scores zt. The hyperparameter γ

controls the sharpness of the drop-rate penalty: with γ > 1, the penalty grows more sharply for over-used layers. As reported in Table 8, EMA improves overall generalisation by stabilising the dropout regularisation, while RMSNorm enhances the effectiveness of importance scoring across candidate layers. # G Broader impacts 

Our work studies domain generalisation in semantic segmentation, with potential applications in autonomous driving, robotics, and urban scene understanding. By improving model robustness across diverse, unseen environments, our method may help systems perform more reliably under real-world conditions such as adverse weather, nighttime, or unfamiliar scenes. However, segmentation models may still produce unreliable predictions on rare or out-of-distribution scenes. Deploying such systems requires careful evaluation, robust failure handling, and complementary safety mechanisms. 20 NeurIPS Paper Checklist 

The checklist is designed to encourage best practices for responsible machine learning research, addressing issues of reproducibility, transparency, research ethics, and societal impact. Do not remove the checklist: The papers not including the checklist will be desk rejected. The checklist should follow the references and follow the (optional) supplemental material. The checklist does NOT count towards the page limit. Please read the checklist guidelines carefully for information on how to answer these questions. For each question in the checklist: • You should answer [Yes], [No], or [N/A]. • [N/A] means either that the question is Not Applicable for that particular paper or the relevant information is Not Available. • Please provide a short (1–2 sentence) justification right after your answer (even for [N/A]). 

The checklist answers are an integral part of your paper submission. They are visible to the reviewers, area chairs, senior area chairs, and ethics reviewers. You will also be asked to include it (after eventual revisions) with the final version of your paper, and its final version will be published with the paper. The reviewers of your paper will be asked to use the checklist as one of the factors in their evaluation. While [Yes] is generally preferable to [No], it is perfectly acceptable to answer [No] provided a proper justification is given (e.g., error bars are not reported because it would be too computationally expensive” or “we were unable to find the license for the dataset we used”). In general, answering [No] or [N/A] is not grounds for rejection. While the questions are phrased in a binary way, we acknowledge that the true answer is often more nuanced, so please just use your best judgment and write a justification to elaborate. All supporting evidence can appear either in the main paper or the supplemental material, provided in appendix. If you answer [Yes] to a question, in the justification please point to the section(s) where related material for the question can be found. 1. Claims 

Question: Do the main claims made in the abstract and introduction accurately reflect the paper’s contributions and scope? Answer: [Yes] Justification: We study the multi-model fusion in domain generalised semantic segmentation. Guidelines: • The answer [N/A] means that the abstract and introduction do not include the claims made in the paper. • The abstract and/or introduction should clearly state the claims made, including the contributions made in the paper and important assumptions and limitations. A [No] or [N/A] answer to this question will not be perceived well by the reviewers. • The claims made should match theoretical and experimental results, and reflect how much the results can be expected to generalize to other settings. • It is fine to include aspirational goals as motivation as long as it is clear that these goals are not attained by the paper. 2. Limitations 

Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors? Answer: [Yes] Justification: We discuss our limitations in the appendix. Guidelines: • The answer [N/A] means that the paper has no limitation while the answer [No] means that the paper has limitations, but those are not discussed in the paper. • The authors are encouraged to create a separate “Limitations” section in their paper. 21 • The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to violations of these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings, model well-specification, asymptotic approximations only holding locally). The authors should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the implications would be. • The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated. • The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach. For example, a facial recognition algorithm may perform poorly when image resolution is low or images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be used reliably to provide closed captions for online lectures because it fails to handle technical jargon. • The authors should discuss the computational efficiency of the proposed algorithms and how they scale with dataset size. • If applicable, the authors should discuss possible limitations of their approach to address problems of privacy and fairness. • While the authors might fear that complete honesty about limitations might be used by reviewers as grounds for rejection, a worse outcome might be that reviewers discover limitations that aren’t acknowledged in the paper. The authors should use their best judgment and recognize that individual actions in favor of transparency play an impor-tant role in developing norms that preserve the integrity of the community. Reviewers will be specifically instructed to not penalize honesty concerning limitations. 3. Theory assumptions and proofs 

Question: For each theoretical result, does the paper provide the full set of assumptions and a complete (and correct) proof? Answer: [N/A] Justification: This paper does not include theoretical contributions. Guidelines: • The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include theoretical results. • All the theorems, formulas, and proofs in the paper should be numbered and cross-referenced. • All assumptions should be clearly stated or referenced in the statement of any theorems. • The proofs can either appear in the main paper or the supplemental material, but if they appear in the supplemental material, the authors are encouraged to provide a short proof sketch to provide intuition. • Inversely, any informal proof provided in the core of the paper should be complemented by formal proofs provided in appendix or supplemental material. • Theorems and Lemmas that the proof relies upon should be properly referenced. 4. Experimental result reproducibility 

Question: Does the paper fully disclose all the information needed to reproduce the main ex-perimental results of the paper to the extent that it affects the main claims and/or conclusions of the paper (regardless of whether the code and data are provided or not)? Answer: [Yes] Justification: Our method is built upon the official public code of prior work. We will release our code upon acceptance. All training details can be found in the open public code of prior work. Guidelines: • The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include experiments. • If the paper includes experiments, a [No] answer to this question will not be perceived well by the reviewers: Making the paper reproducible is important, regardless of whether the code and data are provided or not. 22 • If the contribution is a dataset and/or model, the authors should describe the steps taken to make their results reproducible or verifiable. • Depending on the contribution, reproducibility can be accomplished in various ways. For example, if the contribution is a novel architecture, describing the architecture fully might suffice, or if the contribution is a specific model and empirical evaluation, it may be necessary to either make it possible for others to replicate the model with the same dataset, or provide access to the model. In general. releasing code and data is often one good way to accomplish this, but reproducibility can also be provided via detailed instructions for how to replicate the results, access to a hosted model (e.g., in the case of a large language model), releasing of a model checkpoint, or other means that are appropriate to the research performed. • While NeurIPS does not require releasing code, the conference does require all submis-sions to provide some reasonable avenue for reproducibility, which may depend on the nature of the contribution. For example (a) If the contribution is primarily a new algorithm, the paper should make it clear how to reproduce that algorithm. (b) If the contribution is primarily a new model architecture, the paper should describe the architecture clearly and fully. (c) If the contribution is a new model (e.g., a large language model), then there should either be a way to access this model for reproducing the results or a way to reproduce the model (e.g., with an open-source dataset or instructions for how to construct the dataset). (d) We recognize that reproducibility may be tricky in some cases, in which case authors are welcome to describe the particular way they provide for reproducibility. In the case of closed-source models, it may be that access to the model is limited in some way (e.g., to registered users), but it should be possible for other researchers to have some path to reproducing or verifying the results. 5.
