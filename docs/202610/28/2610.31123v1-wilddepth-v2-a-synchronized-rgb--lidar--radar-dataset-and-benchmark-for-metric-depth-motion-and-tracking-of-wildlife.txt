Abstract 

Measuring where an animal is, how far it is, and how it moves, in metres and metres per second rather than pixels – underpins distance-sampling abundance estimates, morphometrics, and movement ecology. Monocular video, the dominant wildlife data source, carries no metric scale, and existing animal datasets provide no metric ground truth against which to test methods that try to recover it. We previously introduced WildDepth , a synchronized RGB–LiDAR wildlife dataset from three sites on two continents. Here we present WildDepth-v2 , which extends it in three directions. (i) Athird sensing modality : 37 sequences (19,716 synchronized frames) of RGB–LiDAR–4D-imaging-radar recordings of animals, with per-point Doppler velocity and a measured error budget, to our knowledge the first wildlife data captured with imaging radar. (ii) A frozen, held-out benchmark : a checksummed hidden test set for RGB-only distance estimation and RGB+sparse-LiDAR depth completion, scored separately on animal and background pixels. (iii) Motion and tracking tasks : metric 3D animal trajec-tories obtained by lifting 2D tracks with LiDAR, radar, or monocular depth. On 48,723 animal frames, object-level radar range calibrates a monocular depth model better than scene-level LiDAR calibration and without LiDAR at inference (AbsRel 0.120 vs. 0.198): note however LiDAR at inference on the animal remains more accurate (0.052). Over 431 animal tracks, scaling monocular depth by radar once per track reduces 3D position error four-fold (2.20 →0.58 m) and the bias of a distance-sampling detection function from −13% to −2%, matching a LiDAR calibration made once per track. Doppler velocity, transferred onto the co-registered LiDAR, yields motion masks 3.7 × denser than radar alone at 72% precision, is able to gate multi-second LiDAR accumulation on moving animals ( −37% error), and tracks radial animal speed (r = 0.60), although it under-reads it by half. We also map radar’s limits: a 1.8 m vertical versus 0.1 m in-plane extrinsic residual rules out direct per-pixel radar depth at this point. Data, calibration, benchmark, and code are released. 

# 1 Introduction 

Non-invasive monitoring of animals is increasingly vital as biodiversity faces unprecedented pressure from habitat loss, poaching, and climate change [13, 52]. Computer vision has made detection, species classification, and individual identification routine at scale [6, 17, 23], and multi-object tracking now links detections into trajectories in video [38, 49, 61]. Many of the questions ecologists ask of these trajectories, however, are metric : how far away is the animal (the input to distance-sampling abundance estimates [11, 24]), how large is it (body condition, sexual dimorphism, population structure [16]), and how fast and where does it move (behaviour, energetics). The overwhelming majority of wildlife imagery 1arXiv:2610.31123v1 [cs.CV] 22 Oct 2026 – camera traps, hand-held and aerial video [6, 22, 32] – is monocular RGB and carries no metric scale. Modern monocular depth estimators (MDEs) recover plausible relative geometry, but whether their metric outputs can be trusted for a distant, deformable animal in unstructured terrain has been difficult to test, because animal datasets do not contain metric ground truth. In the conference version of this work [5] we introduced WildDepth , a synchronized RGB–LiDAR dataset collected at three ecologically distinct sites including Kgalagadi Transfrontier Park (South Africa), Bubye Valley Conservancy (Zimbabwe), and Longleat Safari Park (UK), and used it to show that off-the-shelf MDEs degrade sharply at range while sparse LiDAR anchoring roughly halves their metric error. LiDAR, however, is expensive, power-consuming, and mechanically delicate, which limits how widely such a rig can be deployed; and neither LiDAR nor a single RGB frame measures motion directly, which is exactly the quantity a tracker must reason about. Millimetre-wave 4D imaging radar offers a complementary trade-off that has transformed automotive perception [12, 43, 44]: it is inexpensive, low-power, robust to dust, rain and darkness, and it measures each return’s radial velocity through the Doppler effect. Radar–camera fusion for depth, detection, and tracking is now an active area in driving [33, 35, 41, 57], but the targets there are large, rigid, and retro-reflective. Wildlife inverts these conditions: animals possess small, low-reflectivity, non-rigid bodies whose Doppler signature is dominated by limb motion, observed at tens of metres superimposed on natural clutter. Whether radar transfers to this regime is, to our knowledge, untested. This paper, WildDepth-v2 , extends WildDepth to address both gaps, a cheaper source of metric scale, and a direct measurement of motion and transforms the dataset into a benchmark for metric animal perception over time. Our contributions are: 

The first RGB–LiDAR–radar wildlife recordings (Sec. 3.2). We add a synchronized Altos V2 4D imaging radar to the Longleat subset: 37 sequences and 19,716 frames in which RGB, LiDAR, and radar are all present, with per-point Doppler velocity and radar cross-section. We release the radar →LiDAR extrinsic together with a measured error budget (radar–LiDAR timing, and a strongly anisotropic extrinsic residual), rather than assuming one. 

A frozen, held-out WildDepth benchmark (Sec. 4). A checksummed hidden test set drawn from unreleased footage, with two tracks, first RGB-only camera-to-animal distance and RGB+sparse-LiDAR dense depth and second a scorer that separates animal from background error, and a documented construction recipe and defect list. 

Radar as a metric anchor and a motion sensor (Secs. 5.3–5.5). Object-level radar range calibrates monocular depth better than scene-level LiDAR calibration without LiDAR at infer-ence (AbsRel 0.120 vs. 0.198 on 20,869 test animal frames), though not as well as LiDAR on the animal. Doppler, transferred to the co-registered LiDAR through anisotropic association, yields motion masks 3.7 × denser than radar alone at 72% precision, and can gate multi-second LiDAR accumulation so that moving animals are not smeared. 

Metric motion and tracking tasks (Sec. 5.6). We lift 2D animal tracks to metric 3D trajectories using LiDAR, radar, or monocular depth and evaluate position, speed, jitter and distance-sampling bias. Scaling monocular depth by radar once per track matches a once-per-track LiDAR calibration (0.58 vs. 0.52 m position error) and cuts the detection-function bias of monocular depth from −13% to −2%. 

An objective map of radar’s operating envelope for wildlife (Sec. 5.7), by including negative results we believe are useful to others: per-pixel radar depth fails because of a 1.8 m vertical extrinsic residual; radar does not extend coverage or see through vegetation in our data; Doppler tracks radial animal speed but under-reads it by half; and per-frame extrinsic refinement can drift far enough to corrupt LiDAR ground truth. 2Relative to the conference paper [5], the radar modality, the hidden benchmark and its baselines, the motion and tracking tasks, and the envelope analysis are new; the monocular-depth evaluation has been recomputed on the publicly released snippets with seven models (Sec. 5.1); and the behaviour and 3D-reconstruction baselines are summarized for completeness (Sec. 5.8). 

# 2 Related Work 

Wildlife datasets. Camera traps, aerial surveys, and hand-held video have produced large animal datasets for detection, identification, and behaviour recognition [6, 47]. MammAlps [22], LoTE [34], KABR [29] and BaboonLand [18] provide video benchmarks for behaviour; Animal Kingdom [42] and MammalNet [14] capture species appearance from internet video; ChimpACT [37] and the Meerkat dataset [48] target tracking and behaviour of a single species. These datasets are RGB-only (occasionally with audio) and contain no metric geometry. WildPose [40] demonstrated long-range RGB–LiDAR capture of wild animals, and WildDepth [5] turned it into a multi-site dataset. WildDepth-v2 adds imaging radar and a held-out benchmark (Table 1). 

Animal tracking. Tracking-by-detection with Kalman-filter motion models and IoU or appearance association, SORT [8], DeepSORT [56], ByteTrack [61], is the default for animals in video, alongside laboratory trackers for collectives [49] and pose trackers [38, 39, 59]. In driving, tracking has moved to metric 3D space [55, 60] and is scored with CLEAR-MOT and HOTA [7, 36]. Animal tracking in the wild remains almost entirely 2D because metric 3D ground truth has been unavailable; WildDepth-v2 provides it through LiDAR and adds per-point radial velocity from radar. 

Monocular depth estimation. MDE has advanced from self-supervised CNNs [30] to large transform-ers DPT [45], Depth Anything [58], Depth Pro [10], and metric geometry models such as MoGe-2 [54] evaluated mainly on driving and indoor benchmarks [31, 51]. Wildlife scenes are unstructured, their subjects are non-rigid and often tens of metres away, and they are under-represented in training data; recent work has begun lifting monocular animal observations to metric 3D [4, 50, 62]. We provide the metric ground truth to test such methods and study which sparse sensor can supply the missing scale. 

Radar for depth, motion, and tracking. Automotive radar datasets such as nuScenes [12], View-of-Delft [44], K-Radar [43] enabled radar–camera fusion for depth completion [33, 35], detection [41], and Doppler-aware tracking of dynamic objects [57]. Doppler also gives platform ego-motion [28], and micro-Doppler from limbs and rotating parts is a well-known signature of articulated targets [15]. All of this work targets vehicles and pedestrians on roads. Wildlife inverts the assumptions: small, low-reflectivity, deformable targets at long range, in natural clutter, with Doppler dominated by gait. We are not aware of prior work that evaluates imaging radar on animals. 

# 3 The WildDepth-v2 Dataset 

WildDepth-v2 consists of the RGB–LiDAR collection of WildDepth [5] (Sec. 3.1), a new radar-equipped subset (Sec. 3.2), and a new held-out test set (Sec. 4). Figure 1 gives an overview. 

3.1 RGB–LiDAR Collection (from WildDepth) 

WildDepth comprises three subsets collected in 2022 and 2025 in wild habitats and a semi-controlled safari park (Table 2). The Kgalagadi subset (Kgalagadi Transfrontier Park, South Africa, December 2022) was captured over 13 days with the vehicle-mounted WildPose system [40], a zoom-lens camera with a Livox TELE-15 LiDAR, and covers 26 species, apex predators such as lion, cheetah and leopard, and herbivores such as springbok and gemsbok, at 12–140 m. The Bubye subset (Bubye Valley Conservancy, Zimbabwe, June 2025) used a custom 4K RGB camera with a Livox Mid-70, in mobile (vehicle window) and stationary configurations, over mopane and acacia woodland; it covers six species at 5–50 m. 3.2 RGB–LiDAR–Radar Subset (new) 

Sensor. On the second rig we rigidly co-mounted an Altos V2 4D imaging radar with the LiDAR and an Intel RealSense colour camera (640 ×480). Per frame, the radar returns a sparse point cloud in which each point carries its Cartesian position ( x, y, z ), its radial (Doppler) velocity h in m/s, its signal strength (a radar-cross-section proxy, s), and a coarse three-way motion class v ∈ {− 1, 0, +1 }; we recommend the continuous h, since the motion class under-counts moving returns by roughly an order of magnitude on our data. Angular resolution is about 1 .38 ◦ in azimuth and 1 .43 ◦ in elevation. A typical frame holds 679 valid returns (median over sequences) against 19,390 LiDAR points; returns extend beyond 200 m, but the animals we study lie within 55 m. On animals, the radar returns associated with a detection (Sec. 4.3) number a median of 45, 28 and 22 at 5–20, 20–30 and 30–55 m, against 109, 54 and 47 single-sweep LiDAR points inside the animal’s mask (Fig. 2); many of the associated radar returns come from ground and background seen past the animal. Radar is therefore much sparser on the animal than the LiDAR it accompanies, but it measures range and velocity directly. 

Coverage. Radar was recorded on two days. On the second day (July 8) it is healthy in all 37 snippets; on the first day (July 7) the sensor stopped publishing and the recorder repeated a stale message, so every radar frame within a snippet is byte-identical. We detected this with a per-snippet checksum test and 

exclude July 7 radar from the release and all experiments. The radar subset thus contains 37 snippets, 22,348 radar frames, and 19,716 frames with RGB, LiDAR and radar all present (one LiDAR file is empty and excluded). 55–20 m 20 –30 m 30 –55 m 020 40 60 80 100 points per animal (median)  109 45 54 28 47 22 LiDAR points in SAM2 mask (1 sweep) radar returns associated to box Figure 2: Sensor density on animals, by ground-truth distance: single-sweep LiDAR points inside the SAM2 mask and radar returns associated with the detection box (median over 48,723 animals). 

Synchronization. All three streams share one global 10 Hz frame index, so radar, LiDAR and RGB with the same index form a synchronized triple; the index is also the key that links the second rig record-ings to the released Longleat snippets. Radar messages are matched to the nearest LiDAR timestamp, and we release the per-frame offset. The mean absolute radar–LiDAR offset is 16.8 ms (95th percentile 31.5 ms), with rare dropouts up to 350 ms that we flag (Fig. 10); at a walking speed of 5 m/s a 20 ms offset corresponds to 10 cm of displacement. 

Calibration and its error budget. The LiDAR →camera extrinsic of the second rig rig was solved once per sequence (the anchor ) and then refined per frame by joint correspondence and gradient opti-mization (intrinsics frozen). The per-frame refinement drifts: by a median of 2.1 ◦ from the anchor and by up to 25 ◦ in some sequences, which is implausible for a rigid mount. An independent check – the spread of LiDAR depth inside SAM2 animal masks, which grows when background leaks into the mask – favours the anchor wherever the drift exceeds 5 ◦ (in-mask IQR 0.83 vs. 1.27 m; 116 vs. 50 LiDAR points on the animal), and the two agree below 1 ◦. We therefore use the anchor extrinsic for all ground truth. The depth maps shipped with the public second rig record were projected with the per-frame extrinsics and are affected in the high-drift sequences; we release re-projected maps with WildDepth-v2. The radar →LiDAR extrinsic is a single rigid transform, because the two sensors share one mount; it was initialized by aligning static structure and refined manually on one sequence. We measured its residual on 827k static radar returns across all 37 sequences, matching each to the nearest accumulated-LiDAR point in the ground plane, and found it strongly anisotropic : a median of 0.10 m in range and azimuth (75th percentile 0.26 m) but 1.84 m in elevation (Fig. 10), a direct consequence of the radar’s few elevation bins. This anisotropy governs every radar experiment in this paper: projecting a radar return into the image can move it by several animal heights vertically, so we always associate radar to an animal at the object level, the robust median range of the radar returns whose projection falls in the animal’s box and never per pixel. We release the extrinsic, the residual analysis, and the association code.

Motion segmentation (T4). Precision is the fraction of points labelled moving that fall inside an animal box; densification is the ratio of moving LiDAR points to moving radar returns. Agreement with an independent motion label is measured per animal against Farnebäck optical flow [19] between consecutive frames (median flow in the mask minus background median flow >1 px).

4.3 Baseline Methods 

Radar-anchored metric scale (RC). Radar returns are transformed to the LiDAR frame and then to the camera. For each animal box b we collect the radar returns whose projection falls within the box columns (azimuth is accurate) and within the box rows padded by one box height on either side (elevation is not). These returns mix the animal with ground and background seen past it, so their median is biased far. We compare four LiDAR-free estimators of the animal’s range rb – the median, the nearest cluster of ≥3 returns within 2 m, the densest such cluster, and the median of the returns within ±35% of the MDE’s own metric prediction, and select one on development sequences (Sec. 5.3). Doppler transfer to LiDAR (motion masks). Radar marks motion but sparsely; LiDAR is dense but static in time. We transfer Doppler to LiDAR by nearest-neighbour search in an anisotropic met-ric: each LiDAR point inherits the Doppler of the nearest radar return within an ellipsoid of radii (0 .6, 1.0, 1.5) m in ( x, y, z ), the elongated vertical axis absorbing the radar’s elevation error. LiDAR points inheriting |h| > 0.5 m/s are labelled moving. Doppler on a moving platform is dominated by ego-motion, which follows a cosine in azimuth; per frame we fit h(ϕ) = vx cos ϕ + vy sin ϕ + c with iterative inlier re-fitting [28] and treat the frame as ego-motion if R2 > 0.5 or the fitted speed exceeds 1 m/s with more than 70% inliers. 9Motion-aware LiDAR accumulation. A single LiDAR sweep is too sparse on a distant animal, so dense depth is obtained by accumulating sweeps over a short window and fusing them with an MDE prior (MoGe-2) via RANSAC scale-shift alignment on SAM2-masked points, with a Sobel boundary filter against telephoto edge artefacts [9, 21, 26]. Accumulation is valid only for still animals; a moving animal is smeared. Our gated variant marks an animal as moving when at least three of the LiDAR points in its mask inherit |h| > 0.5 m/s through the Doppler transfer and, for those animals only, uses the current sweep alone. We evaluate on static-platform frames, where past sweeps are already registered, with windows of 5, 15 and 30 sweeps, and compare against a gate driven by optical flow. 

Metric track lifting. 2D tracks are formed by SORT-style IoU association [8] of MegaDetector de-tections with Hungarian matching (IoU ≥0.3, tracks kept alive for 5 frames). Each observation is back-projected at its mask centroid with a depth from one of the sources – LiDAR (reference), MoGe-2, UC or LC-scene per frame, a LiDAR scale fixed on the first frame of the track, object-level radar range, or MoGe-2 scaled by the median radar/MoGe-2 ratio over the track – and every trajectory is smoothed with the same constant-velocity Kalman filter and RTS smoother [25]. 

# 5 Experiments 

We first re-establish the RGB–LiDAR baselines on the released data and the hidden test (Secs. 5.1–5.2), then study what radar adds for metric scale (Sec. 5.3) and motion (Secs. 5.4–5.5), evaluate metric 3D tracking (Sec. 5.6), and close with radar’s operating envelope (Sec. 5.7). LiDAR is the metric refer-ence throughout. All radar-subset experiments run on every synchronized July-8 frame (19,716 frames): MegaDetector V6 (confidence ≥0.3), SAM2.1 masks, and MoGe-2 depth are computed once per frame, and every number is produced by a released script ( Code availability ). Ground truth uses the anchor LiDAR →camera extrinsic of each sequence (Sec. 3.2). 

5.1 Monocular Distance to Animals (T1) 

Released snippets. We evaluate seven MDEs – Depth Anything v1/v2/v3 [58], DPT [45], Depth Pro [10], MonoDepth2 [30], and MoGe-2 [54] – on 11,498 animal detections from 3,695 frames of the released snippets (every 10th frame, at most 40 per snippet; re-weighting by true frame counts moves the median ground-truth distance only from 20.5 to 21.3 m). Table 4 summarizes the results. Uncalibrated predictions are unreliable in absolute terms (AbsRel 0.38–0.83, δ< 1.25 of 0.26–0.46). Per-frame LiDAR calibration roughly halves RMSE for every model and raises δ< 1.25 to 0.50–0.79. MoGe-2, Depth Any-thing v3 and v1 are the strongest calibrated models (AbsRel ≈0.15), and error is highest in the 30–50 m bin for every model (Fig. 5). 

Hidden test, Track A. Table 5 reports organiser baselines on the frozen hidden test, scored by the public scoring program against the checksum-verified reference. MoGe-2 is clearly strongest (LC AbsRel 0.094), followed by the metric-outdoor Depth Anything V2 (0.169) and the diffusion-based Marigold (0.252). Models that predict relative disparity (DPT, relative Depth Anything V2) are handicapped when inverted to depth for an RGB-only submission, because the scorer’s affine alignment operates in depth; we report them for completeness but recommend metric or affine-invariant-depth models for Track A.

5.3 Radar as a Metric Anchor (T3) 

Can object-level radar range replace LiDAR as the anchor that gives monocular depth its metric scale? We compare anchor sources on 48,723 animals in 18,879 frames (Table 7). All methods are scored against the same held-out ground truth: each mask’s LiDAR points are split at random, one half defines the ground truth and the other half is the LC-box anchor, so no LiDAR baseline is scored on its own anchor points. The LiDAR-free radar range estimator was chosen on odd-numbered (development) sequences (Table 8) and all results are reported on the even-numbered (test) sequences. Four findings follow. (i) Radar is usable only at the object level. Per-pixel radar anchors (RC-pixel) are worse than no anchor at all (AbsRel 0.48), because the elevation error places returns on the wrong image rows. (ii) Object-level radar beats scene-level LiDAR calibration and the MDE alone, without LiDAR. RC-box reaches AbsRel 0.120 (median error 0.60 m) against 0.198 for the scene-level LiDAR calibration used in WildDepth and 0.146 for MoGe-2’s own metric output. A scene-wide affine fit is dominated by ground and background points, which is why it can hurt a model that is already metric. (iii) Where radar sees the animal, its range is nearly as good as LiDAR. On the 15,196 test animals with an associated radar range (73%), that range alone has AbsRel 0.064, against 0.144 for MoGe-2 and 0.046 for held-out LiDAR on the same animals. 5.4 Doppler as a Motion Cue (T4) 

Where is Doppler on animals? On the vehicle-mounted rig most Doppler is ego-motion: the per-frame cosine fit flags 35% of frames as ego-motion frames, which we exclude, leaving 12,596 static-platform frames that contain animals. In these, Doppler above 0.5 m/s is associated with 8.5% of animal detections, concentrated in walking herds. 

Doppler transfer yields dense motion masks. In the 2,783 static-platform frames where an animal box contains at least three moving radar returns, transferring Doppler to single-sweep LiDAR (Sec. 4.3) multiplies the number of moving points by 3.7 ×; 72% of transferred moving LiDAR points fall on an animal, against 65% of the raw moving radar returns (Table 9). Precision varies by sequence from 0.39 – where moving safari vehicles share the view – to 0.96–0.98 on walking camel herds (Fig. 7). The anisotropic association radius is what makes the transfer dense: an isotropic 0.6 m radius yields only 1.75 × densification at the same precision. 5.5 Motion-Aware Densification 

Temporal LiDAR accumulation gives dense, coherent surfaces for still animals (Fig. 11) but can smear moving ones. We test whether Doppler gating (Sec. 4.3) helps, with identical LiDAR densification in all rows and per-pixel error measured against a held-out half of the current sweep (Table 10). The answer depends on the accumulation window. Over 0.5 s (5 sweeps), accumulation helps even moving animals and gating is unnecessary. Over 1.5 s gating reduces the error on Doppler-flagged animals by 11%, and over 3 s by 37% (2.60 →1.64 m), with gate precision rising from 0.54 to 0.85. Across all animals at 3 s, an optical-flow gate is better than the Doppler gate (2.08 vs. 2.17 m) because it fires on more of the movers; Doppler is the more precise gate, and the only one that does not depend on illumination.

Trajectory accuracy. MoGe-2 alone places animals 2.2 m from their true position (distance AbsRel 0.149), and per-frame calibration from a single LiDAR point (UC) is far worse (6.3 m), because its frame-to-frame scale jitter (1.7 m) becomes spurious motion. Per-frame scene-level LiDAR calibration reduces the error to 1.5 m. Calibrating once per track is much better than calibrating every frame: a LiDAR scale taken from the first frame only gives 0.52 m. Without any LiDAR, scaling MoGe-2 by the median radar/MoGe-2 ratio over the track gives 0.58 m position error, 3.3% distance error and 0.10 m/s speed error, matching the once-per-track LiDAR calibration and improving on MoGe-2 alone by a factor of four. Radar range alone is equally accurate in position but twice as jittery (0.72 m). 

Application: distance sampling. Distance-sampling abundance estimates depend on the distribution 

of detection distances [11, 24]. Fitting a half-normal detection function to per-track distances (Fig. 8), MoGe-2 alone underestimates its scale σ by 13% (the distance distribution differs from LiDAR’s, KS 

p < 10 −6); with a per-track radar scale the bias falls to −2.1% and the distributions are statistically indistinguishable (KS p = 0 .58). 

Can Doppler measure animal speed? For 29,347 track observations with radar returns at the animal’s range, in-box Doppler correlates with the LiDAR range rate at r = 0 .57 ( r = 0 .61 on the static platform). On moving animals (range rate above 0.3 m/s, n = 3 ,080) the correlation is r = 0 .60 and the sign agrees 72% of the time, but Doppler under-reads the radial speed by about half (Theil–Sen slope 0.47, median error 0.29 m/s; Fig. 9), consistent with limb micro-Doppler [15] and static returns mixing into the box. Doppler thus measures the direction and approximate magnitude of radial motion; it is a usable, if biased, velocity observation for a tracker. The spread of in-box Doppler also increases with speed (Spearman 0.54), which suggests it as a cue for activity. This corrects an earlier analysis of these data that reported no correlation.

No coverage extension and no foliage penetration. Moving radar returns without nearby LiDAR exist but are vehicles, foliage, or far clutter – none are animals after a depth-consistency check – and a search for animals seen through vegetation found only artefacts; the Longleat enclosures are open grassland, and the vegetated sites have no radar. 

Doppler does not clean calibration anchors. Removing moving points from the anchors changes the calibration error by +0.01 m over 2,426 animals in an earlier analysis on 32 sequences. 

Automatic RGB–LiDAR offset refinement fails. For the MorphoCam rig, maximizing agreement between LiDAR depth edges and RGB gradients over image shifts produced optima that tracked the search bound, and 11 of 13 scenes failed an independent in-mask spread check; edge agreement has no interior optimum on sparse LiDAR in natural scenes. 

Per-frame extrinsic refinement can drift. The per-frame LiDAR →camera extrinsics of the CPS2 rig drift away from the sequence anchor by up to 25 ◦; where the drift exceeds 5 ◦, the anchor extrinsic puts more than twice as many LiDAR points on the animal (116 vs. 50) with a tighter in-mask depth spread (0.83 vs. 1.27 m). We therefore use the anchor extrinsic throughout (Sec. 3.2).

# 6 Discussion 

What radar is for in the wild. Our results separate three roles that are often conflated. As a 

dense depth sensor , radar is not (yet) competitive with LiDAR: it is much sparser on the animal and its vertical extrinsic error rules out per-pixel use. As radar MIMO antenna count increases with technological improvements, point cloud density and angular resolution will increase, making it a viable dense sensor. As a scale anchor , it is genuinely useful: object-level radar range calibrates a monocular model better than scene-level LiDAR calibration, and on the three quarters of animals where it sees them its range is within 6% of LiDAR – on a sensor rig that costs and consumes a fraction of a LiDAR. As a motion sensor , it measures something neither commonly available LiDAR nor a single image can: which returns are moving now, and in which radial direction. Transferred onto LiDAR it gives dense motion masks, and it can decide when multi-second temporal fusion is safe. 

Implications for tracking. For metric tracking, the temporal structure of depth error matters more than its per-frame average: a scale that jitters from frame to frame appears as motion. For ecological inference the consequence is concrete: monocular depth alone biases the scale of a distance-sampling detection function by −13%, which would inflate density estimates, whereas a per-track radar scale reduces the bias to −2%. Doppler is a biased but informative velocity observation (r = 0.60, slope 0.47 on moving animals), suitable as a radial-velocity measurement in a tracker’s filter once the gain is calibrated. 

Implications for sensor design. Elevation resolution drives the dominant extrinsic error and blocks per-pixel radar–camera fusion; a static or ego-compensated mount matters because ego-motion swamped the Doppler in 35% of our frames; and extrinsic calibration should be validated against an independent signal – in our data the per-frame refined extrinsics drifted by up to 25 ◦ while their own optimization objective reported success. An approach to increasingly elevation angular resolution is to use a second radar (V) mounted orthogonally to the original radar (H). In addition, it should be noted that the Altos radar is optimized for automotive applications in terms of its modulation parameters and signal processing chain – a suggested next step would be to capture raw I/Q mmWave radar for custom signal processing. 

Limitations. Radar is available at one site, on one day, in daylight and in open enclosures; its illu-mination invariance and its behaviour in vegetation are observed rather than measured here. The radar results are strongest below 30 m, where most of our animals are, and radar range becomes unreliable beyond it. The best radar range estimator uses the monocular model’s metric prediction to select returns and is therefore an RGB+radar method; the purely radar estimator has a heavy tail. The radar →LiDAR extrinsic is hand-refined on one sequence, and LiDAR ground truth inherits the calibration and synchro-nization errors we characterize rather than eliminate. The tracking reference is itself LiDAR-derived and our tracker is a simple IoU tracker; identity-level metrics (HOTA, IDF1) require identity labels we do not yet have. Future work. A night-time RGB–thermal–radar collection (the CPS2 rig also carries a thermal camera) is the natural next step to measure the low-light regime that most motivates radar; identity-verified herd sequences would allow testing whether range and Doppler improve association; and a session-disjoint, multi-site hidden test would turn the benchmark into a generalization test. 

# 7 Conclusion 

WildDepth-v2 extends WildDepth from a static RGB–LiDAR depth dataset to a benchmark for metric perception of animals over time. It adds the first synchronized RGB–LiDAR–radar recordings of animals with a measured calibration and timing error budget, a frozen hidden test with a public scorer, and tasks for metric scale, motion segmentation, and 3D tracking. Monocular depth models remain meters off at wildlife ranges without a metric anchor; radar supplies that anchor cheaply – enough to track animals in metric 3D and to remove most of the distance-sampling bias of monocular depth without LiDAR – and, through Doppler, adds a motion signal that LiDAR lacks, while its elevation error and attenuated Doppler set clear limits. We release the data, calibration, benchmark, and code, and hope they help turn the millions of existing monocular wildlife images and tracks into metric measurements that ecologists can use.
