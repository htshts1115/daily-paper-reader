Dense Endoluminal Mapping and Localisation
from Monocular Bronchoscopy
Salvatore Esposito1, Matias Mattamala1, Daniel Rebain2, Liang Zhao1, Kev Dhaliwal1, Mohsen Khadem1,
Subramanian Ramamoorthy1
1University of Edinburgh

Abstract—Bronchoscopy inserts a camera through the airways
to diagnose endoluminal disease. Locating the camera along
the airway usually requires additional equipment, such as an
electromagnetic tracker and a preoperative CT scan. In this
paper, we investigate the use of the monocular camera stream to
localise the bronchoscope without requiring additional sensing.
We introduce a monocular SLAM system that localises the tip
of a bronchoscope and reconstructs the airway surface around it
from RGB video alone. A Depth Anything 3 backbone, fine-tuned
on over a million photorealistic airway renderings, returns metric
per-pixel depth and is the system’s only source of scale. DINOv3
features routed through a custom cross-attention matcher supply
sub-pixel correspondences on mucosa where classical descriptors
fail. Both enter a factor graph alongside a dense constraint
from projective iterative closest point (ICP) registration against
the system’s own running reconstruction, so surface evidence
stabilises the directions the visual factors leave weakly observable.
On six airways rendered from patient CT scans, the system
reaches 26.1 mm mean absolute trajectory error and 77.5 % mean
surface F-score. Its mean surface accuracy exceeds that of five
recent monocular SLAM and feed-forward baselines. MASt3R-
SLAM, which fits a free scale to the ground truth, has a lower
mean trajectory error (22.0 mm). ORB-SLAM3 completes only
two of the six airways, whereas our system completes all six and
also reconstructs two ex vivo specimens.

I. INTRODUCTION
B
RONCHOSCOPY is a procedure for examining and
sampling the airway [1]. Asthma, chronic obstructive
pulmonary disease, cystic fibrosis and endoluminal tumours
affect the airway walls or obstruct the lumen [2]–[5]. In each
case an operator advances a bronchoscope to a specific airway
and works under direct vision to sample or inspect it.
In current practice the tip is located with equipment brought
in alongside the bronchoscope: an electromagnetic field gen-
erator tracks the catheter, and that tracking is registered to a
computed tomography (CT) airway map acquired before the
procedure [7]. This locates the catheter but recovers nothing
about the surface it observes, and its accuracy is bounded by
the CT-to-patient registration and by repeat CT imaging that
accumulates ionising radiation [8].
The camera at the tip of the bronchoscope passes within
millimetres of the tissue. Some robotic bronchoscopes support
navigation [9] and camera-pose estimation [10], without map-
ping the surface. Dense reconstruction has been demonstrated
mainly in the gastrointestinal tract [11], [12]. In the lung, the
airway reconstruction we are aware of anchors its geometry
to a preoperative CT scan [13].

Four properties of endoluminal imaging stand between the
video and that goal. First, the mucosa lacks enough texture
to enable feature-based tracking, so endoscopic adaptations
of established visual SLAM systems such as ORB-SLAM
produce sparse maps and cannot hold one track from the start
of a sequence to its end [11], [12], [14]. Second, a single
camera recovers geometry only up to an unknown factor, so
neither the trajectory nor the map built by monocular SLAM
reflects the real dimensions of the lumen. Third, the light
source travels with the camera, so specular highlights slide
across the tissue instead of staying fixed to it, and the depth
estimated at those pixels is corrupted [15]. Lastly, the airway
is locally a tube, which leaves rotation about its axis and
translation along it weakly observable, similar to the problem
LiDAR SLAM faces in tunnels [16].
In this work, we introduce a monocular mapping and
localisation system that addresses these four challenges, shown
in Fig. 1. The system represents the airway wall as a truncated
signed distance field (TSDF), a voxel grid in which each voxel
stores its signed distance to the nearest surface, clipped to
a narrow band around the surface. Our contributions are as
follows.
1) Bronchoscopy metric depth predictor. A fine-tuned depth
estimator that supplies the system’s metric scale and per-
pixel uncertainty.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Medical Visual SLAM and Reconstruction
Medical visual SLAM is more visually challenging than
outdoor SLAM because the tissue lacks the texture ordi-
nary scenes provide. For example, ORB-SLAM [18] relies
on extracted keypoints from corners; LSD-SLAM [19] and
DSO [20] align images by their pixel intensities instead, rely-
ing on a constant brightness assumption. The mucosa breaks
these assumptions, which has been observed when transferring
these methods to endoscopic applications, shown by a reduced
number of matches and sparser maps [14]. For this reason,
prior works have presented colonoscopy-specific approaches,
such as handheld monocular reconstruction [11], multi-map
colonoscopy SLAM [12] to target the gastrointestinal tract, or
self-supervised depth predictors on endoscopic video [21].
Other endoscopic systems estimate tissue deformation to-
gether with the camera. DefSLAM [22] and NR-SLAM [23]
recover a deforming surface from monocular video, MIS-
SLAM [24] couples ORB-SLAM with dense deformable
tracking on stereoscope video, and Zhang et al. carry the same
treatment into endovascular intervention [25]. These methods
model deforming tissue. The airways show little deformation
in our ex vivo ventilated-lung video, so we assume they are
rigid and do not compare against these methods (Sec. IX-H).
Bronchoscopy research itself prioritises navigation over
reconstruction. Catheter localisation against a preoperative
CT airway map is the goal of bronchoscopy registration,
electromagnetic tracking, and learned shared-control assistants
such as the AI co-pilot bronchoscope robot [9]. These recover
the pose of the scope relative to the scan, and none reconstructs
the lumen surface. Concurrent work fits dense Gaussian-splat
reconstructions of bronchoscopy by coupling them to a paired
inhale/exhale preoperative CT scan [13], but still relies on
the volumetric prior to anchor the geometry. The closest
precedent is Deng et al. [10], who benchmarked feature-
based visual odometry on bronchoscopy phantoms and ex vivo
lungs against electromagnetic-tracking ground truth but did
not produce a surface map. Of the systems above, each either
anchors its geometry to a preoperative scan or stops at the
catheter pose without reconstructing the lumen.
B. Learning and Hybrid Approaches to 3D Reconstruction
Such a system now needs measurements available from a
single image. DINOv3 [26] describes every image patch with
a learned feature vector, and those vectors stay distinctive on
surfaces where ORB and SIFT find no corners to describe.
Depth Anything 3 [27] predicts depth, confidence and ray map
from a single image, where stereo and structure from motion
need several views to triangulate it. VGGT [28] predicts the
geometry itself, returning camera parameters, depth maps and
point maps for a set of images in one pass and without any
geometric optimisation. DROID-SLAM [29] also reconstructs
with an end-to-end model, but keeps an explicit optimisation
and learns the update it applies to optical flow, depth and pose.
Several SLAM systems build on these feed-forward models.
MASt3R-SLAM [30] tracks against the per-pixel 3D points a
two-view model predicts and fuses them in real time, VGGT-
SLAM 2.0 [31] aligns per-chunk reconstructions on the SL(4)
manifold, and ViSTA-SLAM [32] pairs a two-view front end
with a pose graph. Endo3R [33] adapts the same idea to
surgical video, predicting globally aligned point maps and
camera poses from monocular endoscopy. In each, the learned
model predicts the geometry and the back end combines the
predictions.
Building upon these methods, we use learnt components
in the front end to mitigate the four challenges described in
the Introduction (Sec. I, which are trained on bronchoscopy-
oriented data. However, we still rely on a factor graph
formulation to fuse the different geometrical cues and their
uncertainties, making our system hybrid.
III. SYSTEM OVERVIEW
Fig. 2 summarises our system. It takes monocular RGB
bronchoscopy video with known intrinsics, and returns a
metric trajectory of the scope tip together with a dense
reconstruction of the endoluminal surface.
For every frame the front end predicts a metric depth
map with per-pixel reliability from a bronchoscopy-adapted
Depth Anything 3 (DA3) backbone [27], and extracts dense
DINOv3 patch descriptors that stay discriminative on mucosa.
The system keeps two maps: a sparse landmark map of
3D points back-projected from those measurements, and a
dense surface map stored as a truncated signed distance field
(TSDF). For each new keyframe, a trained matcher links the
current descriptors to the landmarks predicted to be visible,
and PnP on the surviving correspondences supplies an initial
pose. This pose is used to optimise the factor graph with
the visual landmarks and motion model, and returns a visual-
only pose. That pose initialises a projective point-to-plane ICP
registration against the local TSDF submap, whose surviving
correspondences re-enter the same graph for a second update.
Only the fused pose are used for TSDF integration.

IV. FRONT-END
The front end produces three outputs per frame: metric
depth with per-pixel reliability, a dense descriptor field, and
the 3D landmark candidates that combine them.
A. Monocular Metric Depth Estimation
For every frame we predict a dense metric depth map
Dt with the DA3 backbone, fine-tuned on bronchoscopy
renderings with ground truth depth. Fine-tuning adapts the
predictor to the illumination and diameter of small airways
and fixes its absolute scale. This predictor is the only source
of metric scale in the system.
A second head predicts a per-pixel depth uncertainty. We
train it to output the scale St, in millimetres, of a Laplace
distribution over log-depth, because its heavier tails keep
specular and occluded pixels, where depth error is largest, from
dominating the fit. We combine St with two geometric terms
and one temporal term into a per-pixel reliability

B. Dense Features and Landmark Initialisation
We extract dense image features from a frozen DINOv3
ViT-7B/16 backbone [26]. It produces a 42 × 42 patch grid
of 4096-D descriptors from each 672 × 672 RGB frame. The
matcher of Sec. VI-A projects these into a 256-D matching
space, and the map stores those vectors. We use DINOv3
because its features stay distinctive on textureless mucosa.
Sec. IX-D substitutes ORB into the pipeline to measure the
difference.
Fig. 3 shows an example of sparse keypoint tracking on two
ex vivo sequences as the scope advances through the airway. A
match is kept when the similarity of its best candidate exceeds
that of the second-best candidate, or of the no-match option,
by more than 0.05 (the margin test), and when tracking it
back to the source frame lands within 16 px of its origin (the
cycle test). Several hundred of the 1764 patches pass both tests
at each separation, and 66–123 per sequence pass at all four
separations.
Depth and descriptors differ in resolution: a descriptor
covers a 16 × 16 patch, whereas depth is predicted per pixel.
We compute features on a resized, patch-aligned copy of the
frame, so we scale the matcher’s coordinates back to the
original image before applying the intrinsics. A single K is
then used everywhere and depth is not rescaled.
Each patch is assigned one depth and one reliability, both
area-averaged over the pixels it covers. Patches with non-
positive depth are discarded, as are those whose reliability Q
falls below 0.15; that floor is held fixed across every sequence
reported here. Back-projecting the centre of a surviving patch
through K at its depth gives a 3D point, which inherits the
patch descriptor.

We update the maps from the camera pose of each keyframe,

described in Sec. VI.
A. Maps
The sparse landmark map holds the 3D points that carry the
visual factors. The dense map is the TSDF, which we integrate
from posed depth frames. Because the integration uses the
fused pose, Sec. VII-C describes it with the back end.

• its position ℓj

• its five most recent descriptors, and the representative

• the keyframes that have observed it;
• its state, tentative or confirmed.
A landmark starts as tentative and becomes confirmed at its

C. Updating the Map with New Observations
For a new keyframe t, the predicted pose of Sec. VI
selects candidate landmarks by field of view, positive depth,
and TSDF occlusion. Our matcher (Sec. VI-A) then proposes
correspondences between those candidates and the current
patches. We filter proposals by descriptor tests, visibility and
depth consistency, AP3P RANSAC, and a projection-residual
check after optimisation. Only accepted matches create obser-
vation records and descriptor updates.
D. Landmark Birth
Patches that match no existing landmark are considered for
birth once the current pose has been accepted and optimised.
Birth requires the reliability and depth tests of Sec. IV-B,
a keyframe labelled stable (Sec. VI), and a duplicate check
against nearby landmarks. We initialise the new landmark
with Eq. (4) evaluated at the accepted pose, and it enters the
graph as in Sec. V-B.
The map stores the 256-D descriptors that the matcher
compares (the projection of the 4096-D DINOv3 vectors), and
matches against whichever of a landmark’s five lies closest to
their mean. When a landmark is re-observed, we average the
new descriptor with that representative at rate 0.1, re-normalise
it, and add it to the bank in place of the raw one. This lets
the stored appearance follow gradual change.
VI. CAMERA POSE TRACKING
We localise each new keyframe against the map in two
steps: descriptor matching gives 2D–3D correspondences, and
PnP gives an initial pose. The factor graph of Sec. VII then
refines it together with the map.
A. Image-to-Map Matching
The landmarks predicted to be visible (Sec. V) are matched
with the current frame’s patches at sub-pixel accuracy, using a
custom-trained cross-attention matcher. Its architecture, train-
ing data, and losses are described in Appendix A-B.
Matches are accepted by the margin and cycle tests of
Sec. IV-B and carry the projection-noise model given there.
On the ex vivo sequences we choose the reference keyframes
by estimated pixel displacement, targeting separations of 0, 15
and 30 px. Close references give reliable matches, and distant
ones support triangulation.
B. Pose Initialisation
PnP. Given the landmark correspondences and the current
graph estimates ℓj
W, AP3P RANSAC (3000 iterations, 3.0 px
reprojection threshold) estimates a pose hypothesis, refined
on its inliers. An inlier floor of min(25, max(20, 0.08 nraw))
rejects solutions backed by too few correspondences. The floor
rises from 20 to 25 as the candidate count grows. If RANSAC
fails, iterative PnP on the same correspondences is the fallback,
with the landmark coordinates held at their current graph
estimates. If both fail, a constant-velocity model predicts the
pose, and a weak between-pose factor ties the new keyframe
to the previous one.
Pose quality. A keyframe is marked stable only when the
primary RANSAC pose estimate succeeds with enough inliers.
Only stable keyframes extend the map: the others keep the
trajectory connected, but cannot create landmarks, update
descriptors, or drive TSDF integration. We loosen the between-
pose factor as the pose it follows gets weaker, from 5 mm and
2◦after a RANSAC pose to 50 mm and 10◦after a constant-
velocity one. On the ex vivo sequences, backward motion
beyond 2 mm along the optical axis is rejected and replaced
by the velocity prediction.

B. Point-to-Plane ICP
The first-pass pose rests on a few hundred patch corre-
spondences, which can under-constrain the camera motion.
We therefore register the dense depth prediction against a
local TSDF map of the last 15 keyframes and let the sur-
viving correspondences enter the graph as an extra constraint,
improving both trajectory and surface accuracy (Sec. IX-D).
The registration is standard projective point-to-plane ICP, as
in KinectFusion [35]: ray-cast correspondences, a robust M-
estimator and a damped Gauss-Newton solve, run here against
the TSDF with a custom GPU implementation.

VIII. IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
The system is implemented in Python. The front end builds
on PyTorch modules, and the back end uses the Python
bindings of the GTSAM library. The dense TSDF and the ICP
solver are custom CUDA kernels. The optimisation problem
is implemented with GTSAM and runs on the CPU.
The TSDF uses 0.5 mm voxels on the synthetic sequences
and 1.0 mm on ex vivo data, with a truncation distance of
four voxels. We extract meshes with marching cubes on its
zero level set. Projection factors use isotropic image noise,
σ = 2 px synthetic and 4 px ex vivo, under a Huber kernel.
Depth factors use the standard deviation of Eq. (3), and each
ICP residual uses σicp

G. Computational Performance
We report per-keyframe timings on a single NVIDIA RTX
4090, averaged over the 1,132 keyframes of the six CT-derived
airway runs of Exp. 2, which select every third frame of 30 fps
video. A keyframe is processed synchronously: its pose is
published once tracking, registration and the graph update have
finished. Tracking takes 277 ms (depth preprocessing 61 ms,
matching 60 ms, landmark association 80 ms, PnP 76 ms), ICP
against the local submap 168 ms, and the graph update with
landmark birth 263 ms, of which both iSAM2 passes take
200 ms. Each keyframe is also processed on a copy of the
map state, which replaces the committed state only after it
is validated; the copy takes 1.86 s. A keyframe takes 2.98 s in
total (median 2.87 s, 95th percentile 5.38 s), so the system runs
slower than real time on these sequences. DINOv3 descriptors
are read from a precomputed cache in these runs; extracting
them online adds 200 ms per frame.
H. Limitations
Heavily specular or textureless mucosa weakens both front-
end signals at once. DA3 flags most pixels as unreliable,
which leaves ICP with too few source points against the local
submap, and the DINO matcher finds too few correspondences
to support the projection factors. ICP is also weakest over
the first few keyframes, before the submap holds enough
surface. Diagnosing the ICP Hessian lets the system discard
a degenerate registration, and the visual factors alone then
constrain the pose.
The system performs no loop closure, and each evalu-
ated sequence traverses a single branch without revisiting
it. Exploratory bronchoscopy that withdraws and re-enters a
different branch would revisit mapped geometry, and closing
those loops is required before the system can cover a full
airway tree in one session.
On the ex vivo sequences no ground truth depth is available,
so we can check the inherited metric scale only for internal
consistency. We reconstruct under a rigid-scene assumption,
which holds exactly for the static synthetic sequences. The ex
vivo lungs are ventilated and breathe during acquisition, but
the video shows little deformation of the airways, so we do
not model it. We target the proximal-to-subsegmental airways,
from the primary bronchi down to the 2–4 mm subsegmental
branches, and have not tested larger tidal volumes or the distal
airways, where deformation is likely larger. Modelling the
deformation [22]–[24], or reconstructing at a fixed phase of
the breathing cycle, would address this.
X. CONCLUSION
We introduced a monocular SLAM system that recovers
both the bronchoscope trajectory and a dense reconstruction of
the inner airway surface, without depth sensors or preoperative
scans. It combines a bronchoscopy-tuned depth predictor for
metric scale, a dense matcher for texture-poor mucosa, and
a two-pass factor graph that fuses those visual measurements
with a dense ICP constraint against the system’s own recon-
struction.
On six CT-derived airways, ours has the most accurate
surface of the six systems, with 77.47 % mean F-score against
74.17 % for the strongest baseline, and a mean ATE of
26.13 mm against 22.02 mm for MASt3R-SLAM.
Our results demonstrate that accurate camera pose and a
dense endoluminal surface can therefore be recovered from
bronchoscopy video alone, with no added hardware and no

B. Cross-Attention Matcher Training
The DINO feature matcher of Sec. VI-A maps the DINOv3
patch descriptors of a source and a target frame into a 256-
D matching space and predicts coarse patch assignments and
a sub-pixel offset, supervised on frame pairs with known
correspondences.
Supervision. Ground truth correspondences come from frame
pairs with known dense flow: pairs are precomputed once, each
patch of the source frame carrying either a target location, an
explicit invalid label where the surface leaves the field of view,
or an ignore label where the correspondence is occluded or


14
uncertain. The third state matters: the mucosa self-occludes at
every fold, and a two-state formulation would train the matcher
to guess there. The loss excludes occluded patches.
Losses. We consider a cross-entropy over coarse patch assign-
ment; a Charbonnier regression on the sub-pixel offset, eval-
uated on valid patches only; and a forward-backward cycle-
consistency term (weight 0.5) that penalises correspondences
which do not return to their origin. These terms are applied
in both directions for each pair.
Optimisation. Descriptors are precomputed with the frozen
backbone, which makes large batches affordable: AdamW,
batch size 64, learning rate 3 × 10−4, weight decay 0.01, one
warm-up epoch, on 400 px crops. Training runs to a maximum
of 20 epochs with early stopping (patience 4 epochs, minimum
improvement 0.5 %); the deployed checkpoint is the best of 15
completed epochs.
