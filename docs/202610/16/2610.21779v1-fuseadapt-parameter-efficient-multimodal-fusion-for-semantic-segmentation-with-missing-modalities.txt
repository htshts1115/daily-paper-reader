Abstract 

Multi-modal semantic segmentation benefits from complementary sensors, but this benefit is fragile when some modalities are degraded or missing at test time. Many existing methods rely on dedicated fusion modules or multi-branch cross-modal encoders, and the fusion often becomes unreliable when modalities are absent, leading to substantial performance drops. We propose FuseAdapt , a parameter-efficient framework that performs fusion within the adaptation space of a frozen vision foundation model (VFM), exploring whether VFM priors can serve as an effective shared backbone for multi-modal segmentation. Instead of attach-ing a standalone fusion network, FuseAdapt assigns each modality a lightweight adaptation branch and performs adaptation-space fusion by keeping Q/V low-rank adaptation private to each modality and routing only FFN low-rank residual activations across modalities inside the frozen backbone. The pretrained VFM remains fixed while modality-specific corrections and explicit cross-modal interac-tion are learned through the adaptation paths. To handle missing modalities, we further introduce Predictive Modality Modeling (PMM) , a direct representation-level objective for cross-modal redundancy. During training, PMM masks a tar-get modality and predicts its embedding from the visible ones, supervised by a teacher target computed under full-modality input. PMM is used only during training and adds no inference-time overhead. Across three multi-modal seg-mentation benchmarks (MCubeS, DELIVER, MUSES), FuseAdapt consistently improves both modality-complete and modality-incomplete segmentation with a small trainable budget, yielding up to +3.01 mIoU under full modalities and +12.56 mIoU under missing modalities over prior state-of-the-art. 1 Introduction 

Scene understanding often benefits from combining multiple sensing modalities [Huang et al., 2022, Jia et al., 2023, Park et al., 2017, Zhang et al., 2018]. RGB [Wei et al., 2024, Zhang and Tan, 2025, Zhang et al., 2026, 2024a, Li et al., 2025a, Yuan et al., 2026a, Zhou et al., 2026, Zhang et al., 2024b, Zhang and Chen, 2023], depth [Cao et al., 2021, Song et al., 2022, Ying and Chuah, 2022], LiDAR [Yan et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2022, Li et al., 2023], and event streams [Xia et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2021a, Li et al., 2025b] provide complementary cues about appearance, geometry, and motion. In practice, however, this multi-sensor benefit is fragile: a modality may be noisy [Yang et al., 2026a,b], degraded, spatially imperfect, or unavailable at inference time. Such cases are common in autonomous driving [Caesar et al., 2020, Li et al., 2026] and embodied settings [Wang et al., 2024], where illumination, weather [Du et al., 2026, Teng et al., 2025, Yang et al., 2025], texture, sensor failure, or calibration conditions [Yuan et al., 2025a] can affect sensors unequally. A practical multi-modal segmentation model should therefore work in two settings: modality-complete semantic segmentation (MCSS), where all modalities are available and should be fused effectively,

Existing multi-modal segmentation methods often trade off modality-specific modeling, compactness, and robustness. Separate-encoder designs fuse intermediate features from different modalities [Broe-dermann et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2023a], but can increase parameters and computation as the number of sensors grows. RGB-centric methods treat RGB as the main input and use other modalities as auxiliary cues [Zhang et al., 2023b], which can be limiting when RGB is degraded or absent. Shared-encoder and late-fusion methods improve compactness [Zheng et al., 2024c,b], but their shared stream can favor the most informative or easiest modality, making the contribution of other sensors less stable. For missing-modality robustness, recent methods go beyond modality dropout by using text anchors [Zheng et al., 2024b], subset consistency [Zheng et al., 2024c,a], or modality regularization [Zheng et al., 2025]. These strategies provide indirect supervision for dense prediction: text anchors are coarse, and subset consistency can be affected by cross-modal mismatch. We study how to separate sensor-specific adaptation from cross-sensor communication within a shared frozen backbone. FuseAdapt keeps modality-specific Q/V low-rank residuals private and restricts receiver-conditioned, token-wise exchange to FFN low-rank residual activations (Fig. 1(c)). The frozen dense FFN transformation stays on the receiving stream, while routing is normalized over the available sensors. We call this constraint on the trainable communication path adaptation-space fusion . The exchanged tensors remain intermediate features; the contribution is the separation of private attention adaptation and cross-modal residual communication, rather than a new tensor domain or the individual use of LoRA and routing. A shared VFM supplies the pretrained representation, and controlled experiments with MiT backbones test whether the benefit depends on that representation. To handle missing modalities, we introduce Predictive Modality Modeling (PMM) (Fig. 2(c)). PMM masks a target modality during training and predicts its representation from the visible modalities, supervised by a teacher target computed under full-modality input. PMM applies the joint-embedding predictive principle at modality level: target-specific queries predict an unobserved sensor’s latent representation from visible context. Its EMA teacher supplies a temporally smoothed, stop-gradient target. After projection onto a common token grid, each query can attend to all visible positions rather than imposing same-index visible-to-target regression. This does not remove the common-grid requirement of routing and averaging. The teacher and predictor are used only during training. We evaluate FuseAdapt on MCubeS [Liang et al., 2022], DELIVER [Zhang et al., 2023b], and MUSES [Brödermann et al., 2024] under both MCSS and MISS protocols. FuseAdapt yields up to +3.01 mIoU under full modalities and +12.56 mIoU under missing modalities over prior state-of-the-art, with ablations isolating the contributions of modality-specific adaptation, routed cross-modal exchange, PMM, and the choice of frozen VFM. Our contributions are: • FuseAdapt separates modality-private Q/V adaptation from receiver-conditioned cross-modal exchange restricted to FFN low-rank residual activations, with routing adjusted to the visible sensor subset. 2Figure 2: Comparison of designs for robustness under missing modalities (MISS). (a) Semantic-anchor methods align modality features to text embeddings, but text anchors can be coarse for pixel-level segmentation. (b) Semantic-consistency methods enforce agreement across different modality subsets, but such constraints can be affected by cross-modal mismatch. (c) Predictive Modality Modeling (PMM) casts MISS training as conditional representation prediction. Con-trols distinguish its prediction target from output distillation and generic fused-feature matching. • Experiments on three benchmarks, same-backbone PEFT and information-flow controls support the design. We additionally characterize its inference cost and the scope of its robustness. 

2 Related Work 

Multi-Modal Semantic Segmentation Multi-modal semantic segmentation uses complementary RGB [Wei et al., 2024, Zhang and Tan, 2025, Qian et al., 2024, Zhang et al., 2022, Deng et al., 2020], depth [Wang et al., 2020, Ying and Chuah, 2022, Song et al., 2022, Cao et al., 2021, Park et al., 2017], LiDAR [Li et al., 2022, 2023, Zhuang et al., 2021, Yan et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2022], thermal [Hui et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2023c, 2021b, Wu et al., 2022], and event cues [Xia et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2021a, Jia et al., 2023]. Prior work often uses fixed modality pairs such as RGB-depth [Ying and Chuah, 2022, Song et al., 2022], RGB-centric fusion [Zhang et al., 2023b], or shared-backbone fusion with auxiliary alignment [Zheng et al., 2024b,c,a]. In contrast, FuseAdapt keeps a frozen VFM and performs cross-modal exchange through trainable adaptation paths, avoiding a standalone fusion network. 

Missing or Degraded Modalities Existing approaches use modality dropout, cross-modal distil-lation, and consistency regularization [Liu et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2023, Zheng et al., 2024b,c,a]. CRM [Shin et al., 2024] combines complementary RGB-thermal masking with clean-to-masked self-distillation; M3L [Maheshwari et al., 2024] uses a full-modality teacher to supervise masked-modality segmentation. PMM instead conditions on a designated missing sensor and predicts its full-context latent representation. This follows the latent-context prediction principle of I-JEPA [Ass-ran et al., 2023], applied to modalities rather than image regions. Masking, EMA, and teacher–student optimization are established mechanisms; the prediction target and modality conditioning distinguish our objective. 

Parameter-Efficient Adaptation and Fusion Prompt tuning, adapters, and LoRA reduce the cost of adapting pretrained models [Lester et al., 2021, Jia et al., 2022, Houlsby et al., 2019, Chen et al., 2022, Hu et al., 2022, Yan et al., 2025]. StitchFusion [Li et al., 2024] transfers dense features between encoder streams through MultiAdapters. MLE-SAM [Zhu et al., 2024] combines modality-specific LoRA experts and routing with multi-scale feature/head fusion. MoLE [Wu et al., 2024] composes task/concept LoRAs within a single stream. Concurrent M4-SAM [Liu et al., 2026] combines modality-dispatched convolutional experts with gated feature fusion for RGB-D video, while MILE [Muralidhara et al., 2026] selects isolated continual-task experts. FuseAdapt instead keeps Q/V residuals private and restricts directed source-to-receiver communication to FFN low-rank residuals among currently visible sensors. 3 Proposed Method 

We address robust multi-modal semantic segmentation in two regimes: modality-complete semantic segmentation (MCSS), where all modalities are available, and modality-incomplete semantic seg-mentation (MISS), where only a subset is available at test time. Our goal is to adapt a frozen vision foundation model (VFM) into a multi-modal learner that can fuse available modalities with a small trainable budget and learn redundancy for missing-modality robustness. As shown in Fig. 3, the input contains four aligned image-like modalities of the same scene: RGB R ∈

Rh×w×3, depth D ∈ Rh×w×CD

, LiDAR projection L ∈ Rh×w×CL

, and event stack E ∈ Rh×w×CE

,with pixel labels Y over K classes. Following [Zhang et al., 2023b], we set CD = CL = CE = 3 

and feed all modalities as image-like inputs to a shared frozen VFM. FuseAdapt has two components. First, an adaptation-space fusion design (§3.1) performs fusion in the adaptation space of the frozen VFM. Each modality has lightweight trainable adaptation paths, and cross-modal exchange is implemented by routing FFN low-rank residual activations across modalities inside Transformer blocks. The pretrained backbone remains fixed. Second, Predictive Modality Modeling (PMM) improves MISS by training the model to predict a masked modality representation from visible modalities, using a teacher target computed with full-modality context. PMM is used only during training and discarded at inference. 

3.1 Adaptation-Space Fusion 

We realize adaptation-space fusion through modality-specific low-rank adaptation paths inside the frozen VFM, with cross-modal exchange implemented by routed FFN low-rank residual activations. We keep self-attention adaptation modality-specific to preserve sensor-specific correction, and place cross-modal exchange in the FFN adaptation path.

3.2 Predictive Modality Modeling 

While adaptation-space fusion handles full-modality inputs, MISS also requires the model to behave reliably when some modalities are absent. PMM addresses this by adding a representation-level prediction objective during training. Given visible modalities, the model predicts the representation of a masked target modality, supervised by a teacher target computed with full-modality context. This encourages cross-modal redundancy beyond segmentation supervision alone. After the full-input warm-up, training mixes full and masked inputs. In the masked branch, modalities are independently retained with probability 0.5, conditioned on a nonempty visible subset V. When 

V̸ = M, we sample a target t ∈ M \ V uniformly and aggregate visible features ˆXm ∈ RN ×C as above. When all modalities are visible, segmentation supervision remains active and no missing-target loss is computed. Appendix E.3 gives the masking procedure. 4 Experiments 

Datasets We evaluate on three multi-modal segmentation benchmarks covering both modality-complete semantic segmentation (MCSS) and modality-incomplete semantic segmentation (MISS). DELIVER [Zhang et al., 2023b] contains synchronized RGB, depth, LiDAR, and event inputs for 25-class urban-scene segmentation, with 3,983/2,005/1,897 train/val/test samples and diverse adverse conditions such as fog, rain, night, blur, exposure changes, and LiDAR jitter [Yuan et al., 2026b]. MCubeS [Liang et al., 2022] contains 20-class material segmentation with RGB, NIR, DoLP, and AoLP modalities [Lin et al., 2025], using 302/96/102 train/val/test samples. MUSES [Brödermann et al., 2024] provides synchronized frame-camera, event-camera, and MEMS-LiDAR data with 2D panoptic annotations, enabling evaluation with heterogeneous sensors. 

Implementation Details We use AdamW and train on A5000 GPUs: 8 GPUs for DELIVER and 4 GPUs for MCubeS/MUSES. Batch size is 1 per GPU for DELIVER and 4 for MCubeS/MUSES. The initial learning rate is 4 × 10 −4 for DELIVER/MUSES and 3.5 × 10 −4 for MCubeS, with a 10-epoch warm-up at 0.1 ×. We train for 200 epochs on DELIVER/MUSES and 500 epochs on MCubeS. Input resolutions are 1024 × 1024 for DELIVER/MUSES and 512 × 512 for MCubeS. We use standard augmentations, including random resizing (0.5–2.0), horizontal flipping, color jitter, blur, and random cropping. FuseAdapt uses rank 8 for DELIVER and MCubeS, and 4 for MUSES. 4.1 Modality-Complete Semantic Segmentation (MCSS) Results 

We first evaluate MCSS on MCubeS, MUSES, and DELIVER, where all modalities are available during training and inference. As shown in Tabs. 1, 2, and 3, FuseAdapt achieves the best reported mean performance among the compared methods on all three benchmarks with a small trainable budget. On MCubeS, FuseAdapt obtains 57.62 mIoU with all four modalities (RADN) and 56.30 mean mIoU, compared with 53.29 for StitchFusion and 52.43 for MAGIC. On DELIVER, it achieves 64.97 mean mIoU, outperforming StitchFusion (63.68) and Any2Seg (64.18). On MUSES, it reaches 76.14 mean mIoU, improving over the prior reported results across all modality combinations. These results indicate that adapting a frozen VFM with modality-specific paths and routed adaptation exchange provides effective full-modality fusion.

4.3 Ablation Studies 

We ablate FuseAdapt and PMM under the same training recipe and also examine the effect of changing the frozen VFM backbone. 

FuseAdapt Variants (MCSS) Tabs. 1 and 2 compare variants within the same frozen-VFM frame-work. Without FuseAdapt, the model uses naive aggregation of per-modality features and performs worse. Shared FuseAdapt improves performance but uses one adaptation path for all modalities, lim-iting modality-specific correction. Separate FuseAdapt uses modality-specific paths without routed exchange; it improves over Shared on MCubeS, while their MUSES DINOv2-S means are similar. Full FuseAdapt performs best in both tables. This supports the contribution of both modality-specific adaptation and routed adaptation exchange. 

PMM Variants (MISS) Tab. 5 evaluates PMM variants under MISS on MCubeS. Removing the predictor or replacing it with a linear predictor reduces mean mIoU from 52.89 to 43.67 and 49.71, respectively. This shows that the representation-level prediction objective is important, and that the query-based predictor is more effective than a simple linear prediction head. Additional ablations, including FuseAdapt rank and scalability, are provided in the supplementary material.

4.4 Controlled Comparisons Backbone and recipe controls. On MUSES with frozen MiT-B0, FuseAdapt obtains 57.54 mIoU versus 49.47 for Deep Prompt, 53.56 for Shared LoRA, and 54.80 for Adapter. These internal controls use the same decoder, split, augmentation, optimizer, schedule, PMM, and checkpoint rule, with 2.95/2.81/2.95/2.94M trainable parameters, respectively. External MiT-B0 references MAGIC and Any2Seg obtain 49.02/50.00. With MiT-B2, FuseAdapt obtains 70.60, compared with 58.28/55.36/53.40/59.36 for CMNeXt/MAGIC/StitchFusion/CHARM. External results share the backbone family but are not all recipe-matched; Appendix A gives definitions and protocols. 

Alternative fusion mechanisms. Table 11 further compares fusion designs on MCubeS using DINOv2-B and the same segmentation decoder. Frozen feature averaging and concatenation obtain 51.16 and 52.42 mIoU, while token-attention fusion obtains 52.79. Late-logit fusion with private low-rank adaptation reaches 54.55, compared with 57.62 for FuseAdapt. These controls distinguish adaptation-space exchange from simply combining frozen representations or independently adapted predictions. Separate adaptation without routing reaches 56.09, narrowing the gap but remaining below the full model. 

Where should information cross modalities? Dense-feature routing keeps Q/V private but mixes complete FFN features; All-path routed LoRA routes Q/V and FFN residuals. Both retain the backbone and decoder of the corresponding setting. FuseAdapt outperforms these alternatives on MCubeS and MUSES (Table 7), supporting the separation of private attention adaptation and residual-only exchange. These are internal mechanism controls, not reproductions of complete prior systems.

PMM improves output KD in all 15 nonempty subsets and fused-feature KD in 14/15. Removing EMA from the query predictor gives 50.59; the original per-token regressor gives 49.71. These controls support the target-conditioned prediction objective beyond generic full-to-partial matching. 4.5 Robustness and Deployment Cost Scope of robustness. On DELIVER’s ten predefined weather/degradation conditions, FuseAdapt obtains 67.36 mean mIoU versus 67.19 for Any2Seg, leading in eight conditions but trailing at night and under-exposure. In a separate MCubeS latent-prediction test, shifting the visible sensors together by 16 pixels relative to the masked target reduces cosine similarity by 0.00051 for the query predictor versus 0.01636 for per-token regression. The linear model has higher clean cosine; the evidence concerns lower shift sensitivity, not universally better reconstruction or arbitrary sensor misalignment. Appendix C provides both protocols and full tables. 

Full-context target reliability. A full-input EMA target is useful on average but is not a per-sample oracle. Across 1,428 MCubeS image/subset pairs, comprising 102 images and 14 proper subsets, full-input predictions improve per-image mIoU by 4.44 on average and outperform partial-input predictions in 81.72% of pairs. PMM nevertheless uses detached latent representations, rather than segmentation pseudo-labels, as supervision. This analysis characterizes the benefit and limitation of the full-context assumption: additional sensors usually provide useful context under the evaluated distribution, but can also introduce misleading information. See Appendix B.1 for details. 

End-to-end efficiency. On an idle H100, at 1024 2, batch 1, FP32, with 20 warm-ups and 100 timed runs, three-modality MiT-B0 FuseAdapt takes 21.05 ms versus 19.63 for CMNeXt and 26.69 for StitchFusion. It retains 3.91M unique deployed parameters and removes both PMM components. Parameter efficiency does not imply lowest latency or memory: Any2Seg and MAGIC are faster in this profile. Appendix D reports all measured costs and identifies reconstructed inference graphs. 

5 Discussion and Conclusion 

FuseAdapt separates modality-private attention adaptation from cross-modal exchange through FFN low-rank residuals. PMM complements this design with target-conditioned missing-modality latent prediction. Same-backbone controls support both choices, with improvements across the evaluated MiT scales. 

Limitations and deployment scope. Absolute accuracy depends on the frozen representation and its biases; cross-backbone comparisons alone cannot isolate our contribution. Inference cost still grows with the number of sensor streams. Routing and averaging require a common projected grid, and the shift experiment measures latent prediction sensitivity, not robustness to arbitrary calibration, field-of-view, or temporal errors. The main segmentation comparisons are reported from single runs, without a statistical-significance claim. DELIVER covers predefined adverse conditions, but neither those results nor a full-context EMA target guarantees safe behavior under correlated or out-of-distribution sensor failures, including device-level ones [Yuan et al., 2025b,c]. E Methodological Justification 

E.1 Rationale for Using Modality-Specific LoRA in Self-Attention Adaptation. 

To examine whether self-attention should apply a fused LoRA module across modalities or maintain modality-specific adaptation paths, we analyze the learned LoRA updates ∆W across both the q and v projections for each modality on two datasets, DELIVER and MCubeS. For each projection, we also evaluate shallow, mid, and deep transformer layers and report cosine-similarity matrices. As shown in Fig. 5 and Fig. 6, across all layers and for both projections, the LoRA updates of different modalities exhibit consistently low pairwise cosine similarity, forming near-orthogonal subspaces rather than converging toward a shared direction. This indicates that each modality learns distinct and highly specialized correction directions within the attention mechanism. Low pairwise similarity supports distinct sensor-specific correction directions, but does not by itself establish that routing them is harmful. The all-path routing comparison in Table 7 tests that choice directly. Keeping Q/V private retains each sensor’s attention rule while the FFN residual path permits cross-modal communication. 

E.2 Comparison with Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) 

Each FuseAdapt low-rank branch has a fixed source-sensor identity, while receiver-conditioned routing weights vary by token and input. All available sensor branches participate without top-k

selection or a load-balancing loss; absent sensors are masked. This distinguishes branch assignment from dynamic communication and does not imply fixed mixing weights. A sparse MoE-style LoRA control obtains 54.94 versus 57.62 mIoU on MCubeS. The all-path and dense-feature controls in Table 7 more directly test which activations should cross sensor streams.

F Implementation Details 

We train the proposed method on 8 A5000 GPUs for DELIVER, 4 for MUSES, and 4 for MCubeS. Initial learning rates are 3.5e −4 (MCubeS), 4e −4 (MUSES) and 4e −4 (DELIVER), with a 10-epoch warm-up at 0.1×. Training is conducted for 500, 200, and 200 epochs on MCubeS, DELIVER, and MUSES respectively. Each run begins with a warm-up stage (200 epochs for MCubeS and 15 epochs for both DELIVER and MUSES), during which the model is trained using all modalities without any masking. We use AdamW with batch sizes of 1 (DELIVER) and 4 (MUSES, MCubeS) per GPU. Input resolutions are 1024×1024 (DELIVER, MUSES) and 512×512 (MCubeS), with standard aug-mentations: random resizing (0.5–2.0), flipping, color jitter, blur, and cropping. FuseAdapt uses rank 8 for DELIVER and MCubeS, and 4 for MUSES. DINOv2-B (for DELIVER and MCubeS) [Oquab et al., 2023] and DINOv2-S (MUSES) serve as the frozen VFMs. Standard LoRA with the low-rank matrices A and B such that A is initialized with a small random Gaussian (e.g., N (0 , 0.01) ) and B is initialized as zero, ensuring the initial weight perturbation is zero. The number of learnable tokens in the predictor is the same as the sequence length of the target feature.

Routing Fusion in FFNs We introduce independent, modality-specific routing modules at the FFN stage to enable controlled and dynamic cross-modal fusion. Unlike attention layers that focus 23 Table 17: Results of inserting FuseAdapt LoRAs every N layers under MCSS setting on MCubeS. All models are trained with four modalities. 

N 0 1 2 3RADN 51.16 57.62 56.38 55.87 on intra-modal context, these routing blocks refine each modality’s representation by selectively integrating complementary signals from others, based on the current context. This fusion occurs after independent encoding, preventing early entanglement and allowing the model to adaptively weight modalities depending on availability, especially beneficial under modality masking. Importantly, each modality has its own dedicated routing layer, which maintains structural disentanglement and minimizes interference from masked or undertrained modalities. The routing module adopts a lightweight noisy gating mechanism, adapted from sparsely activated Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) designs. Given hidden states, the router outputs modality-specific routing scores through a linear layer, and injects learned Gaussian noise via a second linear layer followed by softplus. The input size matches the feature dimension of the VFM output, while the output size corresponds to the number of training modalities. The resulting noisy logits enable soft, differentiable expert selection. Routers are trained end-to-end as part of the main optimization, without any additional loss terms. We evaluate the effectiveness of routing mechanisms. As shown in Table 18, incorporating routing fusion into the FFNs consistently improves performance. When no fusion is applied (“without routing”), the model processes each modality independently and therefore cannot benefit from cross-modal interactions. Using a simple average fusion yields only marginal gains, as it assumes that all modalities contribute equally and ignores their heterogeneous characteristics. In contrast, routing fusion adaptively selects and combines modality-dependent FFN experts, leading to a clear improvement (from 56.09 to 57.62 mIoU). This demonstrates that learned routing effectively captures the distinct roles that different modalities play in multi-modal feature refinement. We also observe that a shared router yields a competitive gain (57.24 mIoU), showing that routing is helpful even without modality-specific specialization. Notably, unlike Mixture of Experts structures that perform token-level dynamic expert selection (e.g., top-K gating), our routing deterministically assigns each LoRA expert to a specific modality. All available modality branches participate without top-k selection or a load-balancing objective. Mixing weights remain token-dependent, and absent sources are masked before normalization.
