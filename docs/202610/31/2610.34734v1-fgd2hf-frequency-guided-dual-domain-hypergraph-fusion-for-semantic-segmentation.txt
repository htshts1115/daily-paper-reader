A B S T R A C T
Frequency-domain information is beneficial for semantic segmentation. However, existing frequency-
based methods often suffer from insufficient frequency utilization, inefficient fusion, and high com-
putational complexity. To address these issues, we propose FGD2HF, a dual-domain fusion network
that leverages frequency features to guide spatial-domain fusion and enhancement. Specifically, we
introduce a novel Hybrid-Attention Encoder (NAHA) that jointly models local and global relationships
through neighborhood and hypergraph attention, enhancing the network’s ability to extract effective
features. We further introduce a Wavelet Transform and Enhancement (WTE) module to extract and
refine low- and high-frequency cues from preceding-stage features, followed by Frequency-guided
Dual-domain Fusion (FGDF) modules that use frequency cues to condition current-stage spatial
grouping and feature propagation. This design incorporates both global and local frequency guidance
into spatial feature modulation, enhancement and fusion with low computational cost. Extensive
comparisons with numerous state-of-the-art semantic segmentation methods demonstrate that our
method is highly competitive. Our full FGD2HF-B model achieves 48.7% mIoU on ADE20K (+0.3%
over SegMAN-B with 18% fewer GFLOPs), 83.2% mIoU on Cityscapes (comparable to SegMAN-
B with 21% fewer GFLOPs), and 1.1% higher mIoU than VWFormer-T on SBD with 21% fewer
GFLOPs, while also achieving significant improvements in boundary segmentation accuracy. The
code will be made publicly available upon paper acceptance.
1. Introduction
Semantic segmentation, as an important downstream
task in computer vision, has been widely applied in various
fields, including autonomous driving [48, 72, 65], medical
diagnosis [79, 44], satellite image analysis [66, 2], and
robotic operations [89, 8, 40]. In recent years, with the
rapid development of deep learning and convolutional neural
networks, CNN-based segmentation models such as Fully
Convolutional Networks (FCN) [55], MASK R-CNN [37],
U-Net [63], and DeepLab [16] have gradually replaced tra-
ditional semantic segmentation methods and become the
mainstream research paradigm. Moreover, since the break-
through of the visual attention mechanism represented by
Vision Transformer (ViT) [26], Transformer-based semantic
segmentation models [88, 67, 75, 19, 21, 20] have expe-
rienced rapid development. Compared with conventional
CNN-based models, Transformer-based approaches benefit
from global receptive fields and strong spatial relationship
modeling capabilities, thereby significantly improving seg-
mentation accuracy [70].
However, traditional semantic segmentation models that
rely solely on spatial information have inherent limitations.
While spatial features are intuitive and effective, they are not
sufficiently robust and are easily affected by noise, illumina-
tion changes, and distribution shifts [6]. Furthermore, they
often perform poorly in modeling object boundaries and cap-
turing subtle inter-class differences. In contrast, frequency-
domain features offer unique advantages in global represen-
tation and resistance to interference [4]. First, global spectral
transforms such as Fourier and DCT provide holistic fre-
quency representations, whereas wavelet transforms jointly

acteristics. Second, frequency-domain decomposition can
effectively separate low- and high-frequency components.
High-frequency components capture object contours, edges,
and fine details, while low-frequency components encode
global illumination, color distribution, and semantic struc-
tures [46]. These properties are particularly valuable for
semantic segmentation as a dense prediction task.

Therefore, effectively leveraging frequency-domain fea-
tures to improve segmentation accuracy has gradually be-
come an important research direction in recent years. In
contrast to spatial-domain approaches, frequency-domain
features capture different types of image information from a
signal distribution perspective. By incorporating frequency-
domain feature extraction, selection, and fusion, segmenta-
tion models can effectively suppress semantically irrelevant
noise while reinforcing consistent target representations.
This approach significantly enhances both segmentation ac-
curacy and robustness in complex scenes.
We categorize existing frequency-based semantic seg-
mentation methods into three technical routes. The first
route focuses primarily on low-frequency information to
enhance the consistency of target regions and global struc-
tural representations. Representative methods include DCT-
EDANet [54], FDA [81], and FsaNet [87]. The second
route further exploits the distinction between low- and high-
frequency components. By decoupling, selecting, calibrat-
ing, or adaptively fusing low- and high-frequency compo-
nents, these methods enhance boundary and detail preser-
vation while maintaining global context consistency. Rep-
resentative methods include FDNet [46], FADA [6], FADC
[15], and FFR [83]. The third route underscores the im-
portance of dual-domain collaborative modeling, facilitating
seamless interaction between spatial and frequency repre-
sentations. By integrating low- and high-frequency infor-
mation with spatial features, these methods achieve im-
proved segmentation performance in complex scenes and
areas with ambiguous boundaries. Representative methods
include DDFNet [47] and SFFNet [82]. Compared with the
first two routes, dual-domain methods fully exploit the com-
plementary advantages of frequency and spatial features,
demonstrating stronger adaptability in complex scenarios.
However, existing spatial-frequency collaborative meth-
ods still face challenges. First, dual-domain feature extrac-
tion and fusion often require additional transformations,
feature projections, and multi-branch interactions, which
increase computational cost and memory consumption, es-
pecially for high-resolution images. Second, spatial and fre-
quency features differ significantly in terms of representation
form, semantic level, and responsive regions. Simple oper-
ations such as concatenation, addition, or weighted fusion
may not achieve effective alignment. Instead, they risk intro-
ducing redundant information or semantic conflicts. Third,
CNNs and Transformers tend to exhibit a low-pass filtering
effect during deep feature extraction, where downsampling
and global modeling progressively weaken high-frequency
components, resulting in blurred boundaries, broken fine
structures, and incomplete object contours [70]. Therefore,
achieving effective fusion between spatial and frequency
features with low computational cost, while simultaneously
preserving high-frequency boundary information, remains a
key challenge in frequency-based semantic segmentation.
To address these challenges, we propose a dual-domain
feature fusion framework named FGD2HF for semantic seg-
mentation that systematically addresses three key aspects:
(1) computational efficiency, (2) efficient cross-domain fea-
ture fusion, and (3) precise boundary preservation. FGD2HF
employs Haar decomposition to decouple low- and high-
frequency features from the preceding stage and uses the
resulting frequency subbands to guide current-stage spa-
tial feature enhancement and dual-domain fusion. The spa-
tial locality of Haar decomposition and its downsampling
factor establish a structured region-wise correspondence
with current-stage features, alleviating the alignment burden
in network design and addressing the problem of high-
frequency propagation. Specifically, we introduce an En-
coder called NAHA that jointly models local and global re-
lationships through neighborhood and hypergraph attention,
enhancing the network’s ability to extract effective features.
We further introduce a Wavelet Transform and Enhancement
(WTE) module to extract and refine low- and high-frequency
cues from preceding-stage features, followed by Frequency-
guided Dual-domain Fusion (FGDF) modules that use fre-
quency cues to condition current-stage spatial grouping and
feature propagation. Its frequency-guided hypergraph atten-
tion organizes spatial nodes into overlapping groups, with
soft memberships jointly determined by global frequency
context and aligned local frequency cues. Compared with
standard full self-attention over (𝑁) spatial tokens, using
(𝑀) hyperedges, where (𝑀≪𝑁), reduces the number
of relation weights from (𝑁2) to (𝑁𝑀), enabling compact
spatial-frequency interaction.
In summary, our contributions are as follows:
(1) We propose FGD2HF, a Frequency-guided Dual-
domain Hypergraph Fusion network for semantic segmen-
tation. The framework decomposes preceding-stage features
into low- and high-frequency subbands and adaptively uses
the resulting frequency cues to guide current-stage spatial
feature enhancement and fusion, boosting both segmentation
accuracy and boundary precision.
(2) We develop a novel Hybrid-Attention Encoder, termed
NAHA, which integrates neighborhood attention and hyper-
graph attention to jointly capture local and global dependen-
cies, enhancing the network’s feature extraction capability
while reducing computational complexity.
(3) We design a Wavelet Transform and Enhancement
(WTE) module for high- and low-frequency decomposition
and enhancement, and a Frequency-guided Dual-domain
Fusion (FGDF) module that leverages frequency informa-
tion to modulate spatial information, enabling efficient dual-
domain feature fusion at low computational cost.
(4) Extensive experiments on multiple benchmark datasets
demonstrate that our FGD2HF achieves highly competitive
performance across diverse scenarios. Comprehensive abla-
tion studies further verify the effectiveness and superiority
of each proposed component.
2. Related Works
2.1. Semantic Segmentation
Semantic segmentation is a dense prediction task in
computer vision that aims to assign a label to every pixel in
Page 2 of 20


FGD2HF
an image [29]. It is widely applied in autonomous driving,
robotic perception, medical analysis, and remote sensing.
Early semantic segmentation approaches primarily relied on
hand-crafted, low-level visual features such as edges, colors,
textures, and region similarity [25]. Representative methods
include edge detection techniques [3, 71], clustering-based
algorithms [23, 1], support vector machines [59, 74], and
Markov random fields [30, 64]. Although effective in simple
scenarios, these methods often lack the representation ca-
pacity needed for accurate and robust segmentation in com-
plex scenes with intricate structures, illumination changes,
and noise.
With the rapid advancement of deep learning, CNN-
based models [55, 63, 5, 16] have gradually become main-
stream. These methods significantly improve segmentation
performance by learning hierarchical spatial features in an
end-to-end manner. However, CNNs primarily rely on lo-
cal convolution operations [84]. While effective at mod-
eling local textures, their restricted receptive fields limit
their ability to capture global contextual information. The
introduction of attention mechanisms spurred the devel-
opment of Transformer-based segmentation models. Early
methods such as SETR [88] and Segmenter [67] employ
ViT as the backbone for feature extraction, verifying the
feasibility of Transformer architectures in dense prediction
tasks. However, these approaches incur substantial computa-
tional overhead because the self-attention mechanism scales
quadratically with the number of input tokens. Subsequently,
methods such as Swin Transformer [52], PVT [73], and Seg-
Former [75] introduced hierarchical structures and multi-
scale features to address the lack of local inductive bias
and the high computational overhead in ViT. Furthermore,
MaskFormer [21] and Mask2Former [20] transformed the
segmentation task from pixel-by-pixel classification to a
mask classification problem, achieving unified modeling of
semantic segmentation, instance segmentation, and panop-
tic segmentation. However, these methods mainly rely on
spatial features and correlations, without explicitly modeling
frequency characteristics or boundary details.
Given that frequency features are effective at captur-
ing global structures, texture details, and boundary vari-
ations, using frequency information has become an im-
portant trend in semantic segmentation research. Early
studies primarily focused on low-frequency and global
information. For example, DCT-EDANet directly utilizes
DCT compressed-domain representations for segmentation
[54]. FsaNet reduces computational complexity and en-
hances intra-object consistency through low-frequency self-
attention [87]. FDA achieves domain-style alignment using
Fourier low-frequency components [81]. However, relying
solely on low-frequency information limits performance in
complex backgrounds, sharp boundaries, and subtle inter-
class texture variations. This has motivated the joint opti-
mization of low- and high-frequency features. For instance,
FADA decomposes basic visual features into low- and high-
frequency components using Haar wavelets to improve do-
main generalization [6]. FADC adaptively adjusts the recep-
tive field and frequency response of atrous convolution from
a spectral perspective [15]. FFR corrects the boundary over-
smoothing problem caused by high-frequency attenuation
in ViT-based weakly supervised segmentation [83]. These
methods improve boundary preservation and detail recovery,
but largely model frequency features in isolation, leaving
their complementarity with spatial semantics underexplored.
More recently, advanced methods have begun to explore
deep cross-domain interactions between frequency compo-
nents and spatial features. DDFNet [47] selectively inte-
grates low- and high-frequency components from both do-
mains through a dynamic decoupling and fusion strategy, en-
abling the simultaneous capture of local textures and global
semantics. SFFNet [82] decomposes features using Haar
wavelets and then aligns and fuses low-frequency features
with spatial global semantics and high-frequency features
with spatial local details, thereby achieving effective com-
plementarity between the frequency and spatial domains.
2.2. Frequency Domain Feature Learning
Frequency-domain analysis, as a powerful signal pro-
cessing tool, provides a new perspective for understand-
ing model optimization and generalization. In recent years,
frequency-domain learning has been widely applied to ad-
dress issues such as spectral bias, detail degradation, and
artifacts in neural networks, achieving remarkable progress
in image classification, object detection, and semantic seg-
mentation [68, 38, 50, 4]. Kai Xu et al. introduced a DCT-
based frequency-domain learning framework that reduces
input redundancy while maintaining or improving recog-
nition accuracy [76]. Yijie Zhong et al. incorporated fre-
quency cues beyond RGB information to enhance camou-
flaged object detection [90]. FreqNet forces detectors to
learn frequency-domain representations rather than relying
on forgery-specific artifacts, improving generalization to
unseen deepfake sources [69].
In semantic segmentation, FsaNet [87] introduces a
frequency-domain attention mechanism that performs atten-
tion on low-frequency components, reducing computational
complexity while maintaining segmentation performance.
FADC [15] mitigates high-frequency detail loss and grid-
ding artifacts in dilated convolution by adapting dilation
rates to local high-frequency energy. FFR [83] addresses
the low-pass bias of Vision Transformers with a dual-branch
head that separately processes original and high-frequency
features for sharper boundaries. FDNet [46] decouples low-
and high-frequency components using learnable Gaussian
kernels, recalibrates frequency channels, and applies fre-
quency attention for fusion. DDFNet [47] further performs
dynamic cross-domain decoupling and fusion to jointly
model local textures and global semantics.
2.3. Hypergraph Neural Network
Hypergraphs are an extension of conventional graphs
in graph theory and provide a more flexible framework

FGD2HF
for modeling multi-way relationships [7]. Unlike ordinary
graphs, where each edge connects only two vertices, a hyper-
graph introduces the concept of hyperedges, each of which
can connect an arbitrary number of vertices. In contrast to
self-attention, which models all pairwise token interactions,
hypergraph modeling captures global high-order dependen-
cies through a small set of adaptive hyperedges, achieving
near-linear complexity. This enables hypergraphs to capture
global many-to-many relationships, making them well suited
to complex relational tasks such as clustering, object detec-
tion, and recommendation systems [49, 27, 33, 42]. Eun-
Sol Kim et al. proposed Hypergraph Attention Networks
(HANs), a multimodal learning method designed to address
the inconsistency of information hierarchies across different
modalities, such as images and text [43]. HHGSA [42]
leverages heterogeneous hypergraphs and attention mech-
anisms to model high-order social relationships and item
similarities, significantly improving recommendation accu-
racy. YOLOv13 [45] introduces hypergraph mechanism to
object detection, effectively boosting detection performance.
M2I2HA [80] extends hypergraph attention to multimodal
detection by constructing cross-modal hypergraphs and em-
ploying low-rank decomposition to reduce complexity, sub-
stantially improving detection accuracy.
In semantic segmentation, Shurong Chai et al. proposed
an adaptive hypergraph neural network (AHGNN) for medi-
cal image segmentation, aiming to overcome the limitations
of Transformers and graph convolutional networks in model-
ing non-pairwise relationships [10]. Weipeng Jing et al. pro-
posed Hypergraph BiFormer (HGBT), a novel architecture
for semantic segmentation of high-resolution remote sensing
images. By introducing hypergraph structures, HGBT en-
hances the ability to model local features and segment multi-
scale objects [41]. Manothini Palanisamy et al. integrated
hypergraph neural networks with multi-scale relational en-
coding for prostate cancer diagnosis. This framework com-
bines spatial and spectral attention to improve boundary
segmentation accuracy, while incorporating adaptive feature
selection to enhance model generalization [60]. While hy-
pergraph theory has already found applications in semantic
segmentation, cross-domain relational modeling between
the spatial and frequency domains based on hypergraphs
remains largely unexplored. This underexplored direction
holds great promise and calls for further investigation.

Based on these observations, we formulate the following
design motivations:
(1) High-frequency components encode critical bound-
ary cues. However, neural networks generally exhibit a spec-
tral bias toward low-frequency information, limiting their
ability to capture fine-grained details [62, 77, 57]. Moreover,
attention layers exacerbate this issue [46]. Therefore, we
can propagate high-frequency features from shallow
stages to deeper stages for frequency-aware enhance-
ment and fusion, improving boundary preservation and
feature representation.
(2) Incorporating frequency components inevitably in-
troduces additional computational overhead, especially in
attention-based networks. Haar wavelet decomposition of
preceding-stage spatial features produces spatially localized
frequency subbands at the resolution of the current stage.
These subbands support region-wise correspondence with
current-stage spatial features. Thus, decomposing features
from the preceding-stage to enhance the current-stage
not only supports the first motivation but also natu-
rally aligns the resolutions of adjacent stages, facilitating
spatial-frequency fusion and easing the alignment bur-
den that may arise when fusing independently processed
dual-domain branches.
(3) High-frequency components may contain both boundary-
relevant details and task-irrelevant noise. We therefore
introduce an auxiliary boundary prediction task on the
high-frequency-guided spatial features. This supervision
encourages these representations to encode discrimina-
tive boundary cues, complementing the main semantic
segmentation objective.
3.3. Overall Architecture
Existing spatial-frequency fusion methods often process
spatial and frequency features in parallel before fusion,
which can introduce additional computational overhead and
complicate cross-domain feature alignment. In contrast, our
framework applies Haar wavelet decomposition directly
to the encoded features of the preceding stage. Wavelet
decomposition’s spatial locality, combined with its down-
sampling factor matching the inter-stage resolution change,
enables region-wise correspondence between its subbands
and current-stage spatial features [34]. This design reuses the
existing feature hierarchy for frequency extraction, reducing
the overhead associated with independent frequency encod-
ing. The resulting frequency cues guide the grouping and
enhancement of current-stage spatial features through hy-
pergraph theory, achieving adaptive spatial-frequency fusion
and fine-grained representation. Moreover, the enhanced
features from the high-frequency-guided branch support
multi-scale auxiliary boundary prediction, which encour-
ages adaptive use of boundary-relevant high-frequency cues
and mitigates boundary-detail loss caused by high-frequency
attenuation in deep networks.

3.5. Wavelet Transform and Enhancement
In complex scenes, intricate textures and rapid local ap-
pearance changes make precise boundary delineation chal-
lenging. Repeated spatial aggregation and downsampling
can attenuate fine structural details, leading to overly smooth
predictions and inaccurate boundaries. Frequency cues pro-
vide complementary information: low-frequency compo-
nents capture coarse structures, whereas high-frequency
components represent sharp transitions and fine details,
including boundaries and textures. However, high-frequency
responses may also contain noise, highlighting the need
for selective enhancement. Moreover, relying solely on
frequency representations can complicate the modeling of
spatially localized features, whereas processing spatial and
frequency features in separate branches may introduce ad-
ditional computational overhead and require explicit cross-
domain alignment.

3.6. Frequency-guided Dual-domain Fusion
Simple addition or concatenation provides limited ex-
plicit modeling of interactions between spatial- and frequency-
domain features, whereas full cross-attention between dense
feature maps can introduce substantial computational over-
head. To enable effective dual-domain interaction at low
computational cost, we propose frequency-guided hyper-
graph fusion as shown in Fig. 7. Compared with spatial-only
hypergraph construction, our mechanism conditions node-
hyperedge associations on both global frequency context
and aligned local frequency cues. Global context adap-
tively modulates spatial representations, while local cues
provide position-dependent guidance for node grouping.
The resulting associations support spatial feature aggre-
gation, nonlinear processing, and redistribution through a
compact set of hyperedges, enabling frequency-conditioned
interaction at the group level. We further employ low-rank
projections to improve parameter efficiency. Each FGDF
module contains parallel low- and high-frequency guided
hypergraphs. Taking one branch as an example, the detailed
formulation is provided below.

3.7. HyperDecoder and Prediction Heads
To integrate the enhanced multi-level features, we design
a HyperDecoder equipped with a hypergraph attention (HA)
block. As shown in Fig. 8(a), the features {𝑯2, 𝑯3, 𝑯4}
are first projected to a common channel dimension, yielding
{̂𝑯2,̂ 𝑯3,̂ 𝑯4}. The HA block models high-order relation-

As illustrated in Fig. 8(b), 𝑫2 and 𝑫4 are downsampled
and upsampled, respectively, to match the spatial resolution
of 𝑫3. The aligned features are concatenated along the chan-
nel dimension and fused by a 1×1 convolution. A subsequent

4. Experiments and Results
In this section, we conduct experiments to demonstrate
the effectiveness of the proposed FGD2HF. First, we briefly
introduce the dataset, experimental setup, and evaluation
metrics. Next, we present detailed comparisons between our
method and several benchmark methods on relevant datasets.
Finally, we perform ablation studies to verify the necessity
of each proposed module.
4.1. Settings
Datasets. To comprehensively evaluate the performance
of the proposed FGD2HF, experiments were conducted on
three widely recognized public benchmark datasets: Pascal
VOC (SBD) [35], Cityscapes [24] and ADE20K [91].
Baselines. We selected representative strong baselines
for comparison, including methods without frequency infor-
mation, such as DeepLabV3 [17], DeepLabV3+ [18], Seg-
Former [75], SegNeXt [32], VWFormer [78], EDAFormer
[86] and SegMAN [29], as well as methods that employ
frequency information, such as FsaNet [87], FADC [15],
SegAliasing [13], FreqFusion [11], SFM [12], FDConv [14],
and SFFNet [82].
Implementation details. All experiments were con-
ducted on a Linux server using PyTorch, equipped with an
AMD Ryzen 9 9950X CPU and two NVIDIA RTX Pro 6000
GPUs (96GB). For the unified inference-speed benchmark
reported in Table 5, all models were evaluated on a single
NVIDIA A100 GPU. To ensure fair comparison, we keep
the common parameters consistent across all experiments as
much as possible. We applied standard data augmentation
techniques, such as random flip, random crop and random
scale. However, strong augmentations (e.g., Mosaic, Copy-
Paste, CutMix) were prohibited. Inference was performed
in FP32 mode without augmentation. Inference efficiency
is evaluated using frames per second (FPS), calculated
as the number of processed images divided by the total
model forward-pass time. Measurements use synthetic input
tensors stored on the GPU and exclude data loading, input
preparation, and post-processing. The default training and
inference configurations are summarized in Table 2.
Metrics. We adopt mean Intersection over Union (mIoU)
as the primary metric for evaluating overall segmentation

This section presents a comprehensive comparison be-

the training process, we kept the hyperparameter settings
largely consistent. Specifically, the training resolution was
set to 512 × 512 for the SBD and ADE20K datasets, and
1024×1024 for Cityscapes. For validation and inference, we
adopted 512 × 512 for SBD and ADE20K, and 2048 × 1024
for Cityscapes. The model’s GFLOPs are reported based on
the inference resolutions. Furthermore, as different code-
bases employ distinct data loading and training paradigms,

we standardized the training duration according to their
respective implementations: for iteration-based codebases,

Segmentation Result. Table 4 summarizes the per-
formance of FGD2HF against several leading methods,
including both traditional spatial-domain approaches and
recent frequency-aware models. Among lightweight mod-

FGD2HF-S improves over FreqFusion-SegFormer-B1 by
approximately 1.3%, while using 9.5% fewer GFLOPs than
frequency-based methods. Among non-frequency-based meth-

segmentation accuracy and computational cost. FGD2HF-B
achieves the second-best performance on SBD with an mIoU
of 84.0%, only 0.2 percentage points lower than SegMAN-

Boundary Prediction Result. Table 4 further demon-

models, FGD2HF-T achieves the highest mBIoU and mean

and mBF1 over SegMAN-S by 0.7 and 0.5 percentage
points on SBD, 0.4 and 0.8 points on Cityscapes, and 1.2
and 0.9 points on ADE20K, respectively, while requiring
fewer GFLOPs. FGD2HF-B leads its comparison group on
both boundary metrics across all three datasets, achieving
78.3%/77.8% on SBD, 79.7%/90.0% on Cityscapes, and
39.2%/49.4% on ADE20K. On SBD, it surpasses SegMAN-

mIoU, highlighting the complementary information pro-

The results also show that incorporating frequency infor-
mation does not necessarily yield better boundary segmen-
tation than spatial-only approaches. For example, SFFNet
performs below SegMAN-S on both boundary metrics on

of the complete architectures rather than the isolated contri-
bution of frequency processing. Boundary quality depends
on both the encoder representations and how frequency cues
are incorporated into subsequent feature processing. This
consideration motivates our design of the NAHA encoder,
which combines neighborhood and hypergraph attention to
capture local structural details and global semantic context,
providing informative representations for frequency extrac-
tion and dual-domain fusion.
4.4. Analysis of Inference Speed and GPU
Memory Usage
To evaluate model inference speed and GPU memory
usage, we benchmark FGD2HF on a single NVIDIA A100
GPU using randomly generated RGB input tensors of size
512 × 512, with a batch size of 1 and FP32 precision. The
model is set to evaluation mode, with gradient computation
disabled. After 10 warm-up iterations, we record the total
execution time over 1,000 forward passes and calculate
FPS as the number of processed images divided by the
elapsed time. Only model forward execution is timed, with
input tensors already stored on the GPU. Data loading,
input preparation, and post-processing are excluded to avoid
discrepancies arising from different data pipelines and post-
processing implementations across methods. GPU Memory
denotes the peak GPU memory usage during inference.
As reported in Table 5, FGD2HF-T, -S, -B, and -L
achieve inference speeds of 96.0, 71.7, 46.4, and 36.3 FPS,
respectively, while maintaining peak GPU memory usage
of 188.6, 299.7, 386.8, and 637.4 MB. Together with their
segmentation accuracy on SBD, these results demonstrate
a favorable trade-off between accuracy and inference effi-
ciency.
4.5. Analysis of Frequency-guided Dual-domain
Fusion
To qualitatively assess the effectiveness of frequency-
guided dual-domain fusion in enhancing spatial represen-
tations, we visualize the enhanced feature maps at stages
P3–P5 and compare them with the corresponding NAHA
encoder outputs. As shown in Fig. 9, the enhanced fea-
tures exhibit more distinct object details, clearer boundary
responses, and greater foreground–background activation
contrast. These improvements are particularly evident at P3
and P4, whereas the effect is less pronounced at P5, where
foreground and background activations remain similar in the
displayed maps. This trend is consistent with the finer spatial
detail retained at earlier feature levels. These visualizations
provide qualitative support for the ability of frequency guid-
ance to strengthen spatial detail and boundary-related rep-
resentations, complementing the quantitative segmentation
and boundary evaluation results.

5. Conclusion and Future Work
This article presents FGD2HF, a novel dual-domain
fusion network that enhances semantic segmentation by
leveraging low- and high-frequency components to fuse and
enhance spatial-domain features via frequency-guided hy-
pergraph attention. Our approach includes a NAHA encoder
that extracts multi-level features using neighborhood and
hypergraph attention, boosting the feature extraction capa-
bility of FGD2HF. Through the integration of the WTE mod-
ule and FGDF module, FGD2HF effectively captures both
global semantics and local texture details by fusing low- and
high-frequency information. Wavelet transform is employed
to extract frequency information from the preceding stage,
which is then used to enhance spatial information fusion in
the subsequent stage. This mechanism effectively alleviates
the over-smoothing phenomenon commonly caused by the
loss of high-frequency information in deeper network layers,
while maintaining low computational complexity.
Extensive experiments on multiple public datasets demon-
strate that FGD2HF achieves highly competitive or supe-
rior performance across multiple benchmark datasets. The
frequency-guided dual-domain hypergraph fusion strategy
effectively improves semantic segmentation accuracy, espe-
cially in complex scenes. Furthermore, FGD2HF achieves
superior accuracy with lower computational resource re-
quirements, demonstrating an excellent trade-off between
segmentation accuracy and computational efficiency.
Future work will focus on further optimizing FGD2HF,
including reducing network parameters and model size with-
out sacrificing performance, as well as improving its infer-
ence speed. We will also explore the deep integration of
other advanced model architectures with FGD2HF to further
enhance its segmentation capabilities.
