Abstract—Monocular depth estimation has advanced rapidly
with the development of foundation models that demonstrate
strong zero-shot generalisation across diverse visual domains.
However, the applicability of these models to visual perception
for lower-limb prosthetics remains largely unexplored. Reliable
depth perception is particularly important for prosthesis users,
who often experience significant challenges when navigating
stairs, uneven terrain, and other complex environments. To
address this gap, we introduce the Lower-Limb Prosthesis (LP)
dataset, a benchmark designed to evaluate depth perception from
an emulated knee-level prosthetic viewpoint. Using this dataset,
we evaluate four state-of-the-art monocular depth estimation
foundation models. Our results provide the first systematic
benchmark of monocular depth estimation performance from
a prosthetic viewpoint, while highlighting both the challenges
and the potential of deploying foundation models in prosthetic
perception systems. The code and dataset are available at
https://github.com/Ishivijay/lp dataset.
Index Terms—Monocular depth estimation, foundation models,
zero-shot, medical prosthesis
I. INTRODUCTION
Visual perception is fundamental to autonomous navigation
and environmental understanding. In the context of medical
prosthetics, the ability to accurately perceive and interpret
the surrounding environment is critical for safe and effective
device operation. However, current lower-limb prostheses lack
robust perceptual capabilities, leaving users at significantly
increased risk of falls, particularly when navigating stairs,
uneven terrain, and cluttered environments [1]. Integrating
reliable depth perception into prosthetic systems could enable
real-time hazard detection, adaptive gait planning, and en-
hanced environmental awareness. By improving the ability to
navigate complex environments safely, such capabilities could
reduce fall risk, enhance mobility, and increase user confidence
in daily activities.
Computer vision approaches are increasingly being inte-
grated into lower-limb wearable robotics to provide active
environmental awareness. In lower-limb prosthetics, vision-
in-the-loop control using gaze-tracking cues can be used to
identify user intention and anticipate terrain transitions, pro-
viding volitional control of active knee-ankle prostheses [2].

depth features [5]. While these approaches demonstrate the
value of vision, they generally rely on task-specific training,
custom multicamera setups, or cooperative rehabilitation en-
vironments.
Monocular depth estimation has become an important ca-
pability in the field of computer vision [6]. Recent founda-
tion models have achieved impressive zero-shot generalisation
across a variety of scenes. Models such as Marigold [7], Depth
Anything V2 [8], Depth Pro [9], and VGGT [10] employ
a range of training strategies to predict dense depth maps
from single RGB images. However, these models are typically
developed and benchmarked on conventional datasets featuring
a standard eye-level perspective. These advances raise a natural
question: Can state-of-the-art monocular depth estimation
foundation models generalize zero-shot to the unique, highly
dynamic, and knee-level perspective of a prosthesis-mounted

camera?
While prior datasets have captured egocentric or wearable
camera images of locomotion environments, they focus mainly
on RGB-based scene classification or activity recognition and
do not provide paired ground-truth depth annotations. Existing
depth estimation benchmarks, on the other hand, target indoor
scenes NYUv2 [11], ScanNet [12], or autonomous driving
KITTI [13], none of which reflect the specific viewing angle,
motion patterns, and close-range stair navigation experienced
by a prosthesis user. To our knowledge, no existing dataset
provides paired RGB-depth data recorded from a lower-limb-
mounted camera during locomotion, capturing the perspective
of a lower-limb prosthesis.
To address this gap, we introduce the Lower-limb Prosthesis
(LP) dataset, the first dataset designed specifically for visual
perception in medical prosthesis applications. The LP dataset
consists of synchronized RGB-depth image pairs captured
from a camera mounted on a lower-limb during stair ascent
and descent across six recording sessions. The dataset covers
a variety of scene elements, including staircases, corridors,
landings, and doorways, all captured from an emulated knee-
level prosthetic viewpoint during locomotion.
Using this dataset, we carry out a comprehensive zero-shot
evaluation of four state-of-the-art monocular depth estimation
foundation models. This study provides the first empirical
evidence on the robustness and limitations of modern depth
estimation models when applied to data from a lower-limb-
mounted camera emulating a prosthetic viewpoint, enabling
novel research for visual perception in medical prostheses.
The main contributions of this paper are:
1) We introduce the LP dataset, the first dataset for visual
depth perception for lower-limb prosthesis, comprising
six stair-navigation sequences with ground-truth depth
from a depth camera.
2) We present a systematic zero-shot evaluation of four
state-of-the-art monocular depth estimation foundation
models (Marigold, Depth Anything V2, Depth Pro, and
VGGT) on this novel domain.
3) We provide a detailed analysis of model performance
across different scene types and discuss the implications
for integrating depth estimation into prosthetic percep-
tion systems.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Monocular Depth Estimation
Monocular depth estimation aims to infer scene depth
from a single RGB image and has been extensively studied
in computer vision. Early supervised approaches relied on
paired RGB–depth datasets, such as NYU Depth V2 [11]
and KITTI [13], and employed convolutional neural networks
for depth prediction [14]. To reduce dependence on costly
ground-truth depth annotations, self-supervised methods were
subsequently introduced, leveraging photometric consistency
across video frames or stereo image pairs as a supervisory
signal [15]. More recently, research has shifted toward foun-
dation models designed to generalize across diverse domains
and visual environments. Depth Anything [16] and its suc-
cessor, Depth Anything V2 [8], are trained at scale using a
combination of labelled and unlabelled data. Marigold [7]
repurposes the Stable Diffusion architecture for monocular
depth estimation through a diffusion-based denoising frame-
work. Depth Pro [9] combines a DINOv2 backbone with a
dedicated focal-length estimation module, while VGGT [10]
leverages vision transformers to achieve strong depth estima-
tion accuracy and cross-domain generalisation. Despite their
impressive performance on standard benchmark datasets, the
robustness of these models under the unconventional camera
viewpoints encountered in lower-limb prosthetic applications
remains largely unexplored.
B. Egocentric and Wearable Vision Datasets
Several datasets have been developed using wearable or
body-mounted cameras to support mobility and visual per-
ception research. For example, the ExoNet database [17] pro-
vides wearable-camera images of walkable environments for
scene classification, while other datasets have been proposed
for depth estimation using head-mounted or chest-mounted
cameras. However, these datasets are primarily designed for
general egocentric perception and do not capture the unique
visual characteristics of a lower-limb prosthesis-mounted cam-
era, including its low viewpoint, reduced camera height, and
distinctive motion dynamics during locomotion and stair ne-
gotiation. As a result, they provide limited insight into the
performance of depth estimation models in prosthetic naviga-
tion scenarios, highlighting the need for datasets specifically
designed to evaluate perception from a prosthetic perspective.
C. Visual Perception for Assistive Devices
Research on intelligent lower-limb prostheses has tradi-
tionally focused on mechanical design, control strategies,
and electromyography (EMG)-based intent recognition [18].
Recent work started incorporating environmental sensing to
improve prosthetic functionality. Examples include the use of
wearable-camera imagery for locomotion environment clas-
sification [17] and the identification of walkable surfaces
from egocentric images [19]. While these approaches provide
valuable semantic information about the environment, they do
not explicitly capture its geometric structure. Incorporating
depth-aware perception into prosthetic systems could enable
advanced capabilities such as obstacle detection, step-height
estimation, terrain assessment, and real-time path planning.
Despite these potential benefits, the application of monocular
depth estimation to lower-limb prosthetic navigation remains
largely unexplored.

viewpoint of a knee-level prosthesis-mounted camera. Repre-

transmitted via a USB Type-C 3.2 connection to a laptop
carried by an accompanying person. The dataset contains
3,144 synchronized RGB–depth image pairs distributed across
six recording sequences encompassing stair ascent, stair de-
scent, and corridor walking, as summarized in Table I. Data
acquisition was performed using an Orbbec Femto Bolt depth
camera, which operates on Microsoft’s Azure Kinect Time-
of-Flight (ToF) technology. The device features a 1-megapixel
indirect ToF (iToF) depth sensor configured in wide field-of-
view (WFOV) mode, yielding a 120° × 120° field of view and
producing depth maps at a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels.
Co-registered RGB images were captured via the integrated
4K RGB sensor and synchronized with the depth maps at the
hardware level.
B. Dataset Structure and Formats
For each sequence, the dataset provides sharpened RGB
images sharpen_rgb, raw depth maps raw_depth, col-
orized depth visualizations colored_depth, temporally
smoothed depth smooth_depth and transformed depth
transformed_depth.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
We perform zero-shot inference using four state-of-the-
art
foundation
models
for
monocular
depth
estimation:
Marigold [7], Depth Anything V2 [8], Depth Pro [9], and
VGGT [10]. To ensure a consistent comparison, all models
were evaluated using the same three-stage pipeline: (1) zero-
shot inference on raw RGB images, (2) post-processing and
alignment of predicted depth maps with the spatial dimensions
of the ground-truth depth data, and (3) quantitative evaluation
using standard depth estimation metrics.
1) Model Configurations: Marigold was evaluated using
the pre-trained marigold-depth-v1-1 diffusion check-
point [7], with half-precision (FP16) inference enabled. Depth
Anything V2 was deployed with the large Vision Transformer
encoder (ViT-L) configuration [8], and inference was config-
ured to produce raw depth maps. Depth Pro was loaded using
its official model factory, generating metric depth predictions
in meters together with estimated camera focal lengths [9].
VGGT, originally developed for multi-view 3D reconstruction,
was adapted for single-image depth estimation using the
VGGT-1B checkpoint (1 billion parameters) [10] obtained
from Hugging Face. Input images were preprocessed using
the official VGGT preprocessing pipeline in crop mode [10],
and depth maps were extracted from the model outputs for
evaluation.
2) Evaluation Protocol: All models were evaluated on the
complete set of sequences in the LP dataset under identical
experimental conditions. When the spatial resolution of a pre-
dicted depth map differed from that of the ground-truth depth
map, bilinear interpolation was used to resize the prediction
to the ground-truth dimensions.
To enable fair comparison across models with differing scale
and shift characteristics, a least-squares alignment procedure
was applied per image. Specifically, a global scale factor s
and shift term t were estimated by solving
GT = s · pred + t,
(1)
where GT denotes the ground-truth depth map and pred
denotes the predicted depth map [7]. The evaluation metrics
included absolute relative error (Abs Rel), squared relative
error (Sq Rel), root mean square error (RMSE), root mean
square error in logarithmic space (RMSElog), and threshold
accuracies δ1, δ2, and δ3. Both the least-squares alignment in
Eq. 1 and all evaluation metrics were computed exclusively
over valid ground-truth depth pixels (GT > 0), excluding
unmeasured sensor dropout regions.

RMSE (0.3357), and RMSElog (0.2205), together with the

δ3 = 0.9669). Depth Pro and VGGT remained competitive

C. Future Work
Although
the
examined
zero-shot
foundation
models
demonstrate strong generalisation, their large parameter sizes
and high computational demands currently prevent them from
being deployed directly on active prosthetic hardware. To
address this limitation, future work should investigate two
complementary directions. First, model compression, pruning,
and quantization techniques should be explored to enable
these large foundation models to run efficiently on resource-
constrained hardware. Second, deploying lightweight monoc-
ular depth architectures [20], [21] is necessary for real-time
edge processing. Real-time gait adaptation requires visual
feedback to be processed within the swing phase (typically
under 400 ms). Future work should therefore focus on de-
ploying both optimised foundation models and lightweight
adapted networks on wearable edge accelerators. Furthermore,
because prosthetic locomotion is safety-critical, integrating
monocular depth uncertainty estimation [22] and pixel-wise
out-of-distribution detection [23] is essential to identify unre-
liable depth predictions and unexpected terrain hazards. In this
context, the LP dataset provides a relevant benchmark for eval-
uating the impact of such optimizations on depth estimation
performance under prosthesis-specific operating conditions.
VI. CONCLUSION
In this study, we present the Lower-Limb Prosthesis (LP)
dataset, a benchmark comprising synchronised RGB and
ground-truth depth data collected from a knee-level camera
mounted on healthy participants to emulate a lower-limb
prosthesis. The dataset addresses a significant gap in prosthetic
perception research by providing depth-aware visual data
captured during stair navigation and indoor locomotion from
this emulated prosthetic perspective. Using the LP dataset,
we evaluated four state-of-the-art monocular depth estimation
foundation models and found that, despite being trained on
conventional benchmarks, they generalise effectively to this
previously unexplored domain. These baseline results demon-
strate the feasibility of using zero-shot foundation models to
support downstream prosthetic applications such as obstacle
detection, terrain assessment and adaptive gait planning. This
opens up the possibility of advanced visual perception in
intelligent lower-limb prostheses. By introducing a dedicated
benchmark and establishing strong baseline performance, this
work provides a foundation for future research into visual
perception in lower-limb prostheses.
