ABSTRACT
Recovering detailed geometry from high-resolution images is critical for precise
perception of the surroundings and objects. However, existing methods which use
latent-space modeling and VAE reconstruction can compromise geometric details.
Furthermore, decoding from latent codes introduces substantial inference over-
head. To address those issues, we present EAGLEDEPTH, an efficient framework
for high-resolution monocular depth estimation that combines the geometric pri-
ors of latent diffusion with fine-grained pixel-space generation. Our key idea is
to retain depth-aware latent representations as guidance while generating the fi-
nal depth map directly in pixel space. We train the latent and pixel components
sequentially: first, we fine-tune a pretrained latent diffusion model using paired
RGB–depth supervision; then, we adapt a pretrained pixel diffusion decoder, PiD,
to predict depth conditioned on the learned features. Training of the pixel com-
ponent starts at 1024 resolution and continues across multiple resolutions up to
4K. The latent branch processes resized, lower-resolution RGB images, while the
pixel branch generates depth at the target resolution, bypassing the original VAE
decoder. This design preserves learned geometric knowledge without requiring
the latent backbone to operate at the output resolution. On five commonly used
depth estimation datasets and the high-resolution Synth4K dataset, our framework
achieves state-of-the-art depth estimation performance, with faster inference and
better preservation of fine structures and object boundaries.

INTRODUCTION
Monocular depth estimation provides a fundamental capability for robotic navigation (Redder et al.,
2025; Gode et al., 2025), manipulation (Zhang et al., 2026c;b; Yuan et al., 2025), and 3D scene
understanding (Eigen et al., 2014; Ouyang et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2024). Modern cameras capture
multi-megapixel images containing small objects, thin structures, and closely spaced boundaries,

creating a demand for depth predictions that preserve geometric detail at comparable resolutions.
However, representative generative depth and geometry estimators are trained at resolutions such as
512×512 and 1024×768 pixels (Xu et al., 2026a;b). Downsampling images to these working reso-
lutions can weaken or eliminate visual cues associated with small structures. Efficiently recovering
high-resolution, fine-grained geometry is therefore important for applications such as small-obstacle
avoidance and precise robotic manipulation.
Preserving local detail and global scene consistency at high resolution requires careful control of
computational cost. Interpolation cannot recover unresolved structures, while increasing the work-
ing resolution raises computation and memory demands. Methods such as PatchFusion (Li et al.,
2024) and PatchRefiner (Li et al., 2026) refine local patches using global image context to recover
detailed, coherent depth maps. For diffusion models, iterative denoising can further increase infer-
ence costs, particularly when processing large spatial grids.
Generative methods (Hu et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2024) such as Marigold (Ke et al., 2024b) demon-
strate that pretrained latent diffusion models can acquire depth-specific knowledge through paired
RGB–depth supervision. These representations help resolve ambiguity by providing learned geo-
metric context when visual evidence is incomplete. However, latent compression can obscure narrow
structures and closely spaced boundaries, while reconstruction with a variational autoencoder (VAE)
can introduce additional geometric errors. Boundary degradation occurs even when reconstructing
encoded ground-truth geometry (Xu et al., 2026a;b). Moreover, the decoder must reconstruct the
full-resolution output, adding computation as image size increases. These limitations motivate re-
taining useful latent priors without the original reconstruction pathway.
Pixel-space diffusion avoids VAE reconstruction by generating geometric outputs directly. Pixel-
Perfect Depth (Xu et al., 2026a) predicts depth using pretrained image encoders such as Depth Any-
thing V2, while PointDiT (Xu et al., 2026b) generates dense 3D point maps conditioned on frozen
DINOv3 image features. However, processing high-resolution spatial representations remains com-
putationally demanding as the number of spatial tokens increases, and iterative sampling can further
increase latency. Direct pixel-space generation therefore still faces the challenge of recovering fine
geometry efficiently at multi-megapixel resolutions.
We introduce EAGLEDEPTH, a framework that combines depth-adapted latent representations with
direct pixel-space prediction. Our key idea is to separate geometric prior extraction from high-
resolution detail recovery to allocate computation across different resolutions. The latent branch sup-
plies depth-aware guidance at low resolution, while the original RGB image provides the local evi-
dence needed to resolve fine structures. We adapt the pretrained Pixel Diffusion Decoder (PiD) (Lu
et al., 2026), originally designed to jointly decode and upsample image latents. Depth-specific su-
pervision adapts this formulation to geometric prediction, where details must reflect meaningful
surfaces and depth discontinuities rather than visual sharpness alone.
We train the two components sequentially. First, we fine-tune FLUX.2-klein-4B (Black Forest Labs,
2026), a pretrained latent diffusion model, on paired RGB–depth data. This branch processes re-
sized, lower-resolution RGB images to provide depth-aware guidance. We then freeze this branch
and fine-tune PiD, conditioned on its guidance and the original RGB image. Pixel training starts
with approximately 10242 pixels per image and continues across multiple resolutions up to 4K. The
pixel branch directly generates target-resolution depth, bypassing the original VAE decoder during
inference without scaling the latent backbone to the output resolution.
We evaluate this design on six benchmark datasets, including the high-resolution Synth4K dataset,
covering standard depth estimation and high-resolution prediction up to 4K. EAGLEDEPTH achieves
state-of-the-art depth estimation performance, with better preservation of fine structures and object
boundaries, together with faster inference.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows:
• We introduce EAGLEDEPTH, a generative monocular depth estimator that brings together
the geometric knowledge of depth-adapted latent diffusion and the pixel-space modeling
capabilities for efficient, detailed prediction at high resolutions.
• We show that depth-aware features from downsampled RGB inputs can guide depth gen-
eration at higher resolutions. This enables our framework to retain latent geometric priors
without executing the latent backbone at the output resolution or using its VAE decoder.

• Experiments on five commonly used depth estimation datasets and the high-resolution
Synth4K dataset establish state-of-the-art depth estimation results for our 1024-resolution
model and its multi-resolution extension supporting resolutions up to 4K, with consistent
improvements in fine-structure recovery and boundary fidelity, alongside faster inference.

RELATED WORK
2.1
MONOCULAR DEPTH ESTIMATION
Discriminative approaches such as DPT (Ranftl et al., 2021) and Depth Anything V2 (Yang et al.,
2024) directly predict depth using pretrained visual representations, while PatchFusion (Li et al.,
2024) extends depth estimation to high resolutions through patch-based fusion. Generative ap-
proaches leverage priors learned from image synthesis: Marigold (Ke et al., 2024b) and Lotus-G (He
et al., 2025b) adapt pretrained latent diffusion models for depth prediction. To address geometric
artifacts associated with latent reconstruction, Pixel-Perfect Depth (Xu et al., 2026a) directly gener-
ates depth maps in pixel space, while PointDiT (Xu et al., 2026b) applies pixel-space diffusion to
dense 3D point maps. These methods demonstrate the value of pretrained representations and di-
rect geometric generation, although efficiently recovering fine-grained geometry at multi-megapixel
resolutions remains challenging. Our approach combines these strengths by using depth-adapted
features from a lower-resolution latent backbone to guide depth generation at the target resolution.
2.2
DIFFUSION MODELS
Latent diffusion models (Rombach et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2022) reduce generative modeling costs
by operating in compressed representations and reconstructing outputs through pretrained autoen-
coders. Although computationally effective, this formulation makes output fidelity dependent on
latent compression and reconstruction. Recent advances revisit diffusion directly in pixel space.
JiT (Li & He, 2026) demonstrates effective clean-image prediction with plain transformers, while
PixelDiT (Yu et al., 2026b) combines patch-level and pixel-level processing for efficient pixel-space
generation. Complementing these approaches, PiD (Lu et al., 2026) introduces a latent-conditioned
pixel diffusion decoder that jointly performs decoding and spatial upsampling, replacing conven-
tional VAE decoding. We build on this pretrained decoder and adapt it from image synthesis to
monocular depth estimation. Specifically, depth-aware features learned through latent diffusion
adaptation condition pixel-space depth generation, allowing us to reuse pretrained generative knowl-
edge for efficient, high-resolution geometric prediction.

3.2
FRAMEWORK OVERVIEW
We aim to produce detailed, high-resolution depth maps, which requires capturing both scene-wide
depth relationships and fine local geometry. Pretrained latent diffusion models combine rich visual
priors with compact latent-space modeling. However, spatial compression and autoencoder recon-
struction can lose fine details, and high-resolution VAE decoding incurs substantial computational
and memory overhead. To address these limitations, we draw on the fine-detail modeling capabilities
of pixel diffusion. Specifically, we adapt a pixel diffusion decoder pretrained for image synthesis,
PiD (Lu et al., 2026), to decode coarse depth latents predicted by a depth-adapted latent diffusion
model. During decoding, the pixel model uses high-resolution RGB observations to recover depth
details beyond the coarse representation, producing a detailed depth map at the target resolution.

3.3
ADAPTING LATENT DIFFUSION FOR COARSE DEPTH PREDICTION
Accurate coarse depth provides reliable geometric guidance for subsequent detail refinement, moti-
vating us to improve the accuracy of the latent depth predictor. Prior works have established effective
strategies for adapting pretrained generative models to depth estimation (Ke et al., 2024a; He et al.,
2025a;b). Motivated by these findings, we adopt deterministic single-step prediction and replace
noise inputs with encoded RGB images. We use FLUX.2-klein-4B (Black Forest Labs, 2026), a
pretrained rectified-flow transformer, as our backbone to balance model capability and computa-
tional efficiency. To improve accuracy, we revisit the supervision objective for the latent predictor.
Conventional latent flow matching trains models to predict instantaneous velocities in latent space.
However, flow trajectories are not necessarily straight, so even accurate instantaneous velocities may
yield endpoint errors in a single-step update (Frans et al., 2025). Moreover, although latent-space
supervision encourages predictions to match targets, the decoder may still amplify small residual
errors. Thus, latent-space supervision may be suboptimal. Since our single-step formulation does
not require iterative latent-space sampling, we can directly supervise the decoded depth map:

3.4
DECODING FINE-GRAINED DEPTH WITH PIXEL DIFFUSION
Central to our approach is adapting a pretrained pixel diffusion decoder to depth estimation. Com-
pared with a conventional VAE decoder trained for reconstruction, a pixel diffusion decoder provides
a generative prior learned in pixel space that can help recover fine details beyond the coarse latent
representation. Specifically, we build upon PiD (Lu et al., 2026), which formulates latent decoding
as latent-conditioned image generation in pixel space.
While the original model decodes natural-image latents, we fine-tune it to decode the predicted
coarse depth latents into detailed, high-resolution depth maps using the high-resolution RGB input,
as described in Section 3.2. Following prior findings on pixel-space diffusion (Li & He, 2026), we
use direct clean-sample prediction (x-pred). To initialize the x-pred decoder from PiD’s pretrained
velocity model, we negate the weights and bias of its final linear layer: the negated velocity target,
−ut = x0 −ϵ, differs from the clean-sample target x0 only by a noise term.
The unpatchification head in diffusion transformers maps each patch token to a p × p pixel block
without local spatial mixing, leaving discontinuities along patch boundaries. In depth maps, these
artifacts can be especially noticeable at object boundaries, where one patch spans sharply differ-
ent depths. Convolutions can mitigate these inconsistencies through local feature fusion (He et al.,
2025a; Wang et al., 2026a). However, the large patch size in pixel diffusion makes it costly for
convolutional heads to capture patch-wide context after unpatchification. We therefore insert a
continuity-promoting module before the original output projection, interleaving progressive un-
patchification with small-kernel convolutions to mix hidden features at multiple spatial scales.
Specifically, given the normalized hidden features h(0), the module progressively unfolds their
within-patch dimensions into the spatial grid and applies convolutions after each unfolding step:

To further extend the model’s high-resolution capability, we continue training PiD with mixed res-

are resized to the target patch grid before injection into PiD.

Implementation details. We transform the ground-truth depth into log space via log(1 + d) and
normalize each depth map to −1, 1 using its 2nd and 98th percentiles. We adapt FLUX.2-klein-
4B (Labs, 2025) with rank-256 LoRA for 30,000 steps at a learning rate of 1×10−4. With the latent
model frozen, we fully fine-tune PiD (Lu et al., 2026) at approximately 10242 pixels for 50,000
steps with a learning rate of 3 × 10−5, obtaining Ours (1024). Mixed-resolution training continues
for another 20,000 steps with the learning rate reduced to 10−5, producing Ours (MR). All models
are trained on 8 NVIDIA GPUs, using the AdamW optimizer and a total batch size of 16.
Evaluation protocol. We evaluate all comparison methods under the same resolution settings for
fair comparisons. Specifically, images from the five real-world benchmarks are resized to approxi-
mately 10242 pixels with aspect ratios preserved, whereas Synth4K is evaluated at native 4K reso-
lution. We report AbsRel and δ1 to assess overall depth accuracy. For datasets with dense ground-
truth depth, we further report HF-masked AbsRel and δ1 (Yu et al., 2026a) and boundary F1-score
(r = 1) (Bochkovskiy et al., 2025) to assess fine-detail recovery in the predicted depth maps.
4.2
COMPARISON WITH STATE-OF-THE-ART METHODS
We compare EAGLEDEPTH with several leading methods for depth estimation, including: Mi-
DaS (Ranftl et al., 2020), DA2 (Yang et al., 2024), Marigold (Ke et al., 2024b), Lotus-2 (He et al.,
2025a), DA3 (Lin et al., 2025), MoGe-2 (Wang et al., 2026b), PPD (Xu et al., 2026a), PointDiT (Xu
et al., 2026b), InfiniDepth (Yu et al., 2026a), and URGT (Cui et al., 2026), where the last two
methods are designed for high-resolution depth prediction.
Qualitative results. We present visual comparisons across diverse scenes and image resolutions
in Figure 3. EAGLEDEPTH consistently captures the overall scene structure while preserving local
geometric details. Compared with other methods, our model more faithfully recovers fine structures
and delineates object boundaries that are otherwise blurred or lost. These visual improvements agree
with the gains in HF-masked accuracy and boundary F1, supporting the effectiveness of combining
coarse depth guidance with RGB-guided pixel decoding across scenes and resolutions.
Quantitative results. Table 1 reports depth accuracy on five real-world benchmarks. EAGLEDEPTH
achieves competitive performance across these datasets, with both variants outperforming the com-
parison methods in AbsRel and δ1 on ETH3D and DIODE. Ours (MR) maintains accuracy compa-
rable to Ours (1024), indicating that mixed-resolution continuation largely preserves performance.
On Synth4K, evaluated at native 4K resolution, Ours (MR) achieves strong overall accuracy and
leads all comparison methods in both HF-masked metrics and boundary F1 across all five subsets
(Tables 2 and 3). Meanwhile, the Ours (1024) model, which is trained at a lower resolution like
most comparison methods, remains competitive in overall accuracy and fine-detail recovery at 4K.
Efficiency analysis. Table 6 compares inference speed at two resolutions. Our method is the fastest
among the evaluated methods at 4K. At 1024 × 1024, it is faster than all evaluated generative base-
lines while remaining competitive with discriminative methods.

Patch size in pixel diffusion. Larger patches reduce the number of tokens and accelerate inference,
but compromise depth accuracy. We study this trade-off with patch sizes of 16, 32, and 64. As shown
in Table 5, increasing the patch size degrades prediction quality, with boundary and HF metrics
showing a greater loss of detail than the overall depth metrics suggest. Notably, P32 retains similar
overall accuracy to P16 while providing a 3.1× speedup at 3840×2112 resolution, with degradation
concentrated in fine-detail quality. While we adopt P16 to prioritize fine-grained geometry, P32
offers an efficient alternative for applications with less requirements for depth details.

CONCLUSION
We presented EAGLEDEPTH, a framework that combines depth-adapted latent diffusion with pixel
diffusion for efficient fine-grained depth estimation. The latent branch predicts coarse depth latents
from downsampled RGB inputs, while the pixel branch generates depth directly at the target resolu-
tion conditioned on these latents. This design retains learned geometric priors while reducing detail
loss from latent-space modeling and VAE reconstruction. It also avoids the substantial overhead of
VAE decoding at high resolutions. We train two variants: one at approximately 10242 pixels and
a mixed-resolution extension supporting prediction up to 4K (2160 × 3840). Experiments on five
commonly used depth estimation datasets and Synth4K demonstrate strong overall accuracy and
better preservation of fine structures and object boundaries.

ARCHITECTURE DETAILS
A.1
LATENT BACKBONE: FLUX.2
We use FLUX.2-klein-4B (Labs, 2025) as the latent backbone. This pretrained rectified-flow trans-
former contains approximately four billion parameters, with five double-stream blocks followed by
twenty single-stream blocks. All blocks use a hidden dimension of 3072 and 24 attention heads.
The double-stream blocks maintain separate image and text projections and feed-forward networks,
while jointly attending to tokens from both modalities. The single-stream blocks process the con-
catenated sequence of image and text tokens with parallel attention and feed-forward branches. The
backbone uses query and key normalization, rotary positional embeddings, and timestep-conditioned
feature modulation. Its Qwen3-4B text encoder concatenates features from three intermediate layers
into 7680-dimensional text tokens, which are projected to the transformer width.
The accompanying FLUX.2 autoencoder uses a convolutional encoder–decoder with residual blocks
and a 32-channel latent representation at one-eighth of the image height and width. Packing each
2 × 2 latent neighborhood into the channel dimension produces 128-channel features at a spatial
stride of 16. The encoder uses the posterior mean and normalizes the packed features using fixed
pretrained channel statistics. For an input image of size H × W, the resulting latent representation
has spatial dimensions H/16 × W/16 and 128 channels. The transformer predicts a velocity in this
same representation.
We initialize the latent predictor from the released distilled checkpoint and adapt its transformer
blocks with rank-256 LoRA. The encoded RGB image serves as the input state at t⋆= 0.5, and the
depth latent is obtained by the single flow update in Eq. 3. For text conditioning, we use the fixed
prompt “A dense grayscale monocular depth map of the scene, far surfaces brighter and near surfaces
darker.” Its embedding is computed once and cached. The autoencoder remains frozen; its decoder
is used for the coarse-depth supervision described in the main paper. During pixel adaptation and
final inference, the latent predictor remains frozen and operates at approximately 5122 input pixels,
passing its predicted depth latent directly to the pixel diffusion decoder.
A.2
PIXEL BACKBONE: PID
We use pretrained PiD (Lu et al., 2026) as the pixel backbone, adopting its FLUX.2-compatible v1.5
architecture. Built on PixelDiT (Yu et al., 2026b), PiD combines a patch transformer with a pixel
pathway to jointly perform latent decoding and spatial upsampling. The patch transformer processes
16×16 image patches using 14 MM-DiT blocks with hidden dimension 1536 and 24 attention heads,
allowing image and text tokens to interact through joint attention. The pixel pathway uses two
PiT blocks with 16-dimensional pixel features, conditioned on the patch representations through
adaptive normalization. Each PiT block compresses the features within each patch for attention
across patches, then expands them back for pixel updates.
A convolutional adapter maps the conditioning latent to features aligned with the image patch grid.
For FLUX.2 latents, the packed 128-channel representation is first unpacked into 32 channels and
resized by nearest-neighbor interpolation to H/p × W/p, where p = 16. Two 3 × 3 convolutions
with an intermediate SiLU activation and four residual blocks extract features with 1024 channels.
These features are flattened into tokens and independently projected to the backbone width:

DATASET DETAILS
To improve training efficiency and reduce storage requirements, we filter and subsample the larger
training datasets. The aim is to retain informative samples while reducing low-quality supervision
and redundant observations.
For Hypersim, VKITTI2, TartanAir, and IRS, we adopt training subsets based on the quality fil-
tering of AnyDepth (Ren et al., 2026), which screens samples according to valid-depth coverage,
depth-value distribution, and gradient continuity. For EDEN, we use the smaller sample subset
provided by the dataset authors. For RobbyReal and Rawmantic3D, we observe substantial simi-
larity between neighboring frames within a scene and therefore retain every tenth frame from each
sequence. For UnrealStereo4K, UrbanSyn, and Spring, we retain all available training samples be-
cause these datasets are relatively small and provide valuable supervision for high-resolution depth
prediction. Table 7 summarizes the number of samples used from each dataset, totaling 535,562
image and depth pairs (approximately 536K).

stream uses horizontal flipping only.

C.2
MULTI-ASPECT-RATIO AND MIXED-RESOLUTION TRAINING
Aspect-ratio bucketing. Excessive stretching or compression distorts object shapes and propor-
tions, potentially weakening semantic and geometric cues for depth estimation. At each resolution
level, we therefore use six representative aspect ratios: 1 : 1, 4 : 3, 3 : 2, 16 : 9, 2 : 1, and 10 : 3,
expressed as width to height. Images are cropped to the selected aspect ratio before resizing to pre-
serve object proportions. During bucketed inference, images whose height exceeds their width are
rotated by 90◦, and the predicted depth is rotated back to the original orientation. Table 8 lists the
corresponding image dimensions.
Mixed-resolution sampling. Coarse depth prediction operates at approximately 5122 pixels to
provide scene-level depth structure. The pixel diffusion decoder is first trained at approximately
10242 pixels, allowing it to learn depth prediction at moderate computational cost. We then continue
training with mixed resolutions up to 4K (2160×3840) to adapt the model to a wider range of output
resolutions, while keeping the coarse predictor frozen at the 512-scale setting. The high-resolution
buckets comprise three levels (low, medium, and high) which are trained jointly with 1024-scale
replay to mitigate forgetting. The high-resolution stream uses only UnrealStereo4K, UrbanSyn,
Spring, and Rawmantic3D to learn fine geometric details and depth boundaries. The four levels
(1024-scale and the three high-resolution levels) are sampled with equal probability, each accounting
for 25% of training batches in expectation.
