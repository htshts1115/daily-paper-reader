# Which Depth Estimator for Jetson AGX Orin? 

A 2026 Benchmark of Monocular and Stereo Methods Leaf Jiang NODAR Inc. 

leaf@nodarsensor.com 

# Abstract 

The Jetson AGX Orin is a common computer for robots and vehicles, yet measured accuracy, speed and energy of current depth estimators on it are hard to find. We benchmark nine model families as of October 2026: the monocular Depth Anything V2 and 3, Depth Pro, MoGe-2, Metric3D v2 and UniDepth V2, and the stereo meth-ods libSGM, NVIDIA ESS and Fast-FoundationStereo. Every method runs on the Orin as a TensorRT FP16 en-gine or native CUDA code at up to three input sizes, up to the full 5.7 MP, and is scored for metric accuracy against Middlebury 2014 ground truth (non-occluded pix-els of the 10 scenes whose disparity stays below 256 px), GPU time, energy and licence. Stereo is far more accu-rate. At 30 fps, NVIDIA’s ESS reaches 0.70% AbsRel in 8.6 ms and libSGM 0.73% in 25 ms; with more compute, Fast-FoundationStereo reaches 0.33%. The best monoc-ular models reach 12% with the true focal length (Depth Anything 3 Metric-L in 33 ms, Depth Pro in 803 ms), and 3.8–4.0% only with an oracle per-image scale (MoGe-2-L, UniDepth V2-B, Metric3D v2-L). Stereo error falls as the input resolution grows; no monocular model improves, and the metric scale of most drifts away from the truth outside their training resolution. At depth discontinu-ities, which AbsRel barely registers, the monocular mod-els scatter 17–46% of the edge points between foreground and background, learned stereo 2–9% and libSGM at full resolution 1%. We give recommendations per frame-rate budget and release all code, per-scene results and engine build scripts. 

# 1 Introduction 

Dense metric depth is a core input for driving, robotics and industrial automation, and the NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin is one of the most common computers on which it runs. Two families of camera-based methods compete for it. Monocular depth networks such as Depth Anything V2 [1] and 3 [2], Depth Pro [3], MoGe-2 [4], Metric3D v2 [5] and UniDepth V2 [6] infer metric depth from a sin-gle image with large vision transformers. Stereo methods triangulate depth from two calibrated cameras, with clas-sical matching such as semi-global matching (SGM) [7] or with learned matchers such as NVIDIA’s ESS [8] and Fast-FoundationStereo [9]. Choosing among them for an Orin is hard. Papers re-port accuracy on desktop GPUs, at the networks’ training resolution and often after aligning the prediction to the ground truth; embedded timings, if any, come from differ-ent devices, precisions and input sizes; and licences differ from permissive to non-commercial. This paper is a prac-tical benchmark for that choice. We run every method on the same Jetson AGX Orin 64GB as a TensorRT FP16 engine (accuracy from FP32 where FP16 is unreliable) or as native CUDA code, at up to three input sizes from its native or smallest input to the full 5.7 MP, and score every output against the same full-resolution Middlebury 2014 ground truth [10] on the 10 scenes whose disparity stays within the 256-level range of the semi-global matcher. We report metric accuracy, GPU time, energy and licence, and derive recommendations per frame-rate budget. 

Contributions. (i) Measured metric accuracy, GPU time and energy on Jetson AGX Orin for nine current monocular and stereo model families, each at up to three input sizes (table 7). (ii) Recommendations: the most ac-curate stereo and monocular configuration for each frame-rate budget, with licences (section 5). (iii) A resolution-scaling study: stereo error falls with resolution, monoc-ular error does not, because the monocular metric scale drifts outside the training resolution and the shape does not improve (section 6.1). (iv) An analysis of depth dis-continuities, where AbsRel is blind to the “flying” points that the networks produce and a classical matcher can be the better choice (section 6.3). (v) All code, per-scene results, engine build scripts and this paper’s source, re-leased at https://github.com/nodarhub/orin-depth -benchmark .

# 2 Related work 

Monocular depth. MiDaS [11] and DPT [12] estab-lished zero-shot relative depth by mixing datasets. Depth Anything [13, 1] scales this with DINOv2 features [14] and pseudo-labelled data, with metric variants fine-tuned on Hypersim [15] (indoor) and Virtual KITTI (outdoor). Depth Anything 3 [2] predicts depth and camera rays from any number of views with a plain DINOv2 trans-1arXiv:2610.27199v1 [cs.RO] 19 Oct 2026 former; its monocular metric model predicts depth rela-tive to the focal length. Depth Pro [3] predicts metric depth and focal length from a multi-scale ViT at 1536 2.MoGe-2 [4] predicts an affine-invariant point map, from which it recovers the focal length and shift, plus a global metric scale from the class token. Metric3D v2 [5] pre-dicts depth in a canonical camera space with a fixed focal length and rescales it with the true one; UniDepth V2 [6] predicts a camera representation together with depth and can take the true intrinsics as input. Tile-based meth-ods such as PatchFusion [16] and PatchRefiner [17] tar-get high-resolution monocular metric depth by processing overlapping crops and fusing them with a global estimate. 

Stereo depth. SGM [7] remains the workhorse of em-bedded stereo, with open-source GPU implementations such as libSGM [18]. Learned matchers such as RAFT-Stereo [19] and FoundationStereo [20] are far more ac-curate but expensive. Fast-FoundationStereo [9] dis-tils FoundationStereo into a real-time model. NVIDIA’s ESS [8], distributed with Isaac ROS, is a compact learned stereo network designed for robots on Jetson. Embedded GPUs also ship dedicated stereo hardware: the Orin’s optical flow accelerator (OFA) computes SGM-style dis-parity, exposed through NVIDIA VPI; we do not evaluate it. 

Monocular versus stereo. Pseudo-LiDAR [21] showed that image-based depth representation matters for 3D detection, with stereo far ahead of monocular. Depth networks are also known to smooth across depth discontinuities and produce “flying pixels” between foreground and background. We are not aware of a study that compares how the metric accuracy of current monocular foundation models and of stereo changes with input resolution, or that compares them under a common embedded compute budget. 

# 3 Methods compared 

Each method runs once per frame on the full-resolution Middlebury images. Methods that cannot process the full 5.7 MP image on the 64 GB Orin receive the images downsampled to the largest input they support. Because the monocular networks are trained at much smaller in-puts, we also run each of them at its native input size, and we run libSGM and Fast-FoundationStereo at com-parably small inputs so that both sensor types are com-pared at low compute as well. Every method that can therefore runs at two or three input sizes, which gives the resolution-scaling curves of section 6.1: Depth Anything V2-S and -L, Depth Anything 3, MoGe-2, Metric3D v2 and UniDepth V2 also run at about twice their native size in each dimension, and libSGM at full, half and quarter resolution. ESS has a fixed input size. Table 1 lists the processing resolutions, disparity search range and limiting factor of each method. 

libSGM. The open-source CUDA semi-global matcher of Fixstars [18] (version 3.1.0, Apache-2.0). It computes a per-pixel Hamming cost on symmetric 9 × 7 census codes, aggregates it along four scan paths (horizontal and vertical), selects the winner with a uniqueness test and parabolic sub-pixel refinement, applies a 3 × 3 median fil-ter and invalidates pixels that fail a left–right consistency check. It stores the aggregated cost volume per path with 64-bit offsets, so it runs at full resolution. We run it on grey-scale input with 256 disparity levels at full resolution and at half and quarter resolution (area downsampling); the 256 levels cover 255 px of full-resolution disparity at full resolution, ≈510 px at half and ≈1020 px at quarter resolution. Its smoothness penalties P1 and P2 are tuned separately for each resolution (section C); all other pa-rameters keep libSGM’s defaults. 

Fast-FoundationStereo (FFS). Official research checkpoints 23-36-37 (8 GRU iterations, most accurate) and 20-30-48 (4 iterations, fastest), exported with the authors’ single-ONNX script. The largest input that fits on the 64 GB Orin is 768 × 1152: at 960 × 1440 TensorRT finds no implementation for the cost-volume pooling, and at 1280 × 1920 the ONNX export runs out of host memory. To show how FFS scales, we also run both checkpoints at 512 × 768 and 384 × 576. The maximum disparity is 0.27 × the input width rounded up to amultiple of 32 (320, 224 and 160 px; ≈800 px at full res-olution), which covers the dataset. We also benchmark C-Fast-FoundationStereo, the commercially licensed checkpoint ( nvidia/c-fast-foundationstereo ), at the same three sizes: it has the architecture and configuration of 23-36-37 with separately trained weights. 

Depth Pro. Fixed 1536 × 1536 network input. Its met-ric depth needs a focal length: we evaluate it both with the true focal length from the Middlebury calibration and with Depth Pro’s own field-of-view estimate. 

Depth Anything V2 (metric). The Hypersim (in-door) metric checkpoints, which suit Middlebury’s indoor scenes, at the Small, Base and Large sizes, run at full res-olution (1988 × 2884, i.e. 142 × 206 patches of 14 px) and, for reference, at their native 518 × 770 input (the 518 px short side they are trained at) and, for Small and Large, at 1008 × 1512. Exporting at full resolution requires memory-efficient attention (identical computation); the TensorRT engines take 27–79 min to build and use most of the Orin’s 64 GB.

MoGe-2. The Small, Base and Large checkpoints (ViT-S/B/L, the “-normal” variants) at their default res-3olution level, 3600 patch tokens ( ≈700 × 1008 for Middle-bury’s 3:2 images), and at 14,400 tokens ( ≈1400 × 2030) and full resolution (one token per 14 × 14 px, ≈29,000 to-kens), with the predicted metric scale. Metric3D v2. The ViT-Small and ViT-Large models with the official preprocessing: the image is resized to fit the 616 × 1064 input keeping its aspect ratio and padded, and the depth predicted in a canonical camera with a 1000 px focal length is rescaled with the true focal length of the resized image. We also use inputs of 1232 × 2128 and 1988 × 2884 (full resolution). Its ViT attention is computed in blocks of queries at full resolution, which is the same computation in less memory. 

UniDepth V2. The Small, Base and Large models through the official infer , given the true intrinsics. Its TensorRT engines take the camera as rays (the official UniDepthV2ONNXcam export). 

ESS and Light ESS. NVIDIA’s stereo networks from Isaac ROS (NGC model version 4.1.0), with fixed inputs of 576 × 960 (ESS) and 288 × 480 (Light ESS); custom layers come from the TensorRT plugin library distributed with the model. Both images are resized to the network input, which changes their aspect ratio from 3:2 to 5:3 but not the horizontal disparity, and normalised as on the model card. We use the dense disparity output, without the optional confidence threshold. 

# 4 Experimental setup 

Platform. NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin 64GB developer kit, JetPack 6.2 (L4T R36.4.3), CUDA 12.6, TensorRT 10.3, MAXN power mode. All networks are timed as TensorRT FP16 engines built on the device. Monocular accuracy comes from the FP32 PyTorch models with the official pre- and post-processing, except for Depth Any-thing V2 at native input and V2-B/L at full resolution, whose FP16 engines (and those at 1008 ×1512) agree with FP32 to within 0.25% and are used for both (section 6.5). ESS and Light ESS are evaluated with their TensorRT engines, the only form in which they are distributed. 

Data. Middlebury 2014 [10] with “perfect” rectifica-tion at full resolution ( ≈2.9k × 2.0k). Of the 23 scenes with public ground truth we use the 10 whose non-occluded ground-truth disparity stays below 256 px (at most 243 px), so that the 256-level semi-global matcher is not limited by its disparity range at any of the resolutions we test; section A lists them. In the other 13 scenes near objects reach 265–712 px of disparity at full resolution. 

Non-occluded ground truth. Middlebury’s ground truth comes from structured light, not from stereo match-ing, so it also covers pixels that the right camera cannot see: on average 5.8% of the ground-truth pixels are oc-cluded in the right view and 2.7% fall outside its field of view. No stereo method can match these pixels, so, following MiddEval3, we evaluate every method only on the non-occluded ground truth (91% of it): pixels whose match lies inside the right image and whose right-view ground-truth disparity agrees within 1 px. Monocular methods are evaluated on the same pixels. 

Protocol. Downsampled outputs are upsampled to full resolution, bilinearly for the dense network outputs and with nearest-neighbour interpolation for libSGM, so that its holes stay holes; disparities are rescaled by the width ratio. Depth follows from disparity via Z = f B/ (d +

doffs ); monocular depth is converted to disparity the same way, so a monocular pixel predicted beyond the zero-disparity distance f B/d offs counts as missing. 

Metrics. For disparity: bad-t, the percentage of non-occluded ground-truth pixels with error > t px, where missing predictions count as errors. For depth [22]: Ab-sRel and the threshold accuracy δ < 1.05 (fraction of pix-els within 5% of the true depth), over the predicted pix-els. The uniqueness and left–right checks of libSGM leave holes, so we report it both raw (density, plus errors on pre-dicted pixels) and after MiddEval3-style background hole filling (each hole takes the smaller of the nearest valid disparities to its left and right on the same row). For monocular methods we additionally report oracle-scaled 

metrics, where each prediction is multiplied by the per-image median ratio to the non-occluded ground truth, an upper bound on what any scale-recovery scheme could achieve. 

Accuracy, not precision. All metrics above measure 

accuracy : the error of each depth value with respect to the ground truth. We deliberately do not report precision ,i.e. the noise or repeatability of the depth map around its own local or temporal mean, because metric accu-racy is what downstream tasks such as obstacle detection, ground-plane estimation and free-space computation de-pend on. Precision is particularly misleading for neural networks. A network can produce a perfectly smooth, noise-free surface that is nevertheless in the wrong place: in our experience with field data, a monocular network renders a flat floor as a perfectly flat plane with no visible noise, yet with its tilt off by several degrees. A precision metric would rate such a hallucinated surface as excellent; 4an accuracy metric against independent ground truth ex-poses the error. The oracle-scaled metrics remove only a single global scale factor per image, so such structural errors (tilt, warping, misplaced objects) remain in them as well. Classical stereo shows the opposite pattern: nois-ier depth values and missing pixels where matching fails, but on the pixels it does return, its error is governed by matching and triangulation geometry rather than by a learned prior of what the scene should look like. 

Speed and energy. For networks we report the me-dian trtexec GPU compute time (spin-wait, 3 s warm-up, ≥20 s run). For libSGM we report the median per-frame GPU busy time from an Nsight Systems trace: the union of all kernel and copy intervals, including the up-load of both images and the download of the disparity. Power is sampled with tegrastats (VDD_GPU_SOC + VDD_CPU_CV); dynamic energy per frame is ( Pload −

Pidle )/throughput. 

# 5 Which method to use 

Table 2 summarises the results as recommendations for a single Jetson AGX Orin. 

With two cameras. At 30 fps, ESS is the best choice: 0.70% AbsRel with a dense output in 8.6 ms, and Light ESS 1.16% in 2.8 ms, which leaves room for several cam-era pairs or other networks. libSGM at half resolution is about as accurate (0.73% in 25 ms) on the 85% of the pixels it returns, is open source under Apache-2.0 and can be tuned or modified. Where GPU time is the scarcest resource, libSGM at quarter resolution is the open-source choice: 1.18% AbsRel on 88% of the pixels in only 6.1 ms and 0.18 J per frame, so that at 30 fps it occupies 18% of the Orin’s GPU and leaves the rest to other tasks; only Light ESS needs less (2.8 ms for 1.16%, dense). At 10 fps, Fast-FoundationStereo with 4 iterations at 384 × 576 reaches 0.64% in 68 ms, and with more compute 0.33% (8 iterations, 768 × 1152, 758 ms); its research checkpoints are licensed for non-commercial use only. The commer-cially licensed C-Fast-FoundationStereo is as accurate as the research checkpoint of the same architecture (0.33% at 768 × 1152 in 760 ms, 0.52% at 384 × 576 in 116 ms), so for a commercial product it is the choice when more than 100 ms per frame is available. 

With one camera. No monocular model comes close to stereo in metric accuracy. The most accurate are Depth Anything 3 Metric-Large at its native input (12.3% in 33 ms, Apache-2.0) and Depth Pro (12.0% in 803 ms), both given the true focal length. If the metric scale can be obtained from another source, such as a known object size, a range sensor or odometry, the best shapes come from MoGe-2-L (3.8% with oracle scale, 212 ms, MIT), UniDepth V2-B (3.9%, 85 ms, non-commercial) and Met-ric3D v2-L (4.0%, 265 ms, BSD-2-Clause), and at 30 fps from Depth Anything 3 (6.0%). Running the monocu-lar models above their native input does not help (sec-tion 6.1). 

# 6 Results 

6.1 Resolution scaling 

Figure 1 plots AbsRel against the number of pixels each method processes, for every method that runs at more than one input size. Stereo error falls with every increase in resolution. libSGM goes from 1.18% at quarter reso-lution (0.36 MP) to 0.73% at half and 0.63% at full res-olution (5.7 MP); Fast-FoundationStereo from 0.51% to 0.42% and 0.33% with 8 iterations and from 0.64% to 0.51% and 0.40% with 4, between 0.22 and 0.88 MP. The gains are smaller than the ideal of the triangulation ar-gument, which halves the error with every doubling of the image side at a constant matching error in pixels; at the last step libSGM gains 14%. The matching error in pixels grows at higher resolution, most likely because a fixed-size matching window then covers less of the scene. No monocular model improves with resolution. Depth Anything 3 goes from 12.3% at its native 350 × 504 in-put to 14.2% at 700 × 1008 and 54.9% at full resolution, MoGe-2-L from 19.2% to 23.4% and 24.4%, Depth Any-thing V2-L from 32.5% to 39.5% and 42.7%, and V2-S from 29.9% to 43.8% and 56.0%. Metric3D v2 collapses above its native input: its error rises from 57–60% to 128–162%, and it places 86–95% of the pixels beyond the zero-disparity distance. UniDepth V2 stays at 20–28% at every size, slightly better at full resolution for the Small model (24.7% to 22.6%) and worse for the Large model (20.5% to 28.1%). The cause is the metric scale (table 3). Depth Anything V2 places the scenes 1.45–1.54 times too close at its native input and 1.8–2.4 times too close at full resolution, and Depth Anything 3 is almost unbiased at its native input (scale 0.98) but places the scenes 1.5 times too far at full resolution. The models are trained at 0.2–0.7 MP; at full resolution each 14 px patch cov-ers much less of the scene, the token count is 8–32 times larger and the positional embeddings are interpolated far beyond the training range, and nothing in the networks ties the metric scale to a quantity that becomes more precise with more pixels. With the scale removed by an oracle (fig. 1b) the shape does not improve either: the oracle-scaled AbsRel stays flat or rises, from 6.0% to 6.2% for Depth Anything 3, from 3.8% to 4.0% for MoGe-2-L, from 8.3–8.9% to 9.7– 10.7% for Depth Anything V2, from 3.9–5.7% to 6.5– 9.0% for UniDepth V2 and from 4.0–6.5% to 5.2–8.4% for Metric3D v2. 6.2 Accuracy 

Table 7 in section B lists accuracy, GPU time and energy for every configuration, averaged over the 10 scenes. 

Stereo accuracy. Fast-FoundationStereo is the most accurate method: 0.33% AbsRel with 8 iterations and 0.40% with 4 at 768 × 1152, at 100% density and 99% of pixels within 5% of the true depth. It degrades grace-fully with input size: each step down to 512 × 768 and 384 × 576 cuts its GPU time by 2–3 times and adds only about 0.1 percentage points of AbsRel (0.51% and 0.64% at 384 × 576). libSGM reaches 0.63% AbsRel on 76% of the non-occluded pixels at full resolution, 0.73% on 85% at half and 1.18% on 88% at quarter resolution; each halv-ing of the image side cuts its GPU time by about four. At full resolution libSGM is about as accurate as Fast-FoundationStereo at 384 × 576 (0.64%) on the pixels it returns, at 1.5 times the GPU time but without its den-sity. After hole filling, libSGM reaches 1.92%, 1.79% and 2.13% at full, half and quarter resolution. The tuned smoothness penalties (section C) lower libSGM’s AbsRel by 39%, 30% and 9% relative to its defaults, at 4–8 per-centage points lower density. NVIDIA’s ESS is dense and reaches 0.70% AbsRel at its fixed 576 × 960 input, and Light ESS 1.16% at 288 × 480, the best accuracy per unit of compute of all methods. Their bad-2 rates (20% and 37%) are higher than those of Fast-FoundationStereo (7–17%) because a disparity error of one pixel at their in-put is three or six pixels at full resolution. The commer-cially licensed C-Fast-FoundationStereo matches the re-search checkpoint 23-36-37 , whose architecture it shares, to within 0.01 percentage points of AbsRel at every input size (0.33%, 0.43% and 0.52%), with a slightly lower bad-2 rate at 768 × 1152 (6.0% against 6.5%) and the same GPU time. 

Monocular accuracy. No monocular model is close to metric accuracy at Middlebury’s 1.3–7.6 m depth range. The best raw results come from Depth Pro with the true focal length, 12.0% AbsRel (19.0% with its own focal-length estimate), and from Depth Anything 3 Metric-Large, 12.3% at a fraction of the compute; only 29% and 31% of their pixels are within 5% of the true depth. MoGe-2 reaches 19–33% with the true focal length and 30–40% with its own: its metric scale is unreliable on these scenes, and with the true focal length it places parts of some scenes beyond the zero-disparity distance (76– 97% density). Depth Anything V2 has 30–32% AbsRel at its native input and 41–56% at full resolution, where only 0.1–2% of its pixels are within 5% of the true depth. None of the monocular models benefits from a larger in-put: Depth Anything 3 has 14.2% AbsRel at 700 × 1008 and 54.9% at full resolution, where it places 44% of the pixels beyond the zero-disparity distance, and MoGe-2 with the true focal length has 23–43% at 1400 × 2030 and 24–40% at full resolution, while its oracle-scaled er-ror rises by 0.2–1.1 percentage points. 6.4 Qualitative results 

Figure 3 shows depth and error maps on two scenes for every method at full resolution, or at its maximum or fixed input size, with the monocular networks after or-acle scaling, i.e. with their global scale error removed. Stereo errors are mostly below 1% and concentrate at depth discontinuities and thin structures (leaves, straw); libSGM leaves holes there and on low-texture walls, and its full-resolution output is speckled where the left–right check rejects isolated pixels. The oracle-scaled monocular 8depth maps look plausible and correctly ordered, yet keep 2.7–9.3% error that is spatially structured: smooth bands across the walls and floor of Classroom1 that correspond to tilted or warped surfaces. This is the failure mode that a precision metric would not see (section 4). Without the oracle scale (table 7), the monocular errors are far larger. Figure 4 compares the methods near the monocular models’ native input, as deployed, i.e. without oracle scale. libSGM at quarter resolution (6.1 ms) keeps about 1% error with holes at occlusions and in low-texture re-gions; Fast-FoundationStereo at 512 × 768 is dense and has 0.35–0.48% error. The monocular models at their native input have 11–39% error on these scenes: Depth Anything V2 places almost all of Sword1 at the near end of the depth range and MoGe-2-L at the far end, and the error of Depth Anything 3 falls to 2.0–9.9% only with the oracle scale. 

6.5 FP16 fidelity 

We compared every monocular TensorRT FP16 engine with its FP32 PyTorch model on one scene. Depth Any-thing V2 at native resolution differs by 0.10–0.17%, and Base and Large at full resolution by 0.19% and 0.25% (median relative depth difference). Depth Anything V2-Small at full resolution differs by 17.5%, and Depth Pro by 1.9% (with a 1.6 ◦ shift of its field-of-view estimate). For these two we report accuracy from FP32 and speed from FP16, which favours them. The FP32 Small model is itself inaccurate at this resolution (57.7% AbsRel), so the FP16 error is not the cause of its poor result. For Depth Anything 3 and MoGe-2 we found two Ten-sorRT 10.3 problems, both in fused kernels: with ev-ery intermediate tensor marked as an output the engines are correct, and ONNX Runtime reproduces PyTorch to within 0.002%. First, the attention pattern that PyTorch exports from scaled_dot_product_attention (queries and keys each scaled by the square root of the scale factor) produced a wrong engine; exporting the same computa-tion as softmax( q s k ⊤) v fixed it. The Depth Anything V2 full-resolution engines use the same attention export; Base and Large match FP32, but the 17.5% deviation of the Small engine is consistent with this problem, so its timing may not represent a correct engine. Second, MoGe-2-S and -B’s three-layer metric-scale head on the class token came out wrong even in FP32; we run this neg-ligible computation outside the engine. After these fixes the Depth Anything 3 engine reproduces the FP32 Ab-sRel to within 0.2 percentage points on the check scenes processed at its 350 × 504 input size, and the MoGe-2 engines differ from FP32 by 0.03–1.1% (median relative depth). For the larger inputs, up to ≈29,000 tokens at full resolution, the export processes attention in blocks of 2,048 queries, which is the same computation without tracing the full attention matrix. Their accuracy is re-ported from FP32 and their speed from the fixed FP16 engines. The UniDepth V2 engines, exported with the same at-tention form, agree with FP32 to within 0.05–0.49% (me-dian relative depth difference). The Metric3D v2 engines differ by 1.3–3.3% (99th percentile up to 15%), proba-bly because its iterative refinement decoder accumulates FP16 rounding; for both we report accuracy from FP32 and speed from FP16.

# 8 Discussion and limitations 

Why stereo scales and monocular depth does not. 

Stereo measures depth: the disparity of a point is ob-served in the images, and the calibrated focal length and baseline turn it into metric depth, with an error that shrinks as the pixels become smaller. A monocular net-work infers depth from learned regularities of the scene, and its metric scale comes from a prior over object sizes and camera geometry seen in training. More pixels add detail but no information about that scale, and outside the training resolution the prior itself shifts (table 3). Edges and the limits of AbsRel. The mean AbsRel over predicted pixels rewards dense, smooth output and is nearly blind to depth discontinuities (section 6.3). Neither effect changes the ranking between stereo and monocular methods here, but it means that the most accurate method by AbsRel is not necessarily the best at object boundaries: a classical matcher at full resolution produces the cleanest boundaries of all meth-ods we tested.

Fairness to monocular methods. We gave the monocular models the true focal length or intrinsics (Depth Pro, Depth Anything 3, MoGe-2, Metric3D v2, UniDepth V2), a domain-matched indoor checkpoint (Depth Anything V2 Hypersim) and an oracle scale, none of which is available in deployment. We did not fine-tune any model on Middlebury. We cannot rule out that Middlebury images appear in any model’s training data; if they do, that would favour the networks. This selection favours stereo only in the sense that it removes a configuration problem with a known fix: running the matcher at lower resolution (at half resolution 256 levels span ≈510 px, at quarter resolution ≈1020 px), or a matcher with a larger search range. It also removes the closest-range scenes, in which stereo depth is most accurate. 

Parameter tuning. libSGM’s P1 and P2 were tuned on the evaluation scenes, which favours it; cross-validation with one scene held out raises its AbsRel by only 0.01–0.08 percentage points (section C), so the con-clusions do not depend on it. The networks use their released weights without any tuning. 

Timing methodology. Network timings exclude im-age resizing, normalisation and post-processing (for MoGe-2 the focal-length and shift recovery and the metric-scale head), while the SGM timings include copy-ing the images to and the disparity from the GPU, so the comparison slightly favours the networks. The fractional cost model assumes compute can be shared across tasks; with dedicated hardware, cost rises in steps of whole mod-ules. 

# 9 Conclusion 

On a Jetson AGX Orin, stereo gives metric depth that no current monocular foundation model approaches. At 30 fps, NVIDIA’s ESS reaches 0.70% AbsRel with dense 13 output in 8.6 ms and libSGM 0.73% in 25 ms; with more compute Fast-FoundationStereo reaches 0.33%, as does its commercially licensed checkpoint. The best monocu-lar models, given the true focal length, reach 12% (Depth Anything 3 Metric-L in 33 ms, Depth Pro in 803 ms), and only with an oracle per-image scale do MoGe-2-L, UniDepth V2-B and Metric3D v2-L reach 3.8–4.0%, still several times the error of stereo. Stereo error falls as the input resolution grows, while no monocular model im-proves, because its metric scale drifts outside the training resolution and its shape does not sharpen; and at object boundaries the monocular models scatter 17–46% of the points between surfaces, an error that AbsRel barely reg-isters and where a classical matcher at full resolution is the cleanest of all. For metric depth on an Orin, a second camera with ESS, libSGM or Fast-FoundationStereo is the better choice at every compute budget we measured; monocular models are the choice when only one camera is possible and the metric scale can be recovered by other means.

17 C libSGM parameters 

Table 8 lists the libSGM parameters we use. P1 and P2 were chosen separately for each input resolution by a grid search ( P1 ∈ { 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 10 , 15 , 20 , 30 }, P2 ∈ { 40 , 60 , 90 , 120 , 160 , 200 , 224 }, P1 < P 2) that minimises the mean AbsRel over the 10 evaluation scenes, scored exactly as in table 7. All other parameters are libSGM’s defaults, except that we use four aggregation paths instead of eight and enable sub-pixel output. libSGM stores aggregated path costs in 8 bits, so the census cost (at most 31 for the symmetric 9 × 7 census) plus P2 must stay below 256; the largest usable P2 is therefore 224, and the search selected it at every resolution. The search has no separate training set, so the selected parameters are tuned on the evaluation scenes, which favours libSGM. To estimate how much, we repeated the selection ten times with one scene held out and scored each held-out scene with the parameters chosen on the other nine. The held-out mean AbsRel is 0.65%, 0.75% and 1.26% at full, half and quarter resolution, against 0.63%, 0.73% and 1.18% when tuned on all ten scenes and 1.03%, 1.04% and 1.29% with the default P1 = 10, P2 = 120. The held-out choices agree with the selected ones in 9 of 10 folds at full and half resolution and in 7 of 10 at quarter resolution. Minimising AbsRel over predicted pixels also lowers the density: the stronger smoothing makes more pixels fail the left–right check (76%, 85% and 88% density, against 84%, 89% and 91% with the defaults).
