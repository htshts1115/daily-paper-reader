# ZeroDSS: Semantic Segmentation from Class Names Alone 

Tian Ming, Marzi Heidari, and Yuhong Guo 

School of Computer Science, Carleton University, Ottawa, Canada 

Abstract. Semantic segmentation depends heavily on real images with pixel-level annotations, making dense supervision a major obstacle to deployment in new domains. Recent diffusion-based methods reduce annotation costs by gen-erating synthetic image–mask pairs from text prompts and cross-attention maps. However, they often rely on dataset-aligned captions or image-specific textual supervision, and degrade when only category names are available. We study task-specific data-free semantic segmentation, where training uses generic pre-trained generative priors but has access to no target-domain images, masks, or captions—only a predefined category set. We introduce Zero-Data Semantic Seg-mentation (ZeroDSS), a feedback-driven approach that treats synthetic supervi-sion as an adaptively controllable training distribution rather than a direct surro-gate for real data. ZeroDSS constructs a semantically structured prompt distribu-tion through LLM-guided compositional generation and adaptive class rebalanc-ing, then converts the prompts into image–mask pairs using diffusion attention. Its feedback-driven distribution refinement consists of a two-phase closed-loop training procedure with a single loop-back step. In Phase I, a segmenter is trained using confidence-weighted supervision and dual-scale consistency. In Phase II, model feedback is used to adaptively target underperforming categories and re-fine diffusion-derived masks using model predictions. After this refined initial training, a teacher–student framework is utilized in the final phase to iteratively improve the fused supervision and segmenter through exponential moving aver-age (EMA) updates. Using synthetic training data only, ZeroDSS achieves 35.0% mIoU on MS COCO with DeepLabV3, and 66.3% and 63.5% mIoU on PASCAL VOC with DeepLabV3 and Mask2Former, respectively. These results show that explicitly constructing and iteratively refining the synthetic supervision distribu-tion can substantially reduce the synthetic-to-real gap in dense prediction. 

# 1 Introduction 

Semantic segmentation has advanced substantially through high-capacity architectures and large datasets with dense pixel annotations. Modern systems obtain strong results on curated real-image corpora such as PASCAL VOC [6] and MS COCO [15], with rep-resentative architectures including DeepLab [3]. This progress remains tightly coupled to access to real images and their dense pixel-level labels. In privacy-restricted applica-tions, proprietary data silos, or rapidly emerging categories, collecting and annotating such data can be costly or infeasible, making dense supervision a central obstacle to deployment. arXiv:2610.24405v1 [cs.CV] 17 Oct 2026 2 Tian Ming, Marzi Heidari, and Yuhong Guo 

Generative models offer an alternative source of supervision. GAN-based ap-proaches such as DatasetGAN and BigDatasetGAN synthesize pixel labels with limited additional annotation [12, 37], but are constrained by object-centric genera-tion and limited compositional diversity. Text-to-image diffusion models provide a more scalable route: their token-level cross-attention maps encode spatial–semantic correspondence and can be converted into synthetic image–mask pairs [19, 24, 33]. Existing diffusion pipelines, however, usually depend on dataset-aligned prompts derived from real captions or caption surrogates to approximate the target distribution. When only class names are available, naive prompt sampling produces biased category frequencies, implausible combinations, and unstable attention masks, increasing both semantic and visual mismatch with real data. We therefore study class-name-only data-free semantic segmentation. Training may use generic pretrained language and generative models as semantic priors, but receives no task-specific real images, pixel annotations, or dataset-aligned captions. This setting shifts the key question from how to imitate individual real examples to how to construct an effective synthetic training distribution without observing the target domain. To address this problem, we introduce Zero-Data Semantic Segmentation (Ze-roDSS), which treats synthetic supervision as a distribution to construct, optimize under, and iteratively refine based on feedback rather than a direct proxy for real data. LLM-guided compositional validation and adaptive class rebalancing first shape a structured prompt distribution, which is instantiated as image–mask pairs through diffusion attention. Phase I learns an initial segmenter with confidence-weighted supervision and dual-scale consistency regularization. Phase II uses model feedback to identify underperforming categories, generate targeted samples, and refine diffusion-derived masks using model predictions. The updated training data are then fed back into Phase I for further segmentation model training. Phase III performs class-balanced teacher-student self-training to improve the segmenter: a fixed teacher supplies predictions for fusion with the original attention masks, while iterative EMA updates progressively improve the teacher without introducing real-domain supervision. The key contributions of this work are summarized as follows: 

– We formulate class-name-only data-free segmentation around explicit construction and adaptive refinement of the synthetic training distribution. 

– We develop LLM-guided compositional validation, adaptive class-frequency bal-ancing, and segmentation-oriented prompting for structured synthetic supervision. 

– We combine confidence-weighted consistency training, feedback-driven category and mask refinement, and EMA teacher–student self-training. 

– ZeroDSS reaches 35.0% mIoU on COCO and 66.3/63.5% mIoU on VOC with DeepLabV3/Mask2Former, improving the Phase I–II models by 3.8, 4.6, and 3.2 points; the Phase I–II loop-back also supports novel category expansion. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Synthetic Data for Semantic Segmentation 

Synthetic data has long been used to reduce the cost of dense annotation. GAN-based methods such as DatasetGAN and BigDatasetGAN project lightweight segmentation ZeroDSS: Semantic Segmentation from Class Names Alone 3

heads onto generative feature spaces to obtain pixel supervision [12, 37]; related work extracts semantic structure from generative representations without explicit labels [18, 32]. These approaches establish the value of generative priors, but their object-centric biases restrict scene diversity and large-scale distribution control. Simulator-rendered datasets such as GTA5 and SYNTHIA instead provide exact pixel labels directly from graphics engines [23, 25] and are widely used for synthetic-to-real adaptation [8, 30, 31, 36]. Their scene layouts, co-occurrence patterns, and class frequencies are predefined, however, and changing them generally requires modifying the simulator. Recent work shows that downstream segmentation depends not only on annotation quality but also on synthetic–real distribution alignment [2, 26]. ZeroDSS addresses this issue by con-structing and adapting the distribution through prompts rather than relying on a fixed generator or simulator. 

2.2 Diffusion-based Pixel-level Annotation 

Diffusion models support dense prediction in two distinct ways. SegDiff, DDP, DiffusionInst, and MaskDiff formulate semantic, instance, or panoptic segmentation as supervised denoising [1, 7, 9, 10]; diffusion is the predictor itself, and training requires ground-truth masks. A separate line repurposes pretrained text-to-image models as synthetic supervision engines. Stable Diffusion exposes token-level cross-attention maps [24], which DiffuMask and Dataset Diffusion aggregate into pixel-level pseudo-labels aligned with generated images [19, 33]. This removes the need for ground-truth segmentation masks, but the prompts commonly come from real captions or caption surrogates. ZeroDSS follows the attention-derived supervision paradigm while replacing dataset-aligned text with an explicitly constructed class-name-only prompt distribution. 

2.3 Semi-supervised and Self-training Segmentation 

Pseudo-labeling [11], consistency regularization, and teacher–student methods such as Mean Teacher, FixMatch, and CPS [4, 27, 29] exploit unlabeled data by convert-ing model predictions into training targets. Segmentation-specific approaches including ST++, ReCo, and ClassMix further improve structural reliability through confidence selection, representation consistency, or class-aware mixing [16, 21, 35]. These meth-ods generally assume access to unlabeled real images from the target domain. ZeroDSS instead self-trains exclusively on generated images. Moreover, its teacher predictions do not simply replace the available labels: they are fused class-wise with the origi-nal diffusion attention masks and confidence-filtered before supervising a continuously trained student. The method therefore adopts iterative teacher–student refinement while preserving the class-name-only data-free protocol. 

2.4 Prompt Construction and Language-guided Data Generation 

Text prompts are the main control interface for text-to-image generation, so their formu-lation directly shapes the semantics and composition of synthetic images. # 3 Problem Setting 

We study data-free semantic segmentation in which the only task-specific information available during training is a predefined foreground category set C = {1, . . . , C }; cate-gory 0 is reserved for background. Unlike supervised or semi-supervised settings, train-ing has access to no target-domain images, pixel-level annotations, or dataset-aligned captions. Generic pretrained language and generative models are allowed as reusable semantic priors, but they are not supplied with examples from the target segmentation dataset. Starting from C, LLM-generated prompts condition a text-to-image diffusion model to produce a synthetic dataset Dsyn = {(ˆ xi, ˆmi)}Ni=1 , where ˆxi is a generated image and ˆmi ∈ { 0, . . . , C }H×W is its attention-derived mask. The real evaluation set Dreal = {(xj , mj )}Mj=1 remains strictly unavailable during training. A segmenter

foreground classes and background. It is optimized only on Dsyn and evaluated only afterward on Dreal . The objective is therefore not merely to fit synthetic masks, but to generalize across a distribution shift that affects appearance, context, category co-occurrence, object scale, and mask reliability. Because the real distribution cannot be observed, ZeroDSS must improve both synthetic coverage and supervision quality us-ing signals derived entirely from its generated data and trained segmenter. # 4 Method 

ZeroDSS treats synthetic supervision as an adaptivelly controllable training distri-bution rather than a direct proxy for real data. Without real images, captions, or co-occurrence statistics, naive class-name prompting can produce compositional bias, se-vere class imbalance, and brittle attention masks. ZeroDSS therefore constructs a se-mantically structured prompt distribution with compositional and frequency control, then instantiates it into image–mask pairs through diffusion attention. Training pro-ceeds through two phases of closed-loop refinement with a single loop-back, followed by a third phase of model improvement. Phase I learns a robust initial segmenter under noisy synthetic supervision, while Phase II uses model feedback to regenerate under-covered categories, refine their masks, and continue optimization on the updated distri-bution. Phase III iteratively improves fused pseudo-labels and segmenter through EMA teacher–student self-training. Figure 1 summarizes the framework. 

4.1 Prompt Generation: Synthetic Distribution Shaping in Semantic Space 

Without target-domain category co-occurrence statistics, the synthetic distribution must be designed rather than inherited. Independently sampled categories can create implau-sible scenes and unstable attention supervision. ZeroDSS therefore uses an LLM as a semantic prior to control category composition, frequency, and prompt realization.

Phase II: Feedback-Driven Synthetic Distribution Refinement Although Phase I is trained on a balanced synthetic distribution, its construction remains heuristic: some categories can still lack sufficient scale, context, or appearance diversity. Phase II uses model feedback to identify these under-covered semantic regions and refine the syn-thetic distribution. Let IoU c be the class-wise IoU measure of the Phase I model for class c on a held-out synthetic set Dsyn val generated in the same way as Dsyn . We define a performance-deficiency score for class c as rc = (1 − IoU c) + γ κ c, where κc is a fixed LLM-derived category-level prior obtained by grouping foreground categories into easy, average, and hard scale-difficulty levels and assigning the predefined scores, and γ denotes the trade-off hyperparameter. The first term captures empirical segmenta-tion error under the synthetic distribution, while the second introduces a complementary prior on real-world scale-induced difficulty. The prior is a heuristic category-level cue, not a measured difficulty score from real target-domain images. The computed category deficiency scores provide feedback to drive synthetic distribution refinement through targeted deficient category expansion. 

Targeted Deficient Category Expansion. Let Cdef ⊆ C denote the set of performance-deficient categories selected as the top-K foreground categories with largest rc, defining where the synthetic distribution should be expanded. For the selected deficient cate-gories, we employ LLM-guided prompt generation to enhance reliability and diversity. In particular, we generate new prompts using two complementary instruction templates to control scene complexity. The first template encourages simple scenes with a single large object and minimal context, producing images where object boundaries are clearly visible. These samples provide reliable attention-derived masks and help the model learn accurate object contours and basic shape cues. The second template encourages ZeroDSS: Semantic Segmentation from Class Names Alone 9

more complex scenes involving contextual clutter, occlusion, multi-object interactions, and small-scale instances, better approximating realistic visual configurations in the tar-get domain. Newly generated samples are then added into the synthetic dataset to form an expanded training dataset ˜Dsyn for model retraining. By expanding the performance-deficient categories, we redistribute the probability mass of the training data toward difficult semantic regions. This procedure can be viewed as distribution-level hard-example mining, operating in data space rather than feature space. It increases support around model-identified weaknesses instead of perturbing intermediate features. 

Mask Fusion and Continued Training under the Refined Distribution. The expanded training set ˜Dsyn follows a refined distribution ˜psyn . However, diffusion derived masks provide prompt-aligned localization but can omit valid regions or contain fragmented components, whereas the trained segmenter often predicts more coherent object struc-ture. Therefore, for continued training on the refined distribution, we perform class-wise mask fusion to combine the complementary signals from the cross-attention supervision and segmentation model predictions, selecting the more reliable label and confidence for each class. The fusion details are provided in the appendix. A single loop-back step is then conducted by returning to Phase I for continued segmentation model training, expecting to improve the segmenter with the refined training set and mask supervision. 

4.4 Teacher-Student Based Self-Training 

In addition to the feedback-driven single loop-back refinement procedure, Phase III further enhances the segmenter using a teacher-student framework. It initializes teacher and student networks from the refined model obtained from the single loop-back based two-phase refinement procedure, such as θT ← θII and θS ← θII , but returns to the original diffusion derived attention-masks rather than the Phase II refined masks. The student network is updated using a sampled class-balanced subset in each round, while the teacher remains fixed during the update of the student network, providing stable pseudo-label targets for the student network. Specifically, the teacher prediction and the original attention mask are fused in a class-wise manner to induce a pseudo-label yi for each pixel. # 5 Experiments 

5.1 Experimental Setup 

Datasets. We evaluate ZeroDSS on PASCAL VOC 2012 [6] and MS COCO 2017 [15], containing 20 and 80 foreground categories, respectively. Real images and annotations are strictly reserved for synthetic-to-real evaluation. Stable Diffusion v2.1-base [24] generates approximately 40k VOC and 80k COCO image–mask pairs at 512 × 512 

resolution with guidance scale 7.5. One-, two-, and three-category prompts are sampled with probabilities (r1, r 2, r 3) = (0 .7, 0.2, 0.1) . For frequency balancing, we set α =0.3, ϵ = 10 −6, and τc = 1 except for person , where τc = 2 . We additionally generate 1k synthetic validation images per dataset for Phase II deficiency estimation. 

Implementation Details. For synthetic data construction, prompts are generated us-ing GPT-4.1 accessed via OpenRouter with moderate sampling temperature to encour-age semantic diversity. Following prior synthetic segmentation baselines [19], we use DeepLabV3 [3] with a ResNet-50 backbone and no auxiliary head. AdamW uses learn-ing rate 10 −4, weight decay 0.01 , backbone multiplier 0.1, a polynomial schedule with exponent 0.9, batch size 8, and 40k Phase I iterations; the consistency coefficient is 

λ = 0 .2. Phase II fixes γ = 0 .5, selects the top K = 10 deficient categories, generates 200 additional images per category. Phase III uses 10 rounds, approximately 25 im-ages per foreground category per round, confidence threshold is set as 0.6, and an EMA cosine schedule from 0.999 to 0.9999; the final EMA teacher is evaluated. Additional generation and training details are provided in the appendix. All experiments report the results in terms of the mean Intersection over Union (mIoU).

Evaluation is conducted on MS COCO and PASCAL VOC using DeepLabV3 and Mask2Former backbones pre-trained on ImageNet. All synthetic methods are trained solely on synthetic data (80k samples for COCO and 40k for VOC) and evalu-ated on the corresponding real validation sets. # 6 Conclusion 

In this paper, we presented ZeroDSS for semantic segmentation in the class-name-only data-free setting. Rather than treating generated samples as a fixed surrogate for real data, the method constructs and progressively refines a synthetic supervision dis-tribution in a model feedback driven manner. LLM-guided prompt generation con-trols semantic composition and class frequency. The single loop-back based two-phase loop performs feedback-driven automatic synthetic distribution refinement and seg-menter retraining. An additional Phase III further improves the model through class-balanced EMA teacher-student self-training. Without target-domain images, masks, or captions, the full model reaches 35.0% mIoU on COCO and 66.3/63.5% on VOC with DeepLabV3/Mask2Former, improving the corresponding Phase I–II models by 3.8, 4.6, and 3.2 points. The Phase I–II model also supports novel category expansion while re-taining performance on the original classes. These results indicate that synthetic-to-real transfer depends not only on generative image quality, but also on principled control of what is generated, how uncertain masks are used, and how supervision is updated during training.

# A Instructions for Prompt Generation 

The prompt-generation instruction is fixed throughout the synthetic data generation pro-cess and consists of several components designed to guide the large language model (LLM) toward producing segmentation-oriented captions. First, the instruction explic-itly specifies the task objective and requirements, informing the LLM that the gener-ated descriptions will be used for diffusion-based image synthesis for semantic seg-mentation. Second, it encourages realistic scene descriptions so that the prompts reflect natural visual situations rather than imaginative or fantasy-like compositions. Third, an attention-aware ordering strategy balances the placement of multiple objects in the sentence so that the diffusion cross-attention can focus more reliably on each target category. Fourth, lexical diversity is encouraged by using varied nouns for the same object types, which increases linguistic diversity without introducing additional prompt complexity. Finally, the instruction regulates the overall scene complexity, including object relations and background content, to avoid overly crowded compositions while maintaining natural interactions between objects. During Phase II, additional prompts are generated with different levels of scene complexity to expand the training distribution of performance-deficient categories. Sim-ple scenes emphasize clean spatial relations and minimal background clutter, which typically produce higher-quality cross-attention masks. In contrast, complex scenes in-troduce multiple objects, richer spatial relations, and more detailed backgrounds. These images better approximate the visual diversity of real-world scenes and therefore im-prove generalization. However, their cross-attention responses are often dispersed and therefore less confident. We retain these samples and their attention-derived masks dur-ing training: the confidence-weighted cross-entropy objective naturally assigns uncer-tain pixels less influence, without an explicit mask- or sample-level discard operation. Figure 3 presents the full instruction used for prompt generation, including the struc-tured guidance described above and the two complexity templates used in Phase II.

# G Hyper-parameter Sensitivity Analysis 

We analyze the effect of the consistency weighting coefficient λ used in the training objective described in the main paper: L(θ) = Lseg + λLcons . Figure 8 presents the analysis results by varying the λ value. Performance peaks at λ = 0 .2, indicating that a moderate contribution of the prediction-consistency term Lcons is sufficient to stabi-lize training under synthetic supervision. When λ is too small, the regularization effect is limited, reducing robustness to synthetic-specific artifacts. Conversely, larger val-ues of λ degrade performance, suggesting that excessive emphasis on Lcons suppresses informative gradients from Lseg and leads to underfitting. These results confirm that ZeroDSS benefits from balanced interaction between supervised risk minimization and distributional consistency regularization.
