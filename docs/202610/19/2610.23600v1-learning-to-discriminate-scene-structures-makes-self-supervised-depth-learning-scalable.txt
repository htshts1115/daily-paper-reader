Abstract
Self-supervised monocular depth estimation is appealing for its potential to scale
with unlabeled data, yet existing methods often degrade when trained on diverse
scenes. This study finds that, under self-supervision, models tend to encode dataset-
specific structural biases rather than transferable scene geometry, leading to cross-
domain optimization conflicts. To address this issue, this study proposes the MaSS
framework, aiming to make self-supervised depth learning scalable to diverse data.
MaSS introduces a dedicated structural representation pathway explicitly decoupled
from contextual features, which is guided by a novel scene-structure discrimination
objective to learn generalized, layout-aware structural representations for depth
decoding. Trained and evaluated on four diverse datasets, both individually and
jointly, MaSS substantially outperforms prior self-supervised methods and is the
first to consistently benefit from mixed-domain training. Zero-shot evaluation
on eight additional datasets further demonstrates its remarkable generalizability,
particularly on those entirely out-of-distribution scenes. The source code is publicly
available at https://mias.group/MaSS.

Introduction
The fundamental advantage of self-supervised learning lies in its potential to leverage ubiquitous
unlabeled data for scalable model training. However, most existing self-supervised monocular depth
estimation (SSMDE) research [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] is predominantly confined to training models individually
on single datasets or scenarios, leaving much of this potential unexplored. Recent efforts have
attempted to directly train models with existing SSMDE methods on large-scale video sequences
curated from online platforms [6, 7], as well as to incorporate vision foundation models (VFMs) as
backbones [8, 7]. Despite these advances, overall performance improvements remain limited. As
demonstrated by our preliminary experiments (see Fig. 1(b)), when existing methods are trained
simultaneously on diverse monocular video data (e.g., indoor, outdoor, dynamic, and adverse weather
scenarios), they fail to benefit mutually from the increased diversity and do not exhibit improved
generalization to out-of-distribution scenes. Instead, we observe a performance decline compared to
models trained individually on a single dataset. This observation demonstrates that existing SSMDE
methods have not yet achieved effective scalability to diverse data.
This study begins by investigating the challenges that arise when SSMDE is exposed to diverse
data distributions during training. It is worth noting that the novel-view synthesis-based supervision
inevitably introduces noisy and artifact-laden supervisory signals in scenarios involving occlusions,
textureless regions, and illumination changes [9, 10]. Even setting aside such noise, the gradients
induced by self-supervised losses often fail to reflect depth errors faithfully [4]. As a result, the
optimization process is inherently prone to local minima, leading to poor convergence stability [4].
Although these challenges frequently occur during self-supervised training, existing methods can still
achieve reasonable accuracy on a single, homogeneous dataset [3]. This is largely because the network
can exploit the structural bias inherent to that dataset, thereby compensating for weak supervision.
The key evidence we find is that under the existing SSMDE framework, the coarsest, high-level
feature of the depth encoder does not capture depth-related cues, such as the foreground–background
relation and perspective geometry. Instead, it primarily reflects the dataset-specific structural biases.
When training on diverse datasets, different data domains often require the model to learn inconsistent
structural biases for their respective convergence, leading to the observed performance drop.
These findings motivate us to explicitly guide the network to learn a robust, generalized scene-structure
representation for depth decoding. Based on this core idea, we develop the MaSS framework that
Make Self-supervised depth learning Scalable to diverse data. MaSS bridges a conventional depth
encoder with a frozen pretrained VFM through a bidirectional interaction architecture, drawing
inspiration from [11]. In this design, the depth encoder focuses on extracting geometric cues that are
difficult to recover from VFM features [8]. The interacted VFM features are then fed into two feature
projectors to reconstruct complementary, collapse-free representations under explicit constraints
and learning objectives. Specifically, one projector is adapted to learn a contextual representation
informative for depth estimation, such as object-level spatial relationships, while retaining generalized
semantics with the original VFM features as anchors. The other projector is then constrained to be
decoupled from the contextual one, thereby reserving its representational capacity for scene structure.
To explicitly define this representation, we introduce a scene-structure discrimination objective via
contrastive learning. This objective organizes the feature space based on scene layout similarity, which
is measured by the histogram distribution of predicted depth. Driven by this objective, the second
projector learns transferable structural representations that capture global scene layout and perspective
geometry, rather than dataset-specific biases. Together, these two complementary representations
enable more reliable depth predictions while reducing their reliance on dataset-specific biases.
Extensive experiments demonstrate that MaSS outperforms previous state-of-the-art (SoTA) self-
supervised methods by a large margin (as presented in Fig. 1(a)) and achieves accuracy comparable to
certain supervised depth foundation models. Moreover, MaSS represents the first self-supervised ap-
proach to achieve clear performance gains from increasing the diversity of training data (Fig. 1(b)), re-
sulting in notable generalizability improvements particularly on out-of-distribution scenes (Fig. 1(c)).

typically trained on single domains with limited-scale datasets and rely on task-specific network

have taken initial steps toward scaling SSMDE training, while the others [9, 27, 5] demonstrate
that developing DFMs in a self-supervised fashion requires not only harnessing the large pretrained
models’ capacity, but also addressing challenges unique to self-supervised learning. Among them,
cross-domain learning conflicts under mixed-domain training pose a fundamental yet underexplored
obstacle to scalability, which this work primarily aims to address.
Three lines of research inform our approach. The first draws on pretrained knowledge to improve
depth representations. For example, the study [28] uses a pretrained semantic network to guide
the learning of geometric features. The studies [29, 30] further emphasize preserving transferable
pretrained representations during adaptation. The second focuses on organizing depth representations.
The prior arts [31, 32, 33, 27] have introduced contrastive objectives that structure the feature
space according to weather variations, color or geometric consistency, historical predictions, and
relationships guided by language. The third conditions depth prediction distributions on scene
characteristics to account for domain shifts. For example, the study [34] conditions these distributions
on scene categories. Drawing on these insights, MaSS explicitly decouples contextual and structural
representations, preserves contextual relationships derived from VFMs, and adaptively organizes
structural features according to similarities in scene layout. Depth prediction is then conditioned on
the resulting structural representations to reduce learning conflicts across domains.
3
Methodology
SSMDE models are prone to learning dataset-specific inductive biases and struggle to generalize
across varying depth distributions during both training and inference. For example, Fig. 1(c) presents
depth predictions on out-of-distribution indoor and outdoor scenes from two representative self-
supervised depth models [3, 4], trained on the NYUv2 [35] and KITTI [36] datasets, respectively. It
can be observed that the model trained on driving scenes tends to predict depth with a vanishing point,
while the model trained on small rooms fails to capture the more uniform depth distributions in large
halls. To find the source of such inductive biases, we investigate the internal feature representations.
As shown in Fig. 2(a), the DepthNet encoder [4] trained on driving data produces feature activation
maps with a consistent low-activation region. This pattern closely resembles the biased depth
predictions in Fig. 1(c) and fails to reflect variations aligned with the actual scene geometry. These
results indicate that the dataset-specific inductive biases are already embedded in the intermediate
representations. The above findings inspire us with a possible solution to make self-supervised
learning scalable: explicitly guide the network to learn robust, generalized structural representations
that are discriminative of scene layout before decoding depth.

3.1
MaSS Model
The first restriction of existing approaches towards this goal is from the model architecture design.
Although the pretrained VFM (e.g., DINOv2 [22]) exhibits strong generalization and is widely adopted
as initialization for supervised depth foundation models [24, 37, 38], its adaptation is unreliable
under self-supervision due to noisy training signals [9]. Direct fine-tuning may deteriorate the
pretrained representations [39], yet keeping the VFM frozen leaves its semantic features containing
less sufficient geometric cues for structural modeling [8] (as seen in Fig. 2(b)). This dilemma
motivates a bidirectional interaction architecture between a trainable traditional depth encoder and
a frozen VFM encoder, drawing on the ViT-Adapter design [11]. Another key observation is that
different viewpoints of the same scene, or different scenes containing similar objects, may appear
contextually alike yet differ substantially in spatial structure. Conversely, visually distinct scenes can
share similar structural layouts. Such misalignment suggests that entangling contextual and structural
features within a single representation pathway may lead to representational conflict and collapse.
To address this issue, we decouple contextual and structural representations using two specialized
feature projectors.

3.2
MaSS Learning Objective
Existing self-supervision signals mainly penalize local photometric inconsistencies [4, 9], which
predominantly supervise contextual features and fail to guide the learning of global scene structure.
Consequently, an additional objective is required to formulate structural representation learning. How-
ever, directly supervising structural representations is infeasible, as the mapping between intermediate
feature distributions and the underlying depth distribution is not analytically tractable. We find an
alternative solution drawing inspiration from contrastive learning [42], which organizes the feature
space through relational supervision rather than explicit labels. Unlike prior works [27, 31, 33]
that construct contrastive pairs in a generic instance-level manner [42], we reformulate a dedicated
scene-structure discrimination task, where positive and negative pairs are explicitly defined based on
a proposed structural discrepancy metric.

3.3
Depth Prediction from Learned Representations
The contextual and structural representations learned by MaSS are visualized in Fig. 2(c)(d). The
contextual pathway effectively adapts the VFM representations into depth-related context, while the
structural pathway captures generalized scene geometry. Their corresponding feature pyramids are
then concatenated along the channel dimension and passed through a 1 × 1 convolution for dimension
reduction, and finally fed into the depth decoder.

Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setups
Implementation details. The MaSS model utilizes the pre-trained DINOv2 ViT-B/14 architecture
[22] as its VFM encoder and employs the depth encoder [19]. The interaction module follows
the design of ViT-Adapter [11]. During training, the DINOv2 encoder remains frozen while all
other parameters are updated. The MaSS model can be trained on a single RTX 4090 GPU with a
batch size of 6, which takes approximately 2.5 days to complete. Data augmentation strategy and
optimizer settings adhere to the previous studies [1, 19, 4] without additional tuning. The proposed
scene-structure discrimination objective operates on the depth predictions to formulate supervision for
representation learning. Consequently, it is infeasible to train the depth model from scratch with this
objective, as unreliable early predictions would adversely affect representation learning. Therefore,
an additional initialization stage for one epoch is required to obtain reasonable depth estimates before
applying this objective. During training, we follow the prior work [3] to handle dynamic scenes and
extreme conditions that may compromise training stability [45, 5].
Datasets. Experiments are conducted on a total of 13 public datasets. The KITTI [36], NYUv2
[35], DDAD [46], and nuScenes [47] datasets are used for both training and evaluation, covering
outdoor, indoor, dynamic, and adverse weather scenarios. Eight additional datasets are used to
evaluate the generalizability of MaSS to unseen data, including three driving datasets, Waymo Open
[48], DrivingStereo [49], and Cityscapes [50]; three indoor datasets, iBims-1 [51], SUN-RGBD [52],
and 7-Scenes [53]; and two out-of-distribution datasets, DIML [54] and DIODE [55]. Finally, the

learning to discriminate scene structure, demonstrating the effectiveness of scalable self-supervised
depth learning. Furthermore, MaSS† outperforms the KBR series [6, 7] while requiring less overhead
and significantly less training data, highlighting that our method delivers substantial gains through
improved learning scalability rather than merely increasing data scale. Finally, MaSS† achieves
competitive performance compared to supervised depth foundation models on several outdoor and

indoor ground-truth data (e.g., Hypersim [60] and NYUv2). This analysis is supported by results
on DIODE and DIML, where the performance gap becomes much smaller on indoor scenes with
broader depth ranges and on outdoor scenes that exhibit indoor-style depth distributions.

the originally reported results of the KBR series[6, 7], stem from differences in alignment between
Median and SSI*. Additional results and analysis of this issue are provided in the Supplementary
Material.

object boundaries and finer-grained details (row 3). It also remains robust in complex environments
and preserves more accurate scene geometry in these unseen scenarios (rows 1, 2, 4), benefiting from
the explicitly learned structural representation.
4.4
Ablation Study
Efficacy of each innovative component. To verify that the three key components of MaSS for
learning generalizable structural representations are all indispensable, we conduct ablation studies
in Table 4(a). Removing (a1) the contrastive learning objective eliminates explicit supervision for
structural representation learning, leading to a significant performance drop, particularly on zero-shot
datasets. Removing (a2) the specialized structural pathway for representation decoupling prevents
the model from preserving contextual information while learning structure, resulting in consistent
degradation across all datasets. Removing (a3) the interaction architecture forces the model to learn
structural representations directly from DINOv2 features, which lack sufficient geometric cues, and
thus causes a substantial performance decline.

generalizable semantic priors and are not directly responsible for depth decoding. This naturally
raises a follow-up question: since pretrained depth or geometry foundation models encode rich
geometric priors, can MaSS benefit from them, or even further enhance such models? We investigate
this question in the Supplementary Material.
4.5
Scalability Analyses
To further evaluate the scalability of MaSS as training data grow in size and diversity, 50K additional
samples from five representative scenes in the TartanAir dataset: carwelding, hospital, neighborhood,
office, and seasonforest. This expands the training set from 150K to 200K samples and introduces a
broader range of scene types. As shown in Table 5, the average performance changes in Abs Rel and
δ1 are +1.5% and +0.2% on training domains, −0.3% and −0.2% on unseen domains similar to the
training domains, and +4.3% and +3.0% on more challenging out-of-distribution domains (DIML
and DIODE datasets). These results suggest that MaSS benefits from larger and more diverse training
data, with performance on the original training domains remaining largely stable and generalization
improving significantly on the most challenging unseen domains. Notably, these results provide
only preliminary evidence of the scalability of MaSS, as our evaluation remains limited to a training
scale far smaller than those used by recent supervised depth foundation models, such as the Depth
Anything series [24, 37, 61] and MoGe [62]. How SSMDE approaches would perform when scaled
to such data regimes remains largely unexplored and is left for future research.
5
Conclusion
This study revisited self-supervised depth estimation from the perspective of large-scale, mix-domain
training and prediction, recognizing that the model’s tendency to capture dataset-specific structural
bias is the key barrier. MaSS addressed this by coupling a depth encoder with a pretrained VFM
and explicitly decoupling the structural representations from the contextual ones to learn scene-
generalized structural representations via contrastive learning. MaSS achieved SoTA accuracy and
remarkable out-of-distribution generalization, demonstrating improved scalability with larger and
more diverse data.

A.1
Details of Learning to Discriminate Scene Structures
In the main paper, we define a structural discrepancy metric to determine positive and negative
samples within a training batch, thereby formulating a scene-structure discrimination objective for
learning structural representations. To illustrate how the metric functions and how the objective guides
representation learning, we present several quantitative examples in Fig. S1. The key observations
from Fig. S1 can be summarized as follows:
(1) Our method does not trivially treat each sample as the sole positive instance, nor does it de-
generate into discriminating samples based on coarse scene categories (e.g., indoor vs. outdoor).
As shown in Fig. S1(a), Sample ①is an outdoor scene but is not considered positive relative to
other outdoor samples; this is because its structural layout—a narrow passage flanked by cars and
buildings—differs from more open outdoor environments. Fig. S1(b) provides an inverse case, where
a wide-open outdoor scene (Sample ①) is correctly distinguished from narrow outdoor passages.
These observations are further supplemented by Sample ⑥in Fig. S1(c). Moreover, our method
captures shared structural properties across scene categories. For instance, in Fig. S1(d), the outdoor
scene in Sample ⑥forms positive pairs with multiple indoor samples due to their similar structure.
Similarly, in Fig. S1(e), the indoor library aisle (Sample ①) is identified as positive relative to outdoor
samples ③, ⑤, and ⑥, which share the “narrow corridor” structure. Meanwhile, it remains negative
to the room scene of Sample ②. These results demonstrate that our model prioritizes underlying
structural similarity over nominal scene labels.
(2) The hyperparameters of the proposed structural discrepancy metric, together with the masking
strategy, effectively identify meaningful contrastive relationships. As visualized in the Discrepancy
Metric and Pos./Neg. Mask columns, the resulting similarity maps align well with the expected
structural relationships described in the above analysis. Meanwhile, the threshold used in our imple-
mentation effectively determines the positive and negative sample pairs accordingly.
(3) Finally, within the MaSS framework, the specialized pathway effectively learns structural repre-
sentations that are discriminative with respect to different scene structures. This is evidenced by the
strong consistency between the feature similarities of the learned structural representations and the
in-batch structural discrepancy relationships computed from the predicted depth maps.

A.5
Data Distribution and Model Generalizability Analyses
We provide a quantitative analysis of the data distributions across all datasets used in this work to
better illustrate how our approach improves the scalability of self-supervised depth learning and
enhances model generalizability. As shown in Fig. S2, the training datasets (KITTI [36], DDAD [46],
and NYUv2 [35]) exhibit distinct depth distributions. These discrepancies arise from differences
between indoor and outdoor environments, as well as variations in field of view (FoV) and sensor
configurations during data acquisition. In addition, Fig. S3 presents the RGB distributions of the
datasets. Since monocular depth estimation maps RGB inputs to depth outputs, diversity in both
domains is crucial for robust generalization. Notably, while nuScenes shares a relatively similar depth
distribution with KITTI, its RGB distribution differs significantly.
These observations help interpret the zero-shot evaluation results. For the zero-shot outdoor datasets
(Waymo Open [48], DrivingStereo [49], and Cityscapes [50]), their depth distributions are similar
to those of the driving datasets used for training, whereas their RGB distributions differ noticeably.
This suggests that the limited generalization ability of previous methods is not primarily caused by
differences in depth ranges. Instead, the performance degradation is largely attributed to appearance
shifts, revealing the limited robustness of conventional encoder architectures with restricted pretrain-
ing. In contrast, our method learns robust and transferable contextual and structural representations
from diverse appearance distributions, substantially improving generalization in depth estimation.
For the zero-shot indoor datasets (iBims-1 [51], SUN-RGBD [52], and 7-Scenes [53]), both the
depth and RGB distributions closely align with the NYUv2 training set. This relatively homogeneous
distribution favors supervised depth foundation models, as similar scene statistics are already well

methods. In most SSMDE studies, median alignment is a widely adopted and well-justified evaluation
protocol. This is because SSMDE inherently learns scale-invariant depth that capture scene geometry
without requiring additional shift fitting, whereas LSQ∗alignment changes the evaluation objective
and no longer faithfully reflects this capability. Therefore, in the main text, we report the results of all

with different invariance properties, such as DA v2 and our method, may not be fully well-defined,
even when evaluated under the same LSQ* alignment protocol. This is because DA v2 is explicitly
trained to predict scale- and shift-invariant inverse depth, making it inherently compatible with this
alignment formulation during both training and inference. As discussed in their original paper, this
design facilitates cross-domain depth learning. In contrast, self-supervised methods do not possess
such an inherent advantage.

B.3
Generalizability Evaluation on In-the-Wild Data.
While the previously discussed OOD datasets demonstrate the strong generalization capability of our
model, their diversity and domain shift relative to the training data remain limited. To further evaluate
robustness, we collect a set of in-the-wild images from online repositories and mobile photography,
covering a wide range of real-world challenges.
As illustrated in Fig. S4, the first three rows (a–c) present unstructured natural scenes. Notably, (a)
and (c) contain specular reflections on water surfaces, while (b) includes dense vegetation with high-
frequency textures. Rows (d–f) exhibit complex geometries and structures that differ significantly
from standard urban or indoor benchmarks, such as the detailed roof tiles in (e) and the repetitive
grid structure in (f). Row (g) includes power lines to evaluate the model’s ability to estimate thin
structures. Rows (h–j) further evaluate model robustness under extreme lighting and motion blur
conditions. In particular, row (j) presents a challenging case with strong visual ambiguity at a road
junction caused by shadows and lighting effects.
Qualitative comparisons are conducted among SC-DepthV3 [3] (trained on NYUv2), SC-DepthV3†,
Jasmine [9] (trained on KITTI), and MaSS†. As shown in Fig. S4, SC-DepthV3 performs unsatis-
factorily on complex and unseen structures and tends to interpret scenes with an indoor bias. When
trained under the mixed-dataset setting, SC-DepthV3† further deteriorates, suffering from severe
structural confusion caused by the diverse training data. Jasmine represents one of the strongest
self-supervised generative depth models. Although it produces detailed predictions and exhibits
improved generalization due to diffusion priors, extreme RGB domain shifts can cause the diffusion
process to become unstable and produce depth predictions with significant errors. In contrast, our
method remains robust across these challenging in-the-wild scenes, producing depth predictions with
both fine-grained details and consistent structural layouts.
B.4
Geometric Fidelity Evaluation on In-the-Wild Data.
Since ground-truth depth is unavailable for these in-the-wild images, quantitative evaluation is
infeasible. Instead, we assess the geometric correctness of the predicted depth through monocular 3D
reconstruction.
We compare our method with two representative monocular reconstruction approaches, VGGT [38]
and MoGe [62]. To evaluate geometric fidelity, we synthesize novel viewpoints with slight viewpoint
changes relative to the original image. As shown in Fig. S5, VGGT produces relatively noisy depth
maps on these in-the-wild scenes, leading to noticeable artifacts in the reconstructed geometry. MoGe
represents one of the most robust monocular reconstruction foundation models, benefiting from
strong geometric supervision during training, and achieves high-quality reconstructions in most cases,
with only minor local inconsistencies observed in (b)-(d). Our method, benefiting from the inherent
geometric constraints of self-supervised learning as well as effective scaling with diverse training
data, achieves reconstruction results comparable to these prior approaches, with significantly less
training data. These results highlight the potential of self-supervised methods for building versatile
“DepthAnything”-style models from ubiquitous unlabeled data.

C
Discussion and Future Work
In addition to increasing the training data scale, as discussed in Sect. 4.5, the scalability of SSMDE
also depends on the availability of diverse and suitable training data. Although self supervision
enables learning from imagery collected in the wild, systematically curated datasets covering diverse
scenes remain scarce. Existing methods therefore still rely heavily on commonly used datasets
originally collected for supervised learning. Constraints imposed by data collection platforms
(e.g., vehicles, handheld devices, or UAVs), together with the high cost of sensor calibration and
annotation, often limit these datasets to relatively homogeneous scenes, such as typical indoor rooms
and common driving environments. This limited diversity may constrain the potential benefits of
self-supervision. Such limited diversity may even reduce the potential advantages of self-supervised
learning. Systematically curating large and diverse datasets from imagery collected in the wild is
