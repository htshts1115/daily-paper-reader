Abstract
Single-image refocusing changes the focal plane and depth
of field of a photograph after capture. It is inherently in-
teractive: users try many focal points and blur strengths
on one image, so each edit should be fast and change only
the blur. Recent generative methods, however, rerun large
diffusion models for every edit. We observe that the two
sub-problems differ in kind: recovering the all-in-focus im-
age is ill-posed and needs a generative prior, whereas ren-
dering blur is largely determined by depth and the focal
plane. We therefore propose FlashReFocus, which runs dif-
fusion once per photograph and renders every edit with a
lightweight network. To address the ill-posed restoration,
a one-step diffusion deblurrer with a decoder refiner recov-
ers fine detail efficiently while balancing fidelity and per-
ceptual quality. For precise control, the renderer is con-
ditioned on an explicit defocus map and uses defocus-bias
attention, which lets pixels at similar defocus share infor-
mation without interference from pixels at other depths; in-
focus content stays unchanged, and editing the map yields
tilt-shift and two focal planes. To further test generaliza-
tion to unseen cameras, we collect 3CReal, a three-camera
benchmark of 89 pairs.
FlashReFocus achieves the best
perceptual fidelity for zero-shot bokeh rendering on EBB!
and 3CReal, refocuses 148.3× faster than GenRefocus, and
takes about half a second per further edit, 462× faster.
1. Introduction
Depth of field determines which parts of a photograph ap-
pear sharp and is fixed at capture.
Smartphone portrait
modes move the focus afterwards only by blurring an al-
ready sharp image with depth recorded at capture [61], so
out-of-focus content stays blurred. Single-image refocusing
lifts this limit by recovering an all-in-focus (AiF) image and
re-rendering blur around a new focal plane [41].

and blur strengths on one photograph. Its two sub-problems

This asymmetry suggests three principles for interactive
refocusing, all followed by FlashReFocus (Fig. 2). (i) Gen-
erate once, render many.
A session of N edits costs
Crestore + N Crender, so the generative step belongs outside
the editing loop: Stage I restores the AiF image once with
one-step diffusion [57], and each edit runs only a 1.3M-
parameter renderer. (ii) Edit only the blur. Changing the
focus should leave in-focus content untouched, so Stage II
adds a residual to the fixed AiF image; across edits, in-focus
regions stay at 44.4 dB PSNR against 29.0 dB for GenRefo-
cus. (iii) Control through optics. The renderer is condi-
tioned on an explicit defocus map whose product with the
strength mirrors the CoC, so the strength acts as an aper-
ture and editing the map yields effects beyond a single lens,
such as tilt-shift and two focal planes. Concretely, Stage I
blends a pixel-fidelity and a detail LoRA with one weight ω
to trade fidelity for detail, learns from mixed real and syn-
thetic blur, and adds a decoder refiner that keeps text and
edges VAE decoding distorts; Stage II’s defocus-bias atten-
tion lets pixels at similar defocus share information, so blur
changes sharply at depth boundaries without interference
from unrelated, possibly salient pixels at other depths. The
costly prior thus runs once, where detail must be invented,
and each edit runs a small network on a task that optics con-
strain, which makes FlashReFocus both fast and accurate.
Rendering also needs data with diverse focal settings:
real pairs [22, 50] capture lens-specific bokeh at few focal
planes, and synthetic pairs [38] any focal plane with simpli-
fied optics, so we pre-train on synthetic multi-focal scenes
and adapt to real pairs. Both are single-camera datasets, so
we introduce 3CReal, 89 pairs from three unseen cameras,
to test cross-camera generalization. Our contributions are:
• For interactive refocusing, we propose a restore-once,
render-many framework: diffusion runs once per photo-
graph, and each edit runs only a lightweight renderer at
interactive speed, leaving in-focus content unchanged.
• To address the ill-posedness of defocus deblurring, we
adapt one-step diffusion with a VAE-decoder refiner,
which recovers detail efficiently while balancing fidelity
and perceptual quality in a simple, unified design.
• For precise control without complex designs, we craft
the renderer’s defocus-bias attention and explicit defocus
map, which set focus, strength, and depth edits directly.
• To further test generalization to unseen cameras, we col-
lect 3CReal; on it and EBB!, FlashReFocus leads in per-
ceptual fidelity, far faster than generative rivals.
2. Related Work
Image restoration and defocus deblurring.
Convolu-
tional and Transformer restorers optimize reconstruction
losses [10, 30, 73] and achieve high PSNR and SSIM,
yet the perception–distortion trade-off [7] motivates gener-
ative priors for perceptual quality. Unlike motion or uni-
form blur, defocus blur has a point-spread function that
varies with scene depth and focal distance [6, 74]. Classi-
cal methods estimate a spatially varying kernel and decon-
volve [26, 76, 79], while learned models predict kernels or
adaptive filters or exploit dual-pixel cues [1, 2, 25, 42, 56],
train on light-field pairs [48], or model image formation ex-
plicitly [77].
More recently, diffusion models provide a learned prior
over natural images for ill-posed inverse problems [18, 46].

Restoration methods condition on the degraded image, pre-
dict residuals, or fuse latent priors [13, 33, 69], and recent
defocus methods combine diffusion with Transformer fea-
tures or fidelity-aware guidance [9, 27–29]. However, diffu-
sion priors can hallucinate texture [4] and VAE compression
loses fine structures; these issues motivate our detail LoRA
and decoder refiner.
Bokeh rendering and refocusing. Classical renderers con-
volve depth-dependent kernels, trace rays, or scatter in im-
age space [24, 40, 41, 70], and layered or light-field in-
puts add scene information [37, 61]. Learned renderers in-
stead fit camera-specific appearance from data: they pre-
dict depth from one image [63], learn DSLR blur from
EBB! pairs [22], refine analytical scattering [38], render in-
painted multiplane images [39], add a strength control [50],
or model occlusion [52]. More recently, BokehDiff, Mag-
icBokeh, and DiffCamera bring diffusion to bokeh synthe-
sis [54, 64, 78], including depth-free rendering [53], and
Any-to-Bokeh extends one-step diffusion bokeh to video
with multiplane conditioning [71], while Bokeh Diffusion
controls the blur level of text-to-image generation but not
the focal plane [15]. Across these methods, control signals
range from scalar strength to masks, depth maps, and mul-
tiplane images; in contrast, ours is a depth-derived signed
defocus map with a global strength.
Refocusing further combines restoration with rendering.
RefocusGAN estimates an AiF radiance image before ap-
plying focal blur [49]; DC2 uses a dual-camera rig [5]; and
GenRefocus trains two diffusion transformers, DeblurNet
and BokehNet, for the two operations with semi-supervised
learning [36]. Other methods avoid an explicit AiF image:
Learning to Refocus generates a focal stack with video dif-
fusion [58], and AnyBokeh (not the video Any-to-Bokeh)
edits photographs that already contain bokeh [19]. Con-
versely, EXIF-conditioned text-to-image generation uses
the same AiF, depth, and lens-blur decomposition [55].
FlashReFocus likewise applies it to real photographs but re-
places generative rendering with a lightweight feed-forward
renderer that reuses the AiF image across edits.

the selected focal plane. Around this attention, the encoder–
transformer–decoder uses NAFNet blocks [10], with atten-

with image features and fuses them through a depthwise
separable convolution. Finally, the network predicts a resid-
ual, ˆIbokeh = ˆIAiF + ∆I, which stays near zero in focus and
so keeps in-focus regions intact.

4. Experiments
Datasets and baselines.
We evaluate deblurring on
DPDD [1] and RealDOF [25], and bokeh rendering on
EBB! Val176 [22] and 3CReal. 3CReal is our new bench-
mark of 89 aperture pairs from three camera systems unseen
in training. We compare with seven deblurring and nine
bokeh-rendering methods (Tables 1 and 3).
Evaluation protocol. Baselines use released weights or of-
ficial retraining (Restormer on DPDD). Deblurring is eval-
uated on aligned DPDD regions and RealDOF outdoor
scenes. For bokeh rendering, all methods receive the same
AiF input and focal point. As strength scales differ, each
method renders a range of strengths and is scored on its out-
put with the highest SSIM to the target. Methods that can-
not take a focal point use their official focus strategy (#).
Stage-I settings (the refiner and ω) are chosen by the lowest
rank sum over PSNR, LPIPS, DISTS, and FID on valida-
tion data used neither for training nor in Table 1: the DPDD
validation split, 50 LFDOF test scenes (reused in App. H;
in-domain for Stage I), and 60 RealBokeh validation pairs
at f/2.0.
Training details. Stage I adapts Stable Diffusion 2.1 [46]
following PiSA-SR, starting from its released LoRAs, on
two NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 GPUs with effective batch
size 16, learning rate 5×10−5, and 500 warm-up iterations.
The pixel phase and the detail phase each run 20K itera-
tions, with λdet
LPIPS = 2, λCSD = 1, and CSD guidance 7.5;
the refiner is then fine-tuned for 15K iterations on Stage-I
latents. Inference processes the full image without tiling in
a single step with ω = 1.25.
Stage II, in contrast, is trained on a single RTX 4090.
Synthetic pre-training uses three Hypersim epochs, batch
size 4, and learning rate 5×10−4. Five RealBokeh adap-
tation epochs follow, at 5×10−4 for the defocus-attention
parameters and 5×10−5 for the rest of the network. Both
stages use 512×512 crops, random scale, flip, and right-
angle rotation. Runtime and memory are measured after
warm-up.
Metrics.
For deblurring, we report PSNR, SSIM [66],
LPIPS [75], DISTS [11], the no-reference NIQE [35],
MUSIQ [23], MANIQA [68], BRISQUE [34], and CLIP-
IQA [62], and FID [16]. For bokeh rendering, we add MS-
SSIM [65], LPIPS with VGG features, CLIP-I [44], and LV-
Corr [15], the correlation between the strength setting and
the rendered blur.

4.3. Complete Refocusing and Efficiency
Cost of one refocus. As reported in Table 6, a complete re-
focus at 1536 × 1536 takes 2.50 s and 16.94 GB of peak
memory, 54.5× and 148.3× faster than DiffCamera and
GenRefocus with 161× and 350× fewer FLOPs. Subse-
quent edits reuse ˆIAiF and its depth and run only the ren-
derer, 0.47 s each; the one-step MagicBokeh needs 6.13 s
and 133× more FLOPs per edit, and AnyBokeh 183.38 s af-
ter 8.00 s of defocus-map estimation. Meanwhile, in-focus
regions barely change across edits, at 44.4 dB PSNR to the
restored image on 3CReal against 29.0 dB for GenRefocus.
When the focus moves to a defocused background (Fig. 9),
FlashReFocus restores it and blurs the former focus: Dif-
fCamera and MagicBokeh leave the cliff blurred, GenRe-
focus keeps the horse sharp, and AnyBokeh recovers less
rock structure. With light-field ground truth, it leads the
best baseline by 2.16 dB PSNR (App. H).

5. Conclusion
We presented FlashReFocus, an interactive refocusing
framework that runs diffusion once per photograph rather
than once per edit. One-step Stage I restores an all-in-focus
image, and a lightweight renderer driven by an explicit de-
focus map then renders each edit in about half a second
while leaving in-focus content unchanged. Beyond speed,
FlashReFocus achieves the best perceptual bokeh fidelity
on EBB! Val176 and on the unseen cameras of 3CReal, and
its defocus map further enables aperture, tilt-shift, and two-
plane control for practical, user-driven photo editing.
Limitations. Blur follows monocular depth, so depth errors
misplace it, and occluded background cannot be revealed.
Distilling Stage I and video refocusing are future work.

B. Restoration Implementation Details
B.1. Conditioning and Data Preparation
Stage I follows PiSA-SR [57] on Stable Diffusion 2.1 [46].
At inference the U-Net runs at timestep 1 with an empty
prompt and the VAE encoder uses its posterior mode, so
an input always yields the same all-in-focus image, which
every later edit reuses. Note that the detail weight ω of
Eq. 5 blends the two noise predictions rather than the LoRA
weights: the detail LoRA is trained on top of the frozen
pixel LoRA inside a nonlinear U-Net, so scaling its weights
by ω would not reproduce the blend, and ω̸ = 1 takes two
U-Net evaluations at the single timestep, a one-time cost
per photograph. Within the network, LoRAs adapt the con-
volution, attention, and feed-forward projections of the U-
Net and the convolution and attention layers of the VAE
encoder.
Training starts from the released PiSA-SR Lo-
RAs: the rank-4 pixel LoRA is embedded exactly into the
first ranks of the rank-16 one, and every rank keeps the re-
leased scaling. For distillation, CSD [72] uses the frozen
model as teacher with guidance scale 7.5 and timesteps
from 20 to 500. Its prompt is formed from the 50 highest-
scoring RAM++ tags [21] of the sharp target, and its nega-
tive prompt adds bokeh, shallow depth of field, out of focus,
and defocus blur to the official one; in addition, half of the
training samples give the student an empty prompt. With
ω̸ = 1, the adjustable mode of PiSA-SR computes ϵpix and
ϵpix+det with two U-Net passes.

with 1.30M parameters. Two 3 × 3 convolutions embed
the mean-subtracted ˆIAiF, MC, and a strength map into

E. Experiment Settings
Baselines. Baselines use released weights and official in-
ference settings, except Swintormer [9] and Restormer,
which are retrained on DPDD with their official code and
settings. Unless otherwise specified, deblurring outputs are
evaluated at the reference resolution.
Bokeh rendering. Focal points are manually annotated for
EBB! Val176, 3CReal, and RealBokeh and shared across
methods that support this input; auxiliary depth, dispar-
ity, or masks follow official inference settings. However,
strength scales differ across methods, so each method ren-
ders a fixed set of its own strengths and the output with the
highest SSIM to the target is evaluated. For instance, Any-
Bokeh [19] runs its official inference (Stage 1 on the whole
image at 512 pixels, Stage 2 on overlapping 512-pixel tiles
of the full-resolution image, 20 steps, guidance 30), and its
strength is the bokeh scale of its optical fingerprint transfer,
the ratio Nsrc/Ntgt of input and target f-numbers. The scale
starts at the EXIF ratio (f/16 to f/1.8 on EBB!; per pair on
3CReal, and f/16 to f/2.0 for the eight X100VI pairs with-
out EXIF) and is multiplied or divided by 21/4 while SSIM
improves. Tuning matters for this method: with the EXIF
ratio alone, as in its paper, AnyBokeh reaches 22.27 dB
PSNR and 0.2692 LPIPS on EBB! Val176 and 24.08 dB
and 0.2796 on 3CReal, against 22.81 dB and 0.2291, and
24.74 dB and 0.2298, after tuning. For the other methods,
Table E.1 lists each method’s strength parameter and search;
all methods run at native resolution.

NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 GPU under each method’s of-
ficial inference settings, including patch- or tile-based in-

method loads, and FLOPs follow PyTorch’s FLOP counter
(one multiply-add equals two FLOPs; element-wise opera-
tions excluded), and for diffusion-based methods, the cost
covers denoising and VAE encoding and decoding. Deblur-
ring time (Table 2) excludes disk I/O and is averaged over
repeated runs after warm-up. In Table 6, in turn, New focus

reuses it, and its memory is the joint peak of both stages.
Metrics. We use PSNR, SSIM [66], LPIPS [75], and
DISTS [11] for both stages.
Bokeh rendering and refo-
cusing additionally use MS-SSIM [65], LPIPS with VGG
features, and CLIP-I, the cosine similarity between CLIP
ViT-L/14 [44] image embeddings of output and reference,
following GenRefocus [36]. Beyond fidelity, blur-strength
controllability is measured by LVCorr [15] with the proto-
col of GenRefocus [36]: for each all-in-focus image and
a fixed focal point, using the 50 LFDOF [47] test scenes
and the 89 3CReal inputs resized to a long side of 1024,
a method renders ten strengths evenly spanning its own
range, and LVCorr is the Pearson correlation between the
strength and the rendered blur, measured as the negative
Laplacian variance, averaged over scenes; for GenRefocus
and AnyBokeh, whose ten renderings per scene each re-
quire full diffusion passes, we use the first 20 scenes of
each set, on which FlashReFocus reaches 0.879 (LFDOF)
and 0.792 (3CReal), against 0.690 and 0.666 for GenRe-
focus and 0.672 and 0.672 for AnyBokeh.

MANIQA [68], BRISQUE [34], and CLIPIQA [62] for
deblurring and by CLIPIQA, MUSIQ, and MANIQA for

Qualitative comparisons. Figures F.1 and F.2 add a
DPDD scene with rocks, grass, and leaves and a RealDOF
scene with dense structure. In both, natural textures become
sharper than with the deterministic baselines, with fewer
content changes than GenRefocus introduces, while small
characters and repeated man-made patterns remain suscep-
tible to incorrect reconstruction.

benchmarks, Figure F.6 deblurs shallow-depth-of-field pho-

Failure cases. Figure F.3 shows that heavily defocused
foreground regions may become over-smoothed or exhibit
ghosting when the input provides insufficient structure.
Conversely, textureless regions may produce web-like pat-
terns, because the diffusion prior can hallucinate weak high-
frequency content; the detail-weight study below shows
how this grows with ω.
Ablation. Table F.1 reports all metrics for the cumulative
deblurring ablation of the main paper.
Detail weight. Table F.2 varies ω on the validation sets.
PSNR peaks at ω = 0.5–0.75, whereas LPIPS, DISTS, and
FID improve up to ω = 1.25–1.5. Beyond this range, all
metrics degrade as the extrapolated detail branch halluci-

lowest rank sum.

injection recovers them; severely blurred characters remain

G. Additional Bokeh Results
RealBokeh validation. RealBokeh validation shares its
cameras and lenses with the RealBokeh training split, on
which both Bokehlicious and our renderer are trained, so
Table G.1 measures in-distribution rendering at the origi-
nal resolution. As expected, Bokehlicious, which is condi-
tioned on the target aperture, obtains the best full-reference
scores. FlashReFocus nevertheless follows on PSNR, MS-
SSIM, LPIPS, DISTS, and CLIP-I and has the second-best
MUSIQ, and on the ten pairs evaluated for GenRefocus, it is
better on every metric (PSNR 25.46 vs. 23.38 dB, MUSIQ
47.61 vs. 38.79).
Real apertures. LVCorr measures whether blur grows
with the strength setting, but not whether the selected
strength follows a real aperture. We test the latter on MOD-
EST, where the strength selected for FlashReFocus rises
with the aperture in 16 of the 17 aperture series, with the
highest rank correlation to 1/N (ρ = 0.985, Table G.2), on
par with the physically based BokehMe (0.979) and above
the diffusion-based MagicBokeh (0.903), whose smallest
setting already blurs more than f/16. Moreover, its SSIM
matches BokehMe at small apertures and is the highest at
f/5 and f/2.8. However, the strength of FlashReFocus is
monotonic but not linear in 1/N (Fig. G.1), unlike the blur-
radius parameters of BokehMe and DeepLens, so mapping
f-numbers to strengths needs a per-lens calibration curve
rather than a single scale.

H. Refocusing and Editing
Complete refocusing on LFDOF. Unlike paired pho-
tographs, light fields give exact refocusing ground truth.
Following the LF-Refocus protocol of GenRefocus [36], we
take the 50 LFDOF test scenes [47] at the largest aperture
and, for each, use one defocused focal slice as input and
another slice of the same light field as target, with a focal
point annotated where the target is sharp. Every method
then refocuses the input at that point, and its strength (or
blur level) is searched per image for the highest SSIM to
the target. Table H.1 shows that FlashReFocus is best on
all full-reference metrics, and Figs. H.1 and H.6 show four
scenes, while the ablations show that both stages contribute.
Two caveats apply, however: Stage I is trained on LFDOF
training scenes, so its input domain is familiar, and these
50 test scenes are in the validation data used to select the
refiner and ω (Sec. 4), although the renderer and the evalu-
ation targets were not used for any choice.

Consistency across focal edits. Interactive refocusing
moves the focal point many times on the same image, so
each edit should change the blur and nothing else. We place
eight focal points at depth quantiles from far to near over
three 3CReal and three RealBokeh test inputs at a long side
of 1024, and compare with GenRefocus [36] on two mea-
sures. In-focus fidelity is the PSNR and LPIPS between a
result and the all-in-focus input over pixels whose depth
lies within 0.04 of the focal depth.
Edit consistency, in
turn, is the PSNR between consecutive results over pixels
whose defocus does not change between the two settings,
i.e. whose distances to the two focal depths differ by less
than 0.02. Both measures use the depth of our renderer.
For a fair comparison, the two methods’ out-of-focus
blur is equalized, measured as the mean absolute Laplacian
over pixels more than 0.25 from the focal depth relative to
the input, in two settings. In setting (i), GenRefocus runs
at its default blur parameter KG = 15 and FlashReFocus’s
strength K is matched to it, per focal setting for fidelity and
per scene for consistency; blur beyond the trained range is
obtained by extrapolating the strength up to 2 or applying
the renderer twice. Conversely, in setting (ii), FlashReFo-
cus runs at its default strength K = 0.5 and KG is set per
scene to the same blur (KG = 8.5–14.19 on 3CReal and
5.25–7.69 on RealBokeh).
In both settings FlashReFocus preserves in-focus regions
far better (Table H.2): 44.44 dB against 28.96 dB on 3CReal
in setting (i). This follows from the design: the residual ren-
derer only adds blur to the restored image, whereas Gen-
Refocus regenerates the whole image at every edit, so its
in-focus regions drift from the input and pixels whose blur
should not change still vary; between consecutive edits they
change 2.7–3.7 times more than with FlashReFocus. Each
edit also takes 0.10 s against 77 s. Figure H.7 shows four

Two focal planes. A physical lens has one focal plane,
so two subjects at different depths cannot both be sharp
while the space between them is blurred. With an explicit
depth map, however, FlashReFocus obtains this without re-
training.
With the focal depths DA and DB of two se-
lected points, the renderer receives the edited depth D′ =
DA + min(|D −DA|, |D −DB|) and the focal depth DA,
so each pixel is blurred by its distance to the nearer plane.
Figure H.9 shows three scenes, the third from 3CReal. Mea-
sured as the mean absolute Laplacian relative to the input,
the two planes keep 84%/75%, 81%/82% and 81%/66% of
their sharpness with the edited depth, whereas focusing on
one plane leaves the other at 9–21%; regions far from both
planes keep 8–11%. Figure H.13 applies the same edit to
two portraits of Fig. H.11, keeping the face and a distant

whichever layers are away from it, raising the strength with
the focus on the face progressively blurs the can, and the
depth edit of Appendix H keeps the can and the face sharp
while the lake stays blurred.
I. Limitations and Broader Impact
Depth. The renderer reads the relative depth of a monocu-
lar model, so its errors become blur errors; a misestimated
thin structure, glass surface, or reflection is blurred as if
it lay at the wrong depth. Replacing DA-V2-L by Depth
Pro or by smaller DA-V2 models changes PSNR by at most
0.11 dB on the benchmarks (Table G.4), but these averages
do not bound the error on a single image with such content.
Occlusion. A real large aperture sees slightly around a
foreground edge, so the background shows through its
blurred rim. FlashReFocus, by contrast, renders from one
restored image that contains no occluded content, so it can
only spread visible colors and cannot recover the back-
ground hidden there.

Restoration. Stage I can add texture that the input does

tive refocusing methods, which synthesize new facial detail
(Fig. H.5, third row).
Cost. Restoration accounts for most of the 2.50 s first refo-

whereas each further edit runs only the 1.3M-parameter ren-
derer (0.47 s, 0.85 TFLOPs).
Video. FlashReFocus refocuses single images.
Applied
frame by frame, however, restoration and depth are es-
timated independently per frame, with no temporal con-
straint, so restored detail and blur can flicker; video refo-
cusing needs temporally consistent restoration and depth,
as targeted by video bokeh methods [71].
