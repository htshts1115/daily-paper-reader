Abstract. Few-shot semantic segmentation typically identifies target
objects based on positive support examples of a single semantic class.
In practice, however, positive cues alone are often underspecified: for
instance, “red cars” may be confused with cars of all colors, “vehicles”
may include cars that should be excluded, and “material-defined objects”
may span multiple categories. Fulfilling precise requests requires posi-
tive exemplars to provide inclusion evidence and negative exemplars to
act as intent-defining constraints for exclusion and disambiguation. We
formulate this setting as Specified Visual Prompt Segmentation
(SVPS), where the target concept is specified by both positive and
negative visual prompts. In this work, visual exemplars refer to support-
image regions specified by bounding-box prompts. To study SVPS, we
introduce CASE (Concept-Aware Segmentation from Positive and Neg-
ative Visual Exemplars), a benchmark built on COCO and PACO. CASE
encompasses six category- and attribute-level task families: exclusion,
isolation, union, attribute exclusion, attribute isolation, and attribute
union. We evaluate SAM 3 under this setting and find that, despite
its powerful positive-only few-shot segmentation capability, frozen SAM
3 does not consistently treat negative visual prompts as exclusion ev-
idence. To address this, we present Concept Prompt Adaptation
(CPA), a conservative LoRA-based adaptation baseline that improves
positive-negative prompt interpretation on CASE while remaining com-
petitive under positive-only few-shot segmentation. Code is available at
https://github.com/WongKinYiu/FSS-CASE.

Introduction
Semantic segmentation [3,15,38] is a fundamental task in computer vision that
aims to assign a semantic label to every pixel in an image. Although deep mod-
els have achieved remarkable performance on standard benchmarks, they rely
on large-scale datasets with dense, pixel-level annotations for training. Beyond

Few-shot semantic segmentation (FSS) [26, 28] addresses this limitation by
segmenting novel objects in a query image using only a small number of anno-
tated support examples. Existing FSS methods commonly adopt episodic train-
ing to learn transferable matching mechanisms between support and query im-
ages, such as prototype matching [26, 28], pixel-level correspondence [17, 20],
or cross-image attention [31,35]. Despite their success on standard FSS bench-
marks, these methods largely formulate the problem as category-level matching:
Namely, given one or more positive support examples, the model is expected to
segment all objects of the same semantic class in the query image.
However, this formulation is overly restrictive for practical visual prompt-
ing. In real-world scenarios, user specifications frequently require both inclusion
and exclusion cues, rather than purely positive category matching. For instance,
positive examples may overgeneralize from “red cars” to all cars, from “buses
and trains” to all vehicles (thereby incorrectly including cars), or from “wooden
chairs” to all chairs rather than wooden objects. These complex targets cannot
be precisely encapsulated by positive examples alone: While positive examples
define what to include, they do not explicitly specify what should be excluded.
Consequently, a model needs to jointly interpret positive and negative visual
prompts to infer the target mask from their relationship.
We define this task as Specified Visual Prompt Segmentation (SVPS),
where the target concept is specified by both positive and negative visual exem-
plars. In this work, visual exemplars are support-image regions represented as
bounding-box prompts. Positive exemplars provide visual evidence for inclusion,
whereas negative exemplars act as intent-defining constraints that specify con-
fusing categories, attributes, or instances to exclude. Unlike conventional FSS,

SVPS evaluates whether a model can infer a specified target concept from the
interplay between positive and negative exemplars. As illustrated in Fig. 1, this
changes the role of negative prompts from local mask refinement to concept-
level intent specification: different positive-negative exemplar configurations can
induce different target masks for the same query image.
To evaluate this task, we introduce CASE (Concept-Aware Segmentation
from Positive and Negative Visual Exemplars), a benchmark constructed from
COCO [13] and PACO [22] annotations. The CASE benchmark encompasses six
task families spanning category-level and attribute-level concept specification:
exclusion, isolation, and union. These tasks are designed to assess whether a
segmentation model can leverage negative visual prompts as concept-level exclu-
sion evidence, rather than simply appending them as additional prompts without
modeling their relation to the positive visual evidence.
We further study Segment Anything Model 3 (SAM 3) [1] under the SVPS
setting. SAM 3 is a strong vision foundation model for promptable concept seg-
mentation, supporting segmentation from text descriptions, visual exemplars,
or their combination. A recent shared-canvas formulation [27] has shown that
support and query images can be placed in a single spatial canvas, enabling a
fully frozen SAM 3 to perform few-shot segmentation without additional training
or architectural modification. This makes SAM 3 a suitable testbed for study-
ing whether promptable segmentation models can handle positive-negative visual
prompts beyond positive-only matching. Unlike general promptable concept seg-
mentation in SAM 3 [1], where different prompt types serve as alternative ways
to refer to a concept, SVPS evaluates whether positive and negative exemplars
can jointly specify a constrained target concept.
However, the same line of evidence also reveals a key limitation: directly
adding negative visual prompts to the few-shot segmentation pipeline does not
necessarily improve performance and may even suppress target objects [27]. This
behavior contradicts the intended role of negative prompts as exclusion cues. It
suggests that, although SAM 3 can effectively exploit positive visual prompts, it
does not inherently parse negative prompts as precise exclusion constraints. In
few-shot settings, negative prompts may instead compete with positive prompts
and weaken or suppress predicted target masks.
To address this issue, we propose Concept Prompt Adaptation (CPA), a
lightweight, LoRA-based adaptation strategy tailored for SAM 3 under positive-
negative visual prompting. We use the term concept to highlight that CPA adapts
the model to target concepts specified by positive-negative visual evidence. CPA
updates only low-rank adaptation parameters while keeping the pretrained model
frozen. Furthermore, we introduce positive-only preservation regularization that
keeps the adapted model close to frozen SAM 3 when negative prompts are ab-
sent. This provides a conservative yet effective adaptation baseline that balances
SVPS improvement on CASE while mitigating standard FSS degradation.
The primary contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
– We introduce SVPS, a new segmentation paradigm where positive and neg-
ative visual exemplars jointly specify a target concept.

Related Work
2.1
Few-Shot Semantic Segmentation
Few-shot semantic segmentation (FSS) [26,28] aims to segment novel categories
in a query image given only a small number of annotated support examples. Early
approaches commonly adopt episodic training to learn matching functions. A
prominent line of work represents support evidence with prototypes or prototype-
enhanced class summaries [7,12,26,28]. Although prototype-based methods are
efficient, compressing multiple support masks into a global vector discards fine-
grained spatial details crucial for precise localization.
To preserve richer support-query correspondences, subsequent methods ex-
plore pixel- or region-level matching through hypercorrelation [17], dense match-
ing [20], or cross-attention [31,35]. Despite effectiveness on FSS, these methods
work under the restrictive assumption that the support set provides solely posi-
tive evidence for a single semantic class. Hence, the task is reduced to category-
level matching, where the model merely transfers a positive support concept to
the query image. In contrast, our work studies a more expressive setting where
a target concept is jointly specified by positive and negative visual prompts.
2.2
Foundation Models for Promptable Segmentation
Vision foundation models such as CLIP [21] and DINO [2, 19] provide general-
purpose visual representations that have reshaped many recognition [37,40,41]
and segmentation [16,23,30,39] tasks. Building on this trend, the Segment Any-
thing Model (SAM) [11] demonstrates remarkable class-agnostic segmentation
capabilities through spatial prompts such as points and boxes. Several works
adapt SAM to FSS by generating visual reference prompts from support im-
ages [14,25,34]. While effective, these methods often introduce additional com-
ponents, such as visual reference encoders [25], graph-based prompt refinement
modules [34], or prompt-learning/adaptation modules [4, 9], to bridge the gap
between support examples and query segmentation.
SAM 2 [24] extends promptable segmentation to videos through a memory
mechanism for frame-to-frame mask propagation. Recent FSS methods based
on SAM 2, such as FSSAM [32] and SANSA [5], adapt this memory mechanism
by treating support images as memory references and query images as target
frames. However, these memory-based formulations are primarily designed for
temporal consistency and object tracking, making them suboptimal for semantic
correspondences across temporally disjoint images.

SAM 3 [1] introduces Promptable Concept Segmentation (PCS), where an
object concept can be specified by text prompts, positive or negative image ex-
emplars, or their combinations. Native prompting operates on the image being
segmented, whereas CASE requires cross-image transfer. A recent shared-canvas
pipeline [27] further enables frozen SAM 3 to perform training-free FSS by plac-
ing support and query images into a single canvas, but directly incorporating
negative prompts can degrade segmentation quality and suppress target objects.
Rather than treating negative exemplars as interactive refinements to an existing
concept prompt, SVPS studies whether positive and negative visual exemplars
can jointly define complex target concepts through inclusion and exclusion roles.
2.3
Visual Prompting and Negative Prompts
Prompt design is crucial to foundation segmentation models. Existing works
mainly improve positive guidance through visual reference prompts [25, 36],
support-query feature matching [14,34], learnable prompts or adaptation mod-
ules [4,9], and text- or image-guided prompting [6,16,29]. These mechanisms are
used to localize a target object or transfer a positive support concept to a query
image. Target concept ambiguity is commonly mitigated by strengthening posi-
tive evidence through refined localization [36], support-query matching [14,34],
or multimodal guidance [6,16,23,29].
Negative prompts remain less explored in exemplar-based FSS. While neg-
ative points or boxes are commonly expected to suppress unwanted regions in
user-interactive segmentation [11,24], this assumption may not transfer directly
to FSS, where negative exemplars define semantic exclusion constraints rather
than merely correct local mask errors. Recent SAM 3-based FSS results [27] sug-
gest that directly appending negative prompts reduces segmentation quality and
suppresses target objects. This indicates that current promptable segmentation
models fail to utilize negative visual prompts as exclusion evidence.
Beyond category-level segmentation, referring expression segmentation spec-
ifies targets through language descriptions involving attributes or context [6,10,
29,33], while datasets such as PACO [22] evaluate attribute-level visual under-
standing. Although language can describe constraints, subtle visual distinctions
are easier to specify through visual examples. Motivated by this gap, we define
Specified Visual Prompt Segmentation (SVPS), where positive and negative
visual prompts jointly specify a target concept rather than serving as auxil-
iary inputs. The proposed CASE (Concept-Aware Segmentation from Positive
and Negative Visual Exemplars) benchmark systematically evaluates whether
models can infer target concepts from positive-negative exemplar relationships,
covering exclusion, isolation, union, and attribute-level disambiguation.

3.2
Task Taxonomy
As summarized in Tab. 1, CASE encompasses six task families that evaluate dif-
ferent facets of positive-negative concept specification. The category-level tasks
comprise supercategory exclusion (SuperEx), fine-grained isolation within a su-
percategory (FineIso), and functional union across related categories (Union).
The attribute-level tasks include exclusion (AttrEx), isolation (AttrIso), and
cross-category union (AttrUnion). Together, these tasks assess whether a model
can move beyond naive positive matching and leverage negative exemplars as
exclusion or boundary constraints for precise target concept specification.
3.3
Episode Construction
Following the standard FSS protocol, CASE is organized in an episodic format.
Each episode consists of a support set with positive and negative bounding-box
prompts, a query image, and a binary ground-truth query mask. The support
prompts specify the target concept, and the query mask serves as the expected
segmentation output in the query image for evaluation.
To construct an episode, we first instantiate a positive-negative concept def-
inition using COCO or PACO annotations, such as a supercategory paired with
an excluded class, a function-driven category group, or an attribute-defined ob-
ject group. We then sample support images containing the required positive and

negative exemplars. Query images are sampled independently to contain target
objects and optionally include distractors. The target mask is generated from
the underlying annotations according to the instantiated concept definition.
This design prevents the task from being solved by simply matching a posi-
tive support example. Because negative exemplars explicitly define what should
be rejected or separated from the target, the model must infer the intended con-
cept from the interplay between positive and negative prompts. Detailed per-task
statistics and construction parameters are provided in the supplementary mate-

5
Experiments
5.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets. We evaluate CPA under two settings.
CASE. CASE evaluates specified visual prompt segmentation with positive
and negative visual exemplars. It contains six task families constructed from
COCO [13] and PACO [22]: SuperEx, FineIso, and Union for category-level con-
cepts, and AttrEx, AttrIso, and AttrUnion for attribute-level concepts. Each
episode provides positive and negative support boxes, a query image, and a bi-
nary target mask defined by a structured concept specification, such as category
exclusion, fine-grained isolation, functional union, attribute isolation, attribute
union, or attribute exclusion. The evaluation split is fixed at the episode level
to ensure deterministic comparison across models.
Standard FSS benchmark. We evaluate the CASE-trained CPA checkpoints di-
rectly on all four COCO-20i folds [13, 18], without retraining or fold-specific
tuning. COCO-20i is a standard few-shot segmentation benchmark derived from
COCO, where the 80 object categories are divided into four folds with 20 cate-
gories per fold. We report results under both 1-shot and 5-shot settings. Since
standard FSS uses only positive support annotations, each support mask is con-
verted into its tight bounding box and used as a positive visual prompt.
Evaluation Metrics. We use mean Intersection-over-Union (mIoU) as the pri-
mary metric. For CASE, we report task-wise mIoU and the average mIoU across
all tasks. We further aggregate results into category-level and attribute-level
groups. For COCO-20i, we report fold-wise and mean mIoU under the standard
positive-only 1-shot and 5-shot protocols.
Implementation Details. CPA is built on SAM 3 using the shared-canvas for-
mulation of FSS-SAM3 [27], where support and query images are composed into
a single 1008×1008 canvas. Positive and negative visual prompts are represented
as support bounding boxes. We keep the pretrained SAM 3 weights frozen and
insert LoRA modules into transformer encoder and decoder layers, resulting in
1.13M trainable parameters. CPA is trained with AdamW at a learning rate of
1 × 10−4, with LoRA rank r = 16 and scaling factor α = 32. Standard FSS eval-
uation follows the positive-only prompting protocol, which is also used for the
preservation regularization. Query masks are binarized at τ = 0.5. Additional
details are provided in the supplementary material, Sec. Implementation Details.

Qualitative Results and Limitations
Qualitative Results. Fig. 3 presents representative qualitative comparisons
on CASE. SAM 3 with positive-only prompting often misses valid target regions
or fails to respect the intended exclusion or attribute constraint. Naively adding
negative prompts to SAM 3 remains unstable and may suppress valid target
regions together with distractors. CPA with positive-only prompting remains
responsive to positive prompts, but may retain distractor regions because the
target concept remains underspecified without negative exemplars, especially
for exclusion and attribute-based tasks. CPA with positive-negative prompting
provides the most consistent predictions, producing masks that better match the
concept jointly specified by positive and negative visual exemplars.

cues are subtle or ambiguously annotated, and cross-category attribute union is
particularly difficult because the model must group visually diverse objects by
a shared property rather than by category or shape. CASE is also limited by
the categories, masks, and attributes available in COCO and PACO. Additional
qualitative results and failure cases are provided in the supplementary material,

Conclusion
We introduced Specified Visual Prompt Segmentation (SVPS), a new
segmentation setting, where positive and negative visual exemplars jointly spec-
ify target concepts. To evaluate this setting, we constructed CASE (Concept-
Aware Segmentation from Positive and Negative Visual Exemplars), a bench-
mark built upon COCO and PACO annotations with six category- and attribute-
level task families. Our experiments show that while frozen SAM 3 exhibits
strong few-shot segmentation capabilities with solely positive prompts, it strug-
gles to reliably interpret negative visual exemplars as exclusion evidence. To
address this limitation, we presented Concept Prompt Adaptation (CPA), a
lightweight LoRA-based adaptation baseline to enhance positive-negative prompt
interpretation. CPA significantly improves performance on CASE while keep-
ing competitive performance under positive-only FSS setups. We believe that
CASE can serve as a useful testbed for future research on positive-negative visual
prompting, negative exemplars, and concept-level visual prompt segmentation.

CASE Benchmark Construction
This section provides additional details on the construction of CASE. CASE is
designed to evaluate whether a segmentation model can infer a target concept
from both positive and negative visual exemplars. Unlike standard few-shot se-
mantic segmentation benchmarks, where the support set usually defines a single
positive class, CASE episodes require the model to parse compositional intent
relations such as inclusion, exclusion, fine-grained isolation, functional grouping,
attribute selection, attribute union, and attribute exclusion.
1.1
Data Sources
CASE is built from two annotation sources. The category-level tasks, including
SuperEx, FineIso, and Union, are constructed from MSCOCO instance segmen-
tation annotations [13]. We use COCO train2017 to generate candidate training
episodes and COCO val2017 to generate fixed evaluation episodes. The attribute-
level tasks, including AttrIso, AttrUnion, and AttrEx, are constructed from
PACO-LVIS annotations [22]. PACO provides object-level category annotations
and attribute labels, which allow us to define visual concepts based on color and
material properties.
For PACO-based tasks, we use object-level categories and filter out part-
level categories whose category names contain a part delimiter. This avoids con-
structing episodes in which the target concept is defined over object parts rather
than complete object instances. We further restrict attribute concepts to visu-
ally grounded color and material attributes. The color set contains black, blue,
brown, green, grey, orange, pink, purple, red, white, and yellow. The material set
contains stone, wood, rattan, fabric, crochet, wool, leather, velvet, metal, paper,
plastic, glass, and ceramic.
To specify, not every COCO/PACO image is used: only images satisfying
the task-specific support and target constraints are eligible. Episode and mask
construction is programmatic from the source annotations; only the high-level
Union group definitions are manually specified.

Episode Format
Each CASE episode in the benchmark contains a target image, a frozen support
set, a task label, an intent description, a frozen target mask, and frozen posi-
tive/negative visual prompts. The format is designed for reproducible evaluation:
the support examples, prompt boxes, and target masks are fixed in advance, so
different methods are evaluated on exactly the same episodes.
A support image contains positive and negative bounding-box prompts. Pos-
itive prompts indicate visual exemplars that should be included in the target
concept, while negative prompts indicate exemplars that should be excluded or
used as discriminative counterexamples. The target image contains a ground-
truth mask encoded in COCO-style run-length encoding (RLE). This avoids
requiring users to re-run the benchmark construction code.

1.5
Prompt Sampling Protocol
CASE uses bounding boxes as visual prompts. Each support image may contain
multiple positive and negative boxes. Positive boxes are selected from instances
that satisfy the intended concept, whereas negative boxes are selected from se-
mantically or visually related distractors. Thus, negatives are task-related dis-
tractors rather than arbitrary classes. All prompts correspond to object-level
instances; CASE does not cover stuff categories. This design forces the model to
infer not only what should be segmented, but also what should be rejected.
To reduce annotation noise and duplicated prompts, candidate boxes are
filtered by an IoU-based duplicate suppression rule. A newly selected box is
discarded if it overlaps too strongly with an existing prompt box. We also avoid
selecting positive boxes that collide with negative boxes, since such overlaps
would make the support intent ambiguous.
Training and evaluation differ in strictness. For training, we adopt a target-
relaxed but support-strict protocol. A training target image is required to contain
at least one positive foreground instance, but it is not always required to contain
a negative distractor. This increases concept coverage and avoids over-restricting

1.6
Dynamic Ground-truth Mask Generation
The target mask of each episode is generated from the source annotations accord-
ing to its task-specific intent. For COCO-based tasks, masks are generated from
instance segmentation annotations. For PACO-based tasks, masks are generated
by filtering object annotations according to semantic object groups and visual
attributes. The dynamic mask rules are summarized in Table S4. In exclusion
tasks, the target mask is computed by subtracting the excluded instances from
the base foreground. In union tasks, the target mask is the union of all instances
that satisfy the positive concept. In attribute tasks, the target mask is obtained
by selecting or excluding instances according to PACO attribute labels.

Task-specific Details
This section describes the six CASE task families in detail. Each task is designed
to evaluate a different form of concept reasoning from positive and negative
visual exemplars. In all task illustrations, green boxes on support images denote
positive visual prompts and red boxes denote negative visual prompts. On target
images, the green overlay denotes the ground-truth target mask. Red boxes on
target images are shown only to indicate distractor instances for visualization
and are not provided as model input during evaluation.
2.1
SuperEx: Supercategory Exclusion
SuperEx evaluates whether a model can infer a broad category-level concept
while excluding a specified fine-grained category. Each concept is defined as
  \mathc a l {Y} = \mathcal {C}_{\mathrm {super}} \setminus \mathcal {C}_{\mathrm {neg}}, 
where Csuper is a COCO supercategory and Cneg is one excluded category within
that supercategory. For example, an episode may define the intent “all vehicles
except cars.” Positive prompts are sampled from non-excluded categories in the
same supercategory, such as buses and trains, while negative prompts are sam-
pled from the excluded category, such as cars.
The target mask is generated by first taking the union of all instances in the
base supercategory and then subtracting all instances belonging to the excluded
category:
  M = M(\ma t hcal {C}_{\mathrm {super}}) \setminus M(\mathcal {C}_{\mathrm {neg}}).

This task tests whether a model can use negative exemplars to prevent over-
generalization. A model that relies only on positive prompts may infer the broad
supercategory and incorrectly include the excluded category.
For evaluation, target images are required to contain both valid foreground
instances and excluded-category distractors. Support images are required to in-
clude at least two positive categories and at least one negative exemplar. This
makes SuperEx a direct test of category-level exclusion.
2.2
FineIso: Fine-grained Isolation
FineIso evaluates whether a model can isolate a single fine-grained category from
related categories within the same supercategory. Each concept is defined as
  \mathcal {Y} = \mathcal {C}_{\mathrm {target}}, 
where Ctarget is one COCO category. For example, an episode may define the
intent “only dogs” within the animal supercategory. Positive prompts are sam-
pled from the target category, while negative prompts are sampled from other
categories in the same supercategory, such as cats, sheep, horses, or cows.
The target mask is the union of all instances of the target category:
  M = M(\mathcal {C}_{\mathrm {target}}). 
The negative prompts do not define a category to subtract from a broader fore-
ground. Instead, they act as discriminative counterexamples that specify the
intended granularity of the concept.

2.7
CASE-NegStress
CASE-NegStress is a dedicated 1-shot diagnostic subset that fixes the query im-
age, target mask, positive support, and candidate negative pool while varying
only the number of negative prompts. The 0-Neg condition denotes positive-only
prompting on this same NegStress subset rather than the full CASE. The pur-
pose is not to define a new task family, but to stress-test whether the model’s
predictions change appropriately when the negative evidence increases. A ro-
bust positive-negative visual prompt segmentation model should preserve re-
gions supported by the positive exemplars while suppressing regions that match
the negative exemplars. In contrast, a model that ignores negative prompts will
produce similar predictions under the original CASE setting and the NegStress
setting. Therefore, CASE-NegStress helps distinguish genuine negative-prompt
reasoning from positive-only support matching.

3.5
Inference and Evaluation Protocol
At inference time, we use the same shared-canvas formulation as in training.
Given S and Iq, we compose the canvas Ic, transform the support boxes into
the canvas coordinate system, and apply fθ to obtain composite logits Zc. We
crop the query region to obtain Zq and derive the binary query prediction as
 
 \ h at {M}^ { q} = \mathbf {1}[\sigma (Z^{q})>\tau ], 
where σ(·) denotes the sigmoid function and τ is the mask threshold. We use
τ = 0.5 for all reported results. We then resize the query prediction to the original
query resolution and compute mIoU against the ground-truth query mask M q.
For CASE, the fixed evaluation episode provides the support images and the
positive/negative support boxes. We use the vertical layout for 1-shot evaluation
and the L-shaped layout for 5-shot evaluation. For standard FSS benchmarks,
we follow the conventional positive-only setting: support masks are converted
into tight bounding boxes and used as positive visual prompts, and no negative
prompts are provided. For COCO-20i, the CASE-trained CPA checkpoints are
evaluated directly on all four folds without COCO-20i retraining or fold-specific
tuning. This evaluation measures CPA’s positive-only FSS behavior after adapta-
tion to CASE rather than assuming that the original behavior is fully preserved.

and FSSAM use positive support masks derived from the positive boxes, while
SANSA uses its native positive-box prompt interface. Since these methods were
designed for conventional positive-only FSS, their CASE results should be inter-
preted as positive-only reference baselines rather than fully specified SVPS.

sequently, under positive-only inference it retains more candidate regions than
FSS-SAM3, yielding higher recall but lower precision. When negative exemplars
are provided, CPA recovers precision while retaining substantially higher recall,
producing higher F1 and mIoU than FSS-SAM3 under Pos+Neg prompting.
Same-data training control. To separate the benefit of CASE training data
from the ability to use negative exemplars, we fine-tune SANSA on the same
CASE training set while retaining its native positive-only prompting interface,
which does not support negative visual exemplars. In-domain training improves
SANSA, but CPA remains +5.82/+5.88 mIoU higher in 1-/5-shot, showing that
CASE exposure alone is insufficient to explain CPA’s gains.
SAM 3 text-prompt controls. SAM 3 supports text prompts, but exclusion
intent is not reliably expressed by the tested text formulations. On SuperEx,
the reviewer-inspired exclusion prompt “[supercategory] except [excluded]” ob-
tains 5.79 mIoU as a text-only prompt. Combining text with visual prompts
remains below the corresponding visual-only FSS-SAM3 results in the main pa-
per (28.80/39.07 mIoU in 1-/5-shot).

Failure Cases and Limitations
Although CPA improves the use of positive and negative visual exemplars, sev-
eral limitations remain. Fig. S11 and Tab. S13 summarize representative failure
modes. Attribute-level tasks are particularly challenging. Color and material
labels may depend on lighting, viewpoint, texture, and object state. Some mate-
rials are also visually confounded with object categories; for example, glass may
be correlated with bottles or cups, while fabric may be correlated with furniture
or clothing. As a result, the model may segment objects that match the category
prior rather than the specified attribute.
Fig. S11 illustrates three representative limitations. First, attribute-level tasks
remain vulnerable to category–attribute entanglement: the model may segment
objects that match a strong object-category prior, such as containers, rather
than strictly following the specified attribute constraint. Second, some PACO
attributes are visually ambiguous, especially for color and material labels af-
fected by lighting, texture, viewpoint, or annotation convention. Third, negative
exemplars may still lead to under-exclusion when the excluded category remains
visually salient in the query image.
Negative prompts can also introduce failure modes. When positive and neg-
ative exemplars are visually similar, the model may over-exclude valid target
regions. Conversely, when negative exemplars are visually diverse or weakly re-
lated to the query distractors, the model may under-exclude and behave similarly
to positive-only FSS. These errors indicate that positive-negative visual prompt-

ing requires not only recognizing individual support instances, but also inferring
the intended relation between inclusion and exclusion evidence.
CASE itself is limited by the annotation spaces of COCO and PACO. COCO
provides reliable category-level instance masks but does not contain fine-grained
attributes. PACO provides attribute annotations, but the available color and
material labels are incomplete and sometimes ambiguous. Union concepts are
manually defined and capture only a subset of possible high-level semantic group-
ings. Extending CASE to richer attributes, open-vocabulary concepts, and more
diverse real-world domains is an important direction for future work.
Finally, CPA is designed as a lightweight and conservative adaptation of SAM
3. The preservation regularization mitigates positive-only FSS degradation, but
does not fully preserve the frozen model in the 1-shot setting; it also introduces
an adaptation–preservation trade-off. Stronger adaptation may improve CASE
performance further, but could degrade the original ability of SAM 3. Exploring
stronger architectures or training objectives that improve SVPS without sacri-
ficing positive-only FSS remains future work.
