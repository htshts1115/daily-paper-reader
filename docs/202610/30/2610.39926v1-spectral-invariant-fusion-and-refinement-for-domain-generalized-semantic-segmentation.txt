Spectral Invariant Fusion and Refinement for Domain Generalized
Semantic Segmentation
Yansong Bai, Yunzhi Zhuge, Xinzhuo Yu, Lu Zhang, Jin Zhan, Pingping Zhang, Huchuan Lu, Fellow IEEE
Abstract—Domain generalized semantic segmentation is chal-
lenging because it requires accurate dense prediction on unseen
domains without any access to target-domain data during training.
Existing methods improve transferability through data diversifi-
cation, invariant learning, or direct use of foundation models, but
they often struggle to simultaneously suppress domain-sensitive
interference and preserve fine-grained spatial cues. In this work,
we propose SIFR, a frequency-aware dual-stream framework that
tackles domain shift at both the fusion and decoding stages. SIFR
uses spectral modeling as a unified principle to regulate both
heterogeneous prior interaction and dense decoder refinement.
Instead of directly fusing frozen CLIP and DINOv2 features, SIFR
performs spectral-guided invariant fusion to filter out domain-
sensitive responses while preserving complementary semantic and
structural information. In addition, SIFR incorporates a spectral
decoder refinement module that stabilizes multi-scale decoder
features through low-frequency statistics-based normalization and
adaptive frequency selection, thereby reducing the propagation of
residual domain bias in dense prediction. This design enables SIFR
to jointly improve domain robustness and segmentation precision
under severe distribution shifts. Extensive experiments on multiple
DGSS benchmarks, including synthetic-to-real, real-to-real, and
adverse-weather settings, demonstrate consistent improvements
over previous state-of-the-art methods.
Index Terms—Domain generalized semantic segmentation, vi-
sion foundation model, vision-language model, frequency modeling,
CLIP, DINOv2.
I. INTRODUCTION
Domain Generalized Semantic Segmentation (DGSS) aims
to learn segmentation models from multiple source domains
that generalize well to unseen target domains without any
target-domain adaptation [1]–[7]. Compared with Domain
Adaptive Semantic Segmentation [8]–[10], which addresses
cross-domain discrepancies by leveraging unlabeled target-
domain data during training, DGSS must confront domain
shifts in a more challenging setting where no target-domain
information is available at all. Consequently, a central challenge
in DGSS is to achieve robustness to both low-level appearance
changes and high-level semantic or contextual variations
without compromising the accuracy and structural consistency
of pixel-wise predictions [1], [2], [11].

Existing DGSS methods mainly improve generalization from
two perspectives: data-level generalization and feature-level
representation learning [2], [4], [16], [17]. The former increases
source-domain diversity through perturbation, stylization, or
synthesis, while the latter aims to learn domain-invariant
representations across source domains [2], [4], [17]–[20].
However, both directions face an inherent tension between
domain invariance and spatial discriminability. Increasing
appearance diversity or enforcing feature consistency can
improve robustness to domain shifts, but may also weaken
boundary, texture, and other fine-grained cues that are essential
for accurate segmentation. Consequently, those methods still
struggle to jointly achieve strong domain robustness and precise
spatial prediction [4], [16].
Recent advances in large-scale pretrained models have
opened a promising direction for DGSS, as they provide
more transferable and stable visual priors than conventionally
trained backbones [6]. In particular, vision-language models
(VLMs) capture rich high-level semantic knowledge, while
vision foundation models (VFMs) offer stronger local structural
cues and dense visual representations [15], [21], [22]. However,
simply combining these heterogeneous priors is still insufficient
for DGSS, as it faces two core challenges: i) Cross-prior
misalignment. VLM and VFM are pretrained with different
objectives, which leads to inconsistent feature geometries and
domain responses and thus makes their interaction unstable
under domain shifts. ii) Domain appearance entanglement.
Despite their strong transferability, both priors can still encode
domain-specific style, texture, and low-level statistics, so direct
fusion may propagate domain-sensitive variations instead of
isolating task-relevant cues.
To address these challenges, spectral modeling offers a
complementary perspective. i) Spectral alignment. By orga-
nizing heterogeneous priors in the frequency domain, spectral
modeling promotes more coherent interaction between semantic
and structural cues across domains. ii) Spectral disentangle-
ment. By separating domain-variant appearance components
from task-relevant structural information, spectral modeling
suppresses domain-sensitive responses while preserving the
local details required for precise segmentation.
Inspired by these principles, we propose SIFR, a Spectral
Invariant Fusion and Refinement framework for domain gener-
alized semantic segmentation, which leverages heterogeneous
large-scale priors while explicitly enhancing domain invariance.
i) Spectral-Guided Invariant Fusion (SGIF). SGIF employs
frozen CLIP [21] and DINOv2 [22] as the VLM and VFM,
where CLIP provides high-level semantic priors and DINOv2
captures fine-grained structural representations. By casting
cross-prior interaction into the spectral domain, SGIF explicitly

• We present SIFR, a frequency-aware dual-stream frame-
work for domain generalized semantic segmentation that
leverages frozen CLIP and DINOv2 as complementary
semantic and structural priors, and introduces spectral
modeling in both feature fusion and decoder refinement.
• We introduce a Spectral-Guided Invariant Fusion (SGIF)
module that couples token-level cross-attention with
frequency-domain refinement to promote complementary
interaction between heterogeneous pretrained representa-

• We develop a Spectral Decoder Refinement (SDR) module,
comprising domain-stable normalization and frequency-

• Extensive experiments on multiple DGSS benchmarks
show that SIFR consistently improves cross-domain seg-
mentation performance across synthetic-to-real, real-to-
real, and adverse-weather settings.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Domain Generalized Semantic Segmentation
Domain-generalized semantic segmentation (DGSS) studies
how to learn segmentation models that generalize to unseen
target domains without access to target-domain data during
training. Existing research has explored this problem through
multiple paradigms [1], [4], [11], [16], [27]. Earlier efforts
often improve domain robustness either by simulating domain
variation during training, such as through meta-learning or
source-domain diversification [2], [20], [27], or by reducing
domain-specific bias via feature alignment, normalization,
whitening, and related representation learning techniques [1],
[4], [28]. Beyond these data- and representation-oriented
strategies, some studies further investigate architectural designs,
including transformer-based models, to strengthen cross-domain
feature modeling and dense prediction [13], [29]. More recently,
foundation models have introduced a new opportunity for
DGSS, since large-scale pretraining can provide stronger and
more transferable visual priors than conventionally trained
backbones [6], [11], [12], [15]. Existing attempts have begun
to exploit such priors through synthetic data generation [11],
model adaptation [6], [12], or semantic guidance [29], [30].
Beyond conventional static vision–language alignment, SB-
Gen [31] formulates the transformation from domain-specific
visual representations to domain-agnostic textual semantics as
a cross-modal Schr¨odinger Bridge, and models this alignment
as a stochastic semantic evolution process. However, how
to effectively utilize heterogeneous pretrained representations

B. Vision Foundation Models
Vision foundation models (VFMs) have recently emerged
as a powerful basis for visual transfer learning, primarily
driven by large-scale pretraining on diverse image corpora
using self-supervised objectives [22], [32], [33], masked image
modeling [34], and segmentation-oriented paradigms
[35].
Compared with conventionally supervised backbones, VFMs
generally learn more generalizable and reusable representations,
enabling strong transfer performance across a wide range of
downstream tasks [22], [33], [34]. Representative examples
include DINO series [22], [32], iBOT, MAE [34] and EVA
series [33], [36], [37] which provide strong semantic and
structural representations, as well as the SAM family [35],
[38], which brings foundation-scale modeling to segmentation.
Recent studies have also shown the relevance of VFMs
to dense prediction. DINO [32] and iBOT [39] suggest
that self-supervised ViTs can encode explicit segmentation-
related information and strong local semantic patterns, while
ViT-Adapter [40] demonstrates that pretrained ViTs can be
effecti- vely adapted to semantic segmentation with lightweight
task-specific designs. Nevertheless, existing efforts mostly
emphasize architectural adaptation or downstream transfer,
with limited attention paid to how domain-sensitive appearance
statistics are entangled with transferable structural cues in
pretrained dense representations. This leaves room for spectral
modeling as a complementary mechanism for DGSS, since it
can help expose and suppress domain-variant responses while
preserving the structure-aware cues that VFMs already capture.
C. Frequency Modeling Under Domain Shift
Frequency-based modeling has become an increasingly
important direction in cross-domain learning, since domain
shift often affects not only spatial appearance but also frequency
statistics [12], [18], [41], [42]. Existing studies mainly explore
frequency information from two perspectives [12], [18], [41],
[42]. One line of work applies frequency-domain perturbation,
spectral substitution, or amplitude manipulation at the input
level to expand source-domain diversity and alleviate style-
related bias [18], [41], [42]. Another line of work performs
frequency filtering or modulation at the feature level to improve
the cross-domain robustness of learned representations [12],
[43], [44]. Most recently, HarDiff [45] introduces the Discrete
Hartley Transform into a diffusion-based framework for gener-
alizable dense prediction. It exploits low-frequency structural
priors from the source domain during training and high-
frequency detail guidance from unseen-domain inputs during
iterative sampling. Nevertheless, existing frequency-aware VFM
methods mainly focus on adapting a single pretrained visual
stream. For example, FADA [12] decomposes VFM features
into low- and high-frequency branches through Haar wavelet

transformation, while SET [3] processes the amplitude and
phase components of frozen VFM features with learnable
spectral tokens. In contrast, our method focuses on frequency-
aware interaction between heterogeneous VLM and VFM
representations rather than frequency adaptation within a single
VFM. Although MFuser [15] also integrates VLM and VFM
features, it mainly employs Mamba-based modules to model
sequential and spatial interactions, without explicitly regulating
the domain-sensitive spectral responses propagated from the
two pretrained streams. Our SGIF instead combines token-
level cross-attention with stream-specific adaptive spectral
reweighting, and SIFR further extends spectral regulation to
multi-scale decoder refinement. Therefore, SIFR differs from
existing approaches in both the representation scope and the
stages at which spectral modeling is performed.
III. METHOD
A. Overview
As shown in Fig. 2, our method adopts a unified frequency-
aware segmentation framework that consists of hierarchical
prior extraction, spectral-guided invariant fusion, and decoder-
side refinement. Given an input image, frozen VLM and VFM
encoders first extract multi-level features, where VLM pro-
vides high-level semantic priors and VFM offers fine-grained
structural cues. To effectively exploit these heterogeneous
representations under domain shift, we introduce a Spectral-
Guided Invariant Fusion (SGIF) module during layer-wise
feature interaction. Specifically, SGIF combines token-level
cross-attention with a frequency-guided enhancement branch,
which transforms features into the spectral domain, adaptively
reweights different frequency components, and then projects
them back to the spatial domain. In this way, the fusion pro-
cess suppresses domain-sensitive appearance variations while
preserving domain-stable semantic and structural information
that is more beneficial for semantic segmentation.
The fused features are then fed into the pixel decoder and
further refined by the proposed Spectral Decoder Refinement
(SDR) module. Specifically, SDR module first applies low-
frequency statistics-based normalization to mitigate residual
domain-specific bias in decoder features, and then performs
adaptive frequency selection to enhance segmentation-relevant
spectral responses. The resulting multi-scale features are finally
fed into the Transformer decoder, where they interact with text-
guided semantic priors to progressively produce high-quality
masks. At the framework level, SGIF, the pixel decoder, and
SDR play complementary rather than redundant roles. SGIF
suppresses domain-sensitive interference during heterogeneous
feature interaction and fusion. The pixel decoder subsequently
aggregates the SGIF-fused multi-level representations into
multi-scale dense features, providing spatially structured visual
representations for subsequent mask decoding. SDR then
mitigates the residual domain bias that remains or accumulates
during multi-scale feature aggregation and dense decoding.
Finally, the text-guided semantic priors complement the SDR-
refined visual representations by providing category-level
semantic guidance within the Transformer decoder, thereby
establishing more reliable pixel-to-class correspondences for
mask prediction.

C. Spectral Decoder Refinement
Although the encoder-stage SGIF reduces domain-sensitive
interference during heterogeneous prior fusion, residual appear-
ance deviations may still remain in the fused representations
and propagate through subsequent multi-scale decoding. The
key motivation of SDR is to decouple residual domain-bias
suppression from segmentation-relevant spectral preservation
during dense prediction. Since global and smoothly varying
domain changes, such as illumination, contrast, weather, and
style, are more strongly reflected in low-frequency feature
statistics, DS-Norm estimates normalization statistics from the
low-frequency component while applying normalization to the
original full-spectrum feature. This suppresses global statistical
deviations without explicitly discarding fine-grained spatial
responses. However, normalization alone cannot determine
which frequency components are informative for different
images and feature scales. Therefore, FGE further performs
data-dependent spectral reweighting to adaptively emphasize
segmentation-relevant responses. In this way, SDR follows a
suppress-then-select principle: DS-Norm stabilizes decoder
feature statistics, whereas FGE adaptively preserves and
enhances informative spectral components for robust mask
prediction. As shown in Fig. 2, SDR is inserted between the
pixel decoder and the Transformer decoder. Fig. 3 details the
internal suppress-then-select design of SDR.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Experiment Settings
Datasets and Metrics. We evaluate our method un-
der synthetic-to-real, real-to-real, and real-to-adverse-weather
DGSS settings on five driving-scene benchmarks that share
19 semantic categories. GTA5 [47] is a large-scale synthetic
dataset containing 24,966 densely annotated images with
a fixed resolution of 1914×1052, and is widely used as
a source domain for cross-domain semantic segmentation.
Cityscapes [24] is a standard real-world benchmark for urban
scene understanding, containing 2,975 training images, 500
validation images, and 1,525 testing images, with a fixed
resolution of 2048×1024. BDD100K [25] is a diverse real-world
driving dataset with images of fixed resolution 1280×720; for
semantic segmentation, it provides 10K annotated images and is
commonly organized into 7,000 training, 1,000 validation, and
2,000 test images. Mapillary [26] is a large-scale street-scene
dataset containing 18,000 training images, 2,000 validation
images, and 5,000 test images; unlike Cityscapes [24] and
BDD100K [25], its images are high-resolution but come in
diverse sizes and capture conditions. ACDC [48] is designed
for adverse-condition semantic segmentation and contains
1,600 training images, 406 validation images, and 2,000 test
images under four weather conditions, i.e. fog, rain, snow, and
nighttime, making it a challenging benchmark for evaluations
under severe domain shifts. Following prior DGSS works [6],
[15], we adopt mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) as the
evaluation metric.
Implementation Details. We adopt CLIP [21] as the vision-
language model, DINOv2 [22] as the visual foundation model,
and Mask2Former [46] as the segmentation head. During
training, the CLIP visual encoder, CLIP text encoder, and
DINOv2 backbone are kept frozen, and their parameters are
not updated. Optimization is performed over the parameters
of the SGIF modules and their learnable query tokens, the
feature projection and fusion layers, the pixel decoder, the
learnable layers within SDR, the text-guided cross-attention
module, the Transformer decoder, and the classification and
mask prediction heads. The learnable context tokens used to
construct the class prompts are also optimized, while the CLIP
text encoder remains frozen. All trainable parameters are jointly
optimized in a single end-to-end training stage using the total


IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON MULTIMEDIA, VOL. XX, NO. XX, MONTH YEAR
7
objective in Eq.(12). No stage-wise pretraining or alternating
optimization is adopted. All input images are resized to 512 ×
512. We train the model with a batch size of 2 on each GPU.
We optimize the model using AdamW with a base learning rate
of 1e-4 and a weight decay of 1e-4. The learning rate follows
a poly decay schedule with a power of 0.9 and a minimum
learning rate of 1e-6, together with a linear warm-up over
the first 1900 iterations using a warm-up ratio of 1e-6. The
model is trained for 30,000 iterations. During inference, we
adopt sliding-window evaluation with a crop size of 512 ×
512 and a stride of 341 × 341. Unless otherwise specified,
we use Lq = 64 learnable query tokens in SGIF and set the
low-pass filter ratio in DS-Norm to ρ = 0.25. All experiments
are implemented based on MMSegmentation and conducted
on two RTX 4090 GPUs.
B. Comparison with State-of-the-Art Methods
We compare SIFR with representative domain generalized
semantic segmentation (DGSS) methods under three evaluation
settings: synthetic-to-real generalization from GTA5 [47] to
Cityscapes [24], BDD100K [25], and Mapillary [26] (G →
C, B, M), real-to-real generalization from Cityscapes [24]
to BDD100K [25] and Mapillary [26] (C →B, M), and
real-to-adverse-weather generalization from Cityscapes [24]
to ACDC [48] (C →A). Since our method is built upon
heterogeneous pretrained priors, we place particular empha-
sis on recent foundation-model-based approaches, including
CLOUDS [11], VLTSeg [30], REIN [6], SET [3], MFuser [15],
DepthForge [49], and tqdm [29]. For completeness, we also
include several conventional DGSS methods to provide a

Effect of Normalization Strategy. Tab. IV compares different
normalization strategies in the decoder refinement stage. Among
all variants, the proposed DS-Norm achieves the best average
performance, improving the average mIoU to 73.04. Compared
with conventional normalization schemes, such as LayerNorm,
BatchNorm, GroupNorm, and InstanceNorm, DS-Norm yields
more consistent gains on both BDD100k [25] and Mapil-

normalization operators is insufficient to address domain-
specific distribution shifts in decoder features. By contrast, our
DS-Norm explicitly normalizes features with low-frequency
statistics, which better suppresses residual domain-sensitive
deviations and produces more robust representations for cross-
domain segmentation.
Effect of Pooling Design. Tab. V studies different pooling
combinations for generating the spectral mask in the decoder
stage. Using only two types of pooled descriptors already
brings reasonable performance, but the full combination of min
pooling, average pooling, and max pooling gives the best result,
reaching 73.04 average mIoU. This suggests that different pool-
ing operators capture complementary statistics in the frequency
domain. In particular, max pooling emphasizes strong spectral
responses, average pooling captures global frequency trends,
and min pooling further supplements suppressed yet potentially
informative cues. Their combination therefore enables a more
comprehensive spectral characterization and leads to stronger
domain-generalized segmentation performance.
Effect of SGIF and SDR. Tab. VI further evaluates the
contributions of the two proposed modules. Introducing either
SGIF or SDR alone already improves the cross-domain
performance over the baseline, indicating that both encoder-side
spectral fusion and decoder-side spectral refinement are indi-
vidually effective. Using both modules together yields further
gains, highlighting their complementarity. Specifically, SGIF
strengthens intermediate fused representations by combining
token interaction with frequency-guided invariant cues, while
SDR removes residual domain-sensitive bias during decoding.
These results show that spectral modeling at both the feature
fusion and decoder refinement stages is important for stronger
robustness under domain shift.

V. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we presented SIFR, a frequency-aware dual-
stream framework for domain generalized semantic segmenta-
tion that leverages the complementary strengths of frozen CLIP
and DINOv2 under domain shift. SIFR improves generalization
through both heterogeneous prior fusion and decoder refinement.
It introduces Spectral-Guided Invariant Fusion to enhance
semantic–structural interaction while reducing domain-sensitive
interference, and Spectral Decoder Refinement to stabilize
multi-scale decoding using low-frequency statistics-based nor-
malization and adaptive spectral enhancement. Experiments
on synthetic-to-real, real-to-real, and real-to-adverse-weather
benchmarks show that SIFR consistently improves cross-
domain segmentation, highlighting frequency-aware modeling
as an effective way to exploit heterogeneous foundation priors
for DGSS.
