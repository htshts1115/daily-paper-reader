Efficacy of Monocular Depth Estimation in Lunar Environments
Michael W. Lanighan
Kenji Brameld
Seth Gee
Abstract— Recent advances in monocular depth estimation
have demonstrated highly accurate 3D reconstructions within
in-distribution terrestrial domains. This study investigates the
efficacy of several state-of-the-art approaches to reconstruct
out-of-distribution lunar environments using the NASA Ames
Polar Optical Lunar Analog Reconstruction (POLAR) stereo
dataset and Apollo 17 surface imagery. By leveraging these
datasets, we establish a robust analog-to-field validation pipeline
that evaluates quantitative performance against ground-truth
physical analogs and qualitative performance against historical
field data. Our results indicate that select state-of-the-art
techniques can successfully generalize to produce high-fidelity
relative 3D reconstructions of the unstructured lunar surface.
I. INTRODUCTION
Humanity has decades of experience in conducting and
overseeing remote space operations, both in terms of human
spaceflight and remote robotic assets. Even with this exten-
sive experience, low Earth orbit (LEO) operations on the
International Space Station (ISS) for the Mobile Servicing
System (MSS) present challenges for operators related to
situational awareness. NASA Astronaut Nick Hague recently
outlined the state of operations in LEO:
Robotics and space exploration go hand in hand.
It takes years of training and practice to learn how
to digest multiple 2D views at different angles to
determine if the path will be clear for the arm to
move. We even use the HECA cams on EV1 and
EV2 to help.1
Astronaut Hague’s statement emphasizes a key challenge
in remote robot operations: the lack of tools to maintain
effective, task-relevant situational awareness. The robot op-
erator station on the ISS is dominated by 2D camera views.
Currently, operators must ingest these views and create
mental models that fuse multiple sources of information
together, a skill that, as Astronaut Hague points out, can
take years to master. The heavy cognitive load this induces
is a common pitfall of remote robot operations. Research
has shown that unifying this information in 3D environments
enhances task outcomes [1], [2].
Robot operations from mission control at NASA JSC use
a similar control station as that on station, but face further
challenges brought by downlink/bandwidth constraints. Due
to the finite number of downlinks and fixed bandwidth avail-
able, not all camera views needed to support a remote op-
eration may be available. While tools to support operations,
including 3D views [3] and special purpose capability for

A promising emerging area that can help address these
issues is monocular depth estimation (MDE). These ap-
proaches consume a 2D image and reconstruct a 3D scene for
operators to inspect. Moreover, relying on a single camera
reduces the need for stereo pairs or time of flight sensors to
reconstruct 3D representations of the remote scene, providing
the additional benefit of lowered bandwidth requirements.

II. RELATED WORK
While advancements in LiDAR and RGB-D cameras for
space systems have been made, it is likely that RGB cameras
will be the prevalent form of visual feedback, both on rovers
and lunar infrastructure. As such, techniques to create depth
estimates and 3D representations using traditional cameras
will be needed.
A. Stereogrammetry
Recent NASA missions, including the VIPER mission,
have targeted onboard stereo pairs to provide 3D information
about the operating environment. VIPER CONOPS relies
on sending RGB images back to ground control, where
they will be used to construct 3D models of the rover’s
environment via stereogrammetry [7]. These 3D models will
then be used by operators when planning rover operations.
The core libraries used in this reconstruction were open
sourced in the NASA Ames stereo pipeline (ASP) [8].
Stereo techniques such as those in ASP rely on matching
features across multiple images using known offsets between
cameras to estimate depth. Due to their fundamental reliance
on geometry, these techniques require at least two sen-
sors/images with overlapping visual coverage to obtain depth
estimates. Our ASP baseline was explicitly tuned for lunar
terrain. Leveraging the intrinsic and extrinsic calibration
provided by the POLAR dataset, we configured ASP with the
More Global Matching (MGM) algorithm to handle reduced
surface texture, alongside sub-pixel refinement and a 9x9
correlation kernel to preserve sharp details on rocks and
craters.
B. Monocular Depth Estimation
An alternative approach to estimate depth from RGB
images is to use monocular depth estimation techniques.
This broad family of techniques trains a neural network
to estimate depth in RGB images using large training sets.
Several leading techniques have been open-sourced with per-
missive licenses. These techniques are attractive for remote
operations using monocular cameras, as they can produce
depth estimates without requiring overlapping visual features
from multiple cameras. Provided that similar CONOPS as
those proposed in VIPER are followed [7], cameras could
be streamed to remote operators (human or autonomous sys-
tems), and depth estimates can be created by feeding incom-
ing images through the monocular depth networks to provide
3D context. Such an approach would cut bandwidth and
downlink requirements in half compared to traditional stere-
ogrammetry approaches. While concurrent benchmarking [9]
evaluates the algorithmic performance of MDE for future
autonomous navigation, our work specifically evaluates MDE
as a practical solution to overcome these bandwidth limits
in remote teleoperation and supervision. Broadly speaking,
MDE approaches can be grouped into two classes: single
view reconstruction and multi-view reconstruction. In this
study, we evaluate the following approaches:
1) Single View Reconstruction: Approaches take as input
a single image and estimate depth relative to the imager.
Marigold [10]: A diffusion-based (generative) approach
that denoises a latent image encoding to produce a depth
image. Depth Anything V2 [11]: Uses Vision Transformer
(ViT), DINOv2 [12] and DPT [13], architectures to distill a
large teacher model trained on synthetic data into a robust
estimator using pseudo-labeled real-world imagery. MoGe-
2 [14]: Uses a DINOv2 backbone with multiple decoder
heads to directly output affine-invariant relative point maps
via a CNN and absolute metric scale via an MLP conditioned
on the global CLS token.
2) Multi-View Reconstruction: Approaches consume mul-
tiple images to reconstruct a coherent, fused 3D scene.
VGGT [15]: Uses a DINOv2 backbone with alternating
frame-wise and global self-attention to encode multiple im-
ages. It decodes them with multiple heads (a DPT head for
points, depth, and tracking, and a camera head for both
intrinsics and extrinsics) to directly reconstruct a scene in
a single forward pass. MapAnything [16]: Encodes input
images using DINOv2, fusing optional supporting geometric
inputs (calibration, poses, depth) into the same latent space.
It leverages an alternating attention backbone and features
task-specific heads (DPT, pose, and scale) to directly regress
factored metric 3D geometry. OmniVGGT [17]: Built on
a similar structure to VGGT, it introduces a GeoAdapter
mechanism with zero-initialized convolutions to safely in-
ject arbitrary auxiliary geometric modalities (depth, camera
intrinsics, and extrinsics) without disrupting the pre-trained
visual backbone.
III. METHODOLOGY
To establish the efficacy of the investigated MDE ap-
proaches, we leveraged two datasets of representative lunar
imagery. The first was a subset of images from the NASA
Ames POLAR Stereo dataset [5]. These images are part of
a dataset built to measure stereo reconstruction performance
in simulated lunar terrains and contain ground truth LiDAR
scans of each scene. Scenes are captured from multiple
perspectives and lighting conditions. We selected a subset
of these images that we felt were representative use cases
and would challenge reconstruction ability due to a lack of
textures and the presence of lens flaring/glare. The second
dataset consisted of a subset of images of the Lunar surface
collected during Apollo 17 [6]. While ground truth depth
does not exist for these images, they can be used to qual-
itatively measure reconstruction performance and provide
analog-to-field validation of the MDE techniques.


A. Quantitative analysis: NASA POLAR Stereo dataset
The initial evaluations used NASA’s POLAR Stereo
dataset to conduct a rigorous quantitative analysis of re-
construction performance. Originally developed for Resource
Prospector and later for VIPER, the dataset includes stereo
images of various laboratory simulated lunar terrains with
varying features under numerous lighting conditions from 3
viewing locations. Ground truth data are provided for each
scene using a Faro x330 LiDAR, which is registered to
the stereo imaging pair. We evaluated images taken from
Positions A and C, with the “sun” 30 degrees from A.
For each terrain, capture location C presents lens flaring
that we anticipated would cripple the performance of the
MDE approaches. Images used in evaluations span strewn
rock fields to barren, textureless scenes to evaluate the
MDE approaches under the expected diverse conditions on
the lunar surface. The image from the left camera of the
stereo pair was fed to the MDE approaches, while ASP
leveraged both left and right images to reconstruct depth,
with the output aligned to the left image. While some of
the multi-view approaches support both multiple camera
views and camera calibration data, we withheld these inputs
in our analysis and focused solely on blind reconstruction
capabilities using a single input image.
Quantitative analysis efforts of the targeted MDE ap-
proaches are complicated by the fact that not all MDE
techniques evaluated output metric depth; many only output
relative depth (i.e., normalized) or can incorrectly estimate
the global scale. This leads to difficulties in using an absolute
error metric, as high absolute errors may be present for
a scene reconstructed perfectly but at a different scale.

rather than correctly estimate the scale, as the reconstruction
could be inspected in isolation or the actual scale could be

B. Qualitative analysis: Apollo 17 Images
The second evaluation uses images of the Lunar Surface
collected during Apollo 17 to qualitatively compare the best
performing MDE approaches. Qualitative analysis inspected
the depth reconstruction fidelity in terms of overall structure
and relative noise to validate our findings using the NASA
POLAR stereo dataset. We selected two images from the
dataset: one that prominently features Astronaut Schmitt
conducting sample collection activities against a barren lunar
terrain and a second that prominently features the texture
rich rear of the lunar roving vehicle (LRV) after the “fender
repair” incident, with Schmitt in the LRV again backdropped
by barren lunar terrain. It is expected that these images
will challenge MDE approaches, as they are unlikely to be
representative of the training datasets used to create each
model.

V. CONCLUSIONS
Our results indicate that ViT-based MDE approaches such
as MoGe-2, OmniVGGT, and VGGT provide high-fidelity
relative 3D reconstructions in out-of-distribution lunar envi-
ronments. A surprising result of our investigation was the
relative robustness of MDE approaches to the impact of lens
flaring and shadows. Extreme lighting conditions resulting

on the lunar surface. In the POLAR stereo dataset images,
the investigated MDE approaches successfully mitigated
lens artifacts that traditional stereogrammetry struggled with.
Furthermore, MDE approaches consistently estimated the
depth of shadowed areas in both the POLAR stereo and
Apollo surface images. This finding provides strong support
for leveraging MDE approaches in lunar settings where
remote operator introspection is needed before committing to
actions. Moreover, obtaining such 3D context from a single
image cuts bandwidth needs in half compared to traditional
stereogrammetry approaches, which require two views.
However, the investigated MDE approaches did not con-
sistently output an accurate metric scale, if they output one
at all. As such, while the reconstructions were internally
consistent, they cannot be readily integrated into additional
visualization tools without further downstream processing.
For envisioned lunar surface use, particularly in remote
teleoperation contexts, this processing could leverage the
known dimensions of rover components (e.g., manipulator
arm, grippers, etc.) or other infrastructure (e.g., landers or
habitats) in the camera’s view to dynamically anchor the
global scale. Furthermore, even relative reconstructions can
be used in isolation to provide vital 3D context, allow-
ing operators to evaluate a scene before committing the
additional bandwidth required to downlink stereo pairs for
accurate metric depth. The top-performing MDE techniques
estimated depth for every pixel in the image. Given this
complete coverage, it may also be possible to fuse MDE and
stereogrammetry results to fill in any holes from traditional
stereo reconstructions that occur due to insufficient feature
matching across the pair [20].
This study focused on single-view reconstruction, but
several of the investigated techniques support multi-view
reconstruction. This is a promising direction for future anal-
ysis, particularly if these approaches can render internally
consistent fused barren lunar landscapes. Early qualitative
exploration indicates that multi-view approaches can render
such landscapes in the POLAR stereo dataset, but new lunar
terrain datasets are needed to fully quantify the errors in the
resulting fused scenes. If viable, such multi-view approaches
would be capable of providing rich, 3D reconstructions
from viewing geometries selected to support remote robot
operations [21].
